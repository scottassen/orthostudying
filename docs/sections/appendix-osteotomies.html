Osteotomies

By region, with eponym, indication, a brief technical description and the pitfalls. Pelvic osteotomies are grouped so the redirectional, reshaping and salvage distinction — and the hyaline-versus-fibrocartilage question — stays clear.

PELVIS & ACETABULUM
Salter innominate osteotomy (Robert Salter, Toronto, 1961)Residual acetabular dysplasia in DDH, age ~18 months to 6–10 years, with a concentrically reducible, congruent hip and adequate motion.A single straight cut through the innominate bone from the sciatic notch to the AIIS. The distal fragment is rotated anterolaterally and hinged on the PUBIC SYMPHYSIS, held open with a triangular iliac crest graft and two threaded pins.Provides ~15–25° of additional anterolateral coverage and corrects the acetabular index by roughly 10–15° — a modest correction, inadequate for severe dysplasia. Slightly lengthens the limb. Requires a concentric reduction; the hinge on the symphysis limits the achievable correction in an older child.
Pemberton pericapsular osteotomy (1965)Acetabular dysplasia in a child with an OPEN triradiate cartilage (~1–8 years), particularly with a large, capacious acetabulum.An incomplete curved osteotomy of the ilium above the acetabulum, extending from the AIIS toward the triradiate cartilage, which acts as the hinge. The acetabular roof is levered down and held with a wedge graft.Reshapes rather than redirects — it DECREASES acetabular volume, so it is the right choice when the acetabulum is too large. Requires an open triradiate cartilage (which is the hinge). Can stiffen the hip if over-corrected; no fixation is usually needed.
Dega osteotomyPOSTERIOR/posterolateral acetabular deficiency — classically the cerebral palsy hip, and in neuromuscular dysplasia generally.An incomplete transiliac osteotomy hinging on the medial ilial cortex and the triradiate cartilage, with the graft placed to direct coverage posterolaterally.The osteotomy of choice for CP hip reconstruction, because CP dysplasia is posterosuperior, unlike the anterolateral deficiency of DDH. Usually combined with a femoral VDRO and adductor ± psoas release — the combination, not the femoral osteotomy alone, is what prevents recurrence.
Steel / Tönnis triple innominate osteotomyModerate to severe dysplasia in the older child or adolescent with a closed or closing triradiate cartilage, where a Salter gives insufficient correction.Three cuts — ilium, superior pubic ramus and ischium — freeing the acetabular fragment for greater rotation, then fixed with screws.Greater correction than a Salter but a larger operation with more fixation. Superseded in most centres by the PAO, which preserves the posterior column and allows earlier weightbearing.
Bernese periacetabular osteotomy (Ganz PAO, 1988)Symptomatic acetabular dysplasia in the adolescent or young adult with a CLOSED triradiate cartilage, a congruent joint and minimal arthritis (Tönnis 0–1).A series of cuts — incomplete ischial, superior pubic ramus, and a biplanar iliac/retroacetabular cut — through a single anterior (modified Smith-Petersen) approach, freeing the acetabular fragment which is then reoriented (lateralised coverage, corrected version, medialised centre of rotation) and fixed with screws.Preserves the POSTERIOR COLUMN, so the pelvic ring stays intact and the patient can weightbear early. Does not distort the true pelvis (important for future childbirth). Contraindications: significant arthritis, an incongruent or non-spherical head, and an open triradiate cartilage. Avoid over-correction into retroversion, which creates iatrogenic impingement — an arthroscopy or head-neck osteochondroplasty is often combined.
Chiari medial displacement osteotomy (1955)SALVAGE for a subluxated, incongruent hip that cannot be concentrically reduced, with pain and inadequate coverage.A straight or slightly curved cut just above the capsular insertion, angled ~10–15° cephalad; the distal fragment is displaced MEDIALLY so the overhanging ilium sits above the femoral head with the joint capsule interposed.Coverage is by FIBROCARTILAGINOUS METAPLASIA of the interposed capsule — NOT hyaline cartilage. This is the classic exam distinction. It MEDIALISES the hip (reducing the joint reaction force) and narrows the pelvis. It does not lateralise — a common false statement.
Shelf (slotted acetabular augmentation, Staheli)SALVAGE for inadequate lateral coverage where redirection is not possible — an incongruent or aspherical head, or as an adjunct.A slot is created just above the capsule and cancellous bone graft strips are laid laterally over the capsule to extend the acetabular roof.Like the Chiari, coverage is by fibrocartilage over the capsule, not hyaline cartilage. It adds bone laterally and does NOT medialise the hip — the standard false statement. Graft resorption is a risk if not adequately unloaded.
PROXIMAL FEMUR & HIP
Proximal femoral varus derotation osteotomy (VDRO)Cerebral palsy hip displacement (migration percentage >40–50%); DDH with excessive anteversion and coxa valga; Perthes containment.Intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric osteotomy with a varus-producing wedge and derotation to correct excessive anteversion, fixed with a blade plate or locking plate.In cerebral palsy, a VDRO ALONE is inadequate once migration exceeds ~40–50% — it must be combined with a pelvic (Dega) osteotomy and adductor ± psoas release, because the acetabular dysplasia will not remodel in a non-ambulator. Excessive varus causes trochanteric overgrowth and abductor weakness.
Femoral shortening derotational osteotomyOpen reduction of DDH in a child over ~2–3 years, or any high-riding dislocated hip.Subtrochanteric segment resection with derotation, allowing the head to be reduced without tension.Preferred to preoperative traction — it reduces the pressure on the femoral head and substantially lowers the rate of AVN compared with forceful reduction.
Valgus intertrochanteric osteotomy (Pauwels)Femoral neck NONUNION with a vertical fracture line; hinge abduction in Perthes; severe coxa vara.A closing-wedge valgus osteotomy at the intertrochanteric level, fixed with a blade plate, which converts the shear force across the nonunion into a compressive force.The principle is the conversion of shear to compression — the reason it works for a Pauwels III nonunion. Also shortens and medialises; assess limb length. For hinge abduction in Perthes it moves the impinging lateral head out of the way.
Southwick triplane osteotomyModerate to severe chronic SCFE with residual deformity and impingement.Biplane (flexion and valgus) closing wedge at the intertrochanteric level with derotation — deliberately performed away from the physis and the retinacular vessels.Performed distal to the deformity to avoid the AVN risk of operating at the neck. It corrects alignment without correcting the underlying head-neck offset, so residual impingement is common.
Imhäuser osteotomyChronic SCFE with a moderate slip and impingement.Intertrochanteric flexion, valgus and derotation osteotomy, often combined with a head-neck osteochondroplasty.Similar rationale to the Southwick; the combination with osteochondroplasty addresses impingement directly.
Modified Dunn procedure (subcapital realignment)Severe SCFE, particularly unstable slips, where anatomic correction of the deformity is desired.Performed through a surgical hip dislocation with a trochanteric flip osteotomy; the retinacular flap containing the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery is elevated, the physis is opened, the callus resected, and the epiphysis anatomically reduced onto the neck and fixed.Achieves the best anatomic correction but carries a significant AVN risk even in expert hands (reported 0–25%+). Absolutely dependent on protecting the MFCA retinacular flap. High-volume centres only.
Ganz surgical hip dislocation with trochanteric flipAccess for femoral head fractures (Pipkin), FAI osteochondroplasty, modified Dunn, osteochondral lesions, and intra-articular pathology.Lateral approach, trochanteric flip (digastric) osteotomy leaving gluteus medius and vastus lateralis attached, Z-shaped anterior capsulotomy, anterior dislocation with the leg in a sterile bag.Preserves the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery by keeping the obturator externus intact — the key safety principle. Gives 360° access to the head. Trochanteric nonunion and heterotopic ossification are the main complications.
Trochanteric advancement / distalisationTrochanteric overgrowth with abductor insufficiency and a Trendelenburg gait after Perthes, septic arthritis of infancy, or a prior VDRO.The greater trochanter is osteotomised and moved distally and laterally, restoring the abductor lever arm and the articulotrochanteric distance.Improves gait and reduces impingement; a trochanteric epiphysiodesis performed early in a growing child can prevent the problem altogether.
Schanz osteotomyHistorical salvage for a painful, unreconstructible high-riding hip (untreated DDH, failed reconstruction) in a low-demand patient.Subtrochanteric abduction (valgus) osteotomy that redirects the femoral shaft so that the proximal fragment articulates against the pelvis.Largely of historical interest; arthroplasty has replaced it in most settings, but it appears in exam lists of salvage options.
KNEE & TIBIA
High tibial osteotomy — medial opening wedgeMedial compartment osteoarthritis with VARUS malalignment in a young, active patient with good range of motion, intact lateral compartment and ligaments, and a BMI within reason.Medial incomplete cut below the tibial tubercle, hinged on the lateral cortex, opened to the planned correction and held with a locked plate ± graft.Overcorrect to slight valgus (mechanical axis passing through the Fujisawa point, ~62% across the plateau). Advantages: adjustable, no fibular osteotomy, avoids the peroneal nerve. Disadvantages: it INCREASES posterior tibial slope (mitigate by opening the gap anteriorly less than posteriorly) and lowers the patella (patella baja), complicating later arthroplasty.
High tibial osteotomy — lateral closing wedge (Coventry)Same indication as the opening wedge.Lateral closing wedge above the tubercle, usually with a fibular osteotomy or proximal tibiofibular joint disruption, fixed with staples or a plate.Heals more reliably (bone-on-bone) and allows earlier weightbearing, but risks the PERONEAL NERVE, shortens the limb, and removes bone stock that complicates later TKA. Decreases posterior slope.
Distal femoral osteotomy (medial closing or lateral opening wedge)Lateral compartment osteoarthritis with VALGUS malalignment, where the deformity is femoral in origin (abnormal LDFA).Osteotomy just proximal to the condyles, correcting to a neutral or slightly under-corrected mechanical axis, fixed with a locking plate.Measure the LDFA and MPTA first — correcting on the wrong side of the joint creates joint line obliquity, which is poorly tolerated. Valgus deformity is usually femoral; varus is usually tibial.
Tibial tubercle osteotomy — Fulkerson (anteromedialisation)Patellofemoral instability or lateral/distal patellar chondrosis with an elevated TT-TG, in a skeletally mature patient.An oblique osteotomy of the tubercle in the coronal plane; sliding the tubercle along the oblique cut moves it both medially and anteriorly, the ratio determined by the obliquity of the cut.Anteriorisation unloads the distal and lateral patellar facets — so it is ideal for distal/lateral chondrosis and contraindicated in proximal or medial facet or diffuse patellar chondrosis, which it makes worse. Requires closed physes.
Tibial tubercle osteotomy — Elmslie-Trillat (medialisation)Patellar instability with an elevated TT-TG but no significant chondral wear.A flat, sagittal osteotomy allowing pure medial translation of the tubercle, fixed with two screws.Purely medialising, with no anteriorisation and therefore no unloading effect. Distalisation can be added for patella alta (Caton-Deschamps >1.3).
Maquet osteotomy (anteriorisation)Historical — patellofemoral arthritis.Pure anterior elevation of the tubercle on a bone block.Largely abandoned because of skin necrosis over the prominent tubercle and a high complication rate.
Distal femoral extension osteotomy with patellar tendon advancementFixed knee flexion contracture with crouch gait in ambulatory cerebral palsy (GMFCS II–III) — typically >20–30° of fixed contracture.Anterior closing-wedge extension osteotomy of the distal femur with a blade or locking plate, plus advancement/plication of the patellar tendon to correct the associated patella alta and extensor lag.Isolated hamstring lengthening is inadequate for a FIXED contracture and risks worsening crouch. The patellar tendon advancement is essential — the extensor mechanism is chronically stretched, and correcting the bone alone leaves an extensor lag. Never over-lengthen the Achilles in crouch — it is the classic irreversible error.
Proximal tibial valgus osteotomy for Blount diseaseInfantile or adolescent tibia vara with progressive deformity, Langenskiöld III+ or failure of guided growth.Valgus and derotational osteotomy of the proximal tibia (with a fibular osteotomy), often with slight overcorrection; acute correction with plating, or gradual with an external fixator.Slight overcorrection is desirable because recurrence is common, especially under age 4 and in the obese adolescent. In Langenskiöld V–VI, an established physeal bar must be resected or the physis completed or the deformity simply recurs. Watch for compartment syndrome and peroneal nerve palsy after acute correction.
Derotational tibial osteotomySymptomatic excessive tibial torsion (in-toeing or out-toeing) persisting past age 8–10, or as part of correction of miserable malalignment.Supramalleolar (safer) or proximal tibial rotational osteotomy with fibular osteotomy, fixed with plate or pins and cast.Supramalleolar is preferred — the proximal tibia carries a higher risk of compartment syndrome and peroneal nerve injury. Most rotational profiles resolve spontaneously; operate only for a significant, persistent, functionally limiting deformity.
FOOT & ANKLE
Dwyer lateral closing-wedge calcaneal osteotomy (1959)Hindfoot VARUS — cavovarus foot (CMT), post-traumatic varus, where the Coleman block test shows the deformity is hindfoot-driven.Oblique lateral incision behind the peroneals; a laterally based closing wedge is removed from the calcaneal tuberosity and closed, correcting varus. A lateralising translational osteotomy achieves a similar effect without removing bone.The Coleman block test decides between this and a first metatarsal osteotomy: if the hindfoot varus corrects on the block, the driver is the plantarflexed first ray, and a dorsiflexion first metatarsal osteotomy is the answer instead. Both are often needed.
Medialising (medial displacement) calcaneal osteotomy — MDCOFlexible flatfoot / stage II posterior tibial tendon dysfunction with hindfoot VALGUS.Oblique lateral osteotomy of the tuberosity, translated medially ~1 cm and fixed with a screw, moving the Achilles line of pull medial to the subtalar axis.Converts the Achilles from a hindfoot evertor into an invertor. Usually combined with an FDL transfer to the navicular. The sural nerve is at risk with the lateral incision. Assess for equinus and add a gastrocnemius recession.
Evans lateral column lengtheningFlexible flatfoot with significant FOREFOOT ABDUCTION (talonavicular uncoverage) — a component of stage II PTTD and of paediatric planovalgus.Opening-wedge osteotomy of the anterior calcaneus ~1–1.5 cm proximal to the calcaneocuboid joint, distracted with a graft or wedge.Addresses abduction, which the MDCO cannot. Over-lengthening causes lateral column overload and calcaneocuboid arthritis — the principal complication. Often combined with an MDCO and a Cotton osteotomy.
Cotton osteotomy (medial cuneiform opening wedge)Residual forefoot SUPINATION / elevation of the first ray after hindfoot correction in flatfoot reconstruction.Dorsal opening-wedge osteotomy of the medial cuneiform with a graft, plantarflexing the first ray to restore a plantigrade tripod.The finishing step of a flatfoot reconstruction — check for residual forefoot varus after correcting the hindfoot and add this if the first ray does not reach the floor.
Dorsiflexion (extension) osteotomy of the first metatarsalForefoot-driven cavovarus — a plantarflexed first ray, when the Coleman block test corrects.Dorsally based closing-wedge osteotomy at the base of the first metatarsal, elevating the first ray.The forefoot-driven counterpart to the Dwyer. Do not shorten excessively — transfer metatarsalgia follows.
Chevron / Austin osteotomyMild to moderate hallux valgus — HVA <30–40°, IMA <13–15°, with a congruent or mildly incongruent joint.A V-shaped osteotomy in the first metatarsal head, apex distal, with lateral translation of the capital fragment and fixation with a screw or K-wire.Correction is limited by the width of the metatarsal — roughly 5–6 mm of translation. AVN of the metatarsal head is the feared complication, historically increased by combining an aggressive lateral release with the osteotomy. A biplanar chevron can also correct an increased DMAA, which matters in juvenile hallux valgus.
Scarf osteotomyModerate hallux valgus, IMA up to ~15–18°.A long Z-shaped (step-cut) diaphyseal osteotomy of the first metatarsal allowing translation, rotation and lengthening/shortening, fixed with two screws.Powerful and stable with a large cancellous contact area allowing early weightbearing. 'Troughing' (the fragments telescoping into each other) is the characteristic complication, causing loss of correction and elevation of the first ray.
Ludloff and Mau osteotomiesModerate to severe hallux valgus with a large IMA.Oblique diaphyseal osteotomies of the first metatarsal — the Ludloff runs proximal-dorsal to distal-plantar; the Mau is the mirror image (proximal-plantar to distal-dorsal).The Mau is more stable under weightbearing load because the plane resists dorsal displacement. Both permit greater correction than a distal osteotomy.
Akin osteotomyResidual hallux valgus interphalangeus — deformity within the proximal phalanx, or a residual valgus after metatarsal correction.Medial closing-wedge osteotomy of the proximal phalanx, fixed with a screw or staple.A supplementary, not a primary, procedure — it corrects the appearance of the toe but does nothing to the IMA. Used as the final step to balance the great toe.
Lapidus procedure (first tarsometatarsal arthrodesis)Severe hallux valgus with a large IMA (>15–18°), first ray HYPERMOBILITY, or recurrence after a previous correction; also with generalised ligamentous laxity.Resection of the first TMT joint surfaces with correction of the IMA and fixation with screws or a plate. Strictly an arthrodesis, but performed for the same deformity.Contraindicated with an OPEN physis — it crosses the base of the first metatarsal. Nonunion and shortening of the first ray with transfer metatarsalgia are the main risks. Powerful for the hypermobile or recurrent deformity.
Weil osteotomyMetatarsalgia and lesser MTP instability/dislocation — a long or plantarflexed lesser metatarsal.Oblique intra-articular osteotomy of the lesser metatarsal head, translated proximally to shorten the ray, fixed with a small screw.'Floating toe' (loss of MTP ground contact) is the characteristic complication, from the intrinsics being rendered non-functional as the head translates proximally. Respect the metatarsal cascade — shortening one ray may require shortening the next.
Supramalleolar osteotomyDistal tibial varus or valgus malalignment with asymmetric ankle arthritis, in a joint that is still congruent and salvageable.Opening- or closing-wedge osteotomy of the distal tibia (± fibular osteotomy) proximal to the plafond, correcting the tibial articular surface angle.Realigns load away from the worn compartment and can defer arthrodesis or replacement in a young patient. Also used to correct the hindfoot alignment before total ankle replacement — a total ankle placed on an uncorrected varus hindfoot will fail.
Calcaneal Z / Malerba osteotomyComplex hindfoot deformity requiring both translation and wedge correction.A Z-shaped calcaneal osteotomy permitting simultaneous translation, wedge removal and lengthening.More versatile than a single-plane osteotomy but technically demanding, with more fixation required.
UPPER LIMB
Ulnar shortening osteotomyUlnar impaction (ulnocarpal abutment) syndrome with POSITIVE ulnar variance; some TFCC tears; distal radius malunion with relative ulnar positive variance.Transverse or oblique diaphyseal osteotomy of the ulna with removal of a segment to bring variance to neutral or slightly negative, fixed with a compression plate.An oblique cut with a lag screw gives better rotational stability and union than a transverse cut. It also tightens the ulnocarpal ligaments, which helps in DRUJ instability. Contraindicated in significant DRUJ arthritis — it loads the joint. Plate irritation frequently requires removal.
Wafer procedureUlnar impaction with minimally positive variance (<4 mm) and no DRUJ instability.Open or arthroscopic resection of the distal 2–4 mm of the ulnar dome, preserving the styloid and the foveal TFCC attachment.Avoids a diaphyseal osteotomy, hardware and nonunion, but corrects less and does not tighten the ulnocarpal ligaments. Preserve the foveal TFCC insertion or you create DRUJ instability.
Darrach procedureSymptomatic DRUJ arthritis in a LOW-DEMAND, elderly patient; salvage after failed DRUJ surgery.Resection of the distal ulna (limited, subperiosteal, preserving the TFCC and stabilising soft tissues where possible).Avoid in young or high-demand patients — painful convergence and radioulnar impingement is common and difficult to salvage. Stabilise the remaining ulna (e.g. ECU or pronator quadratus interposition).
Sauvé-Kapandji procedureDRUJ arthritis in a YOUNGER, higher-demand patient, particularly with ulnar translation of the carpus or rheumatoid disease.Arthrodesis of the DRUJ combined with creation of a pseudarthrosis (segmental resection) in the distal ulnar neck to restore forearm rotation.Preserves ulnar support of the carpus, which the Darrach does not — the reason it is preferred in the young and in rheumatoid patients with ulnar carpal translation. Instability of the proximal ulnar stump is the main complication.
Corrective osteotomy for distal radius malunionSymptomatic dorsal malunion with loss of volar tilt, radial shortening and DRUJ incongruity, causing pain, weakness and limited rotation.Opening-wedge osteotomy through the malunion with structural graft (or a volar locking plate with 3D-printed patient-specific guides), restoring inclination, height and volar tilt.Earlier correction (within 3–6 months) gives better results before secondary carpal adaptation and DRUJ arthritis. Assess and address ulnar variance — an ulnar shortening may be needed as well or instead.
Lateral closing-wedge (French) osteotomy for cubitus varusSymptomatic cubitus varus (gunstock deformity) after supracondylar malunion — for cosmesis, for posterolateral rotatory instability, or for tardy ulnar palsy.Lateral closing wedge of the distal humerus, planned from the contralateral carrying angle, fixed with plate, screws or crossed K-wires.A dome osteotomy or step-cut osteotomy corrects better in multiple planes and avoids the lateral condylar prominence that a simple closing wedge creates. Cubitus varus is a MALUNION, not a growth arrest — it does not remodel and it does not progress.
Humeral derotational osteotomyFixed internal rotation contracture after obstetric brachial plexus palsy, in a shoulder with an incongruent joint that cannot be reduced.External rotation osteotomy of the proximal humeral shaft, fixed with a plate.It improves function in a fixed contracture but does NOT treat glenohumeral dysplasia, and it can worsen anterior instability by rotating the head out of a posteriorly deficient glenoid. Joint-preserving anterior release with latissimus/teres major transfer is preferred when the joint can still be reduced.
Derotational osteotomy for congenital radioulnar synostosisSevere fixed hyperpronation (>60°), especially when bilateral or when it prevents functional tasks.Osteotomy through the synostosis mass (or of both bones) with rotation into a functional position — neutral to 10–20° pronation for a unilateral case; asymmetric positions for bilateral.The goal is REPOSITIONING, not restoration of rotation — excision and interposition to recreate motion has poor, unpredictable results with recurrence. Acute large corrections risk neurovascular compromise and compartment syndrome — correct gradually with an external fixator for large angles.
Scaphoid osteotomy / wedge grafting (Fisk-Fernandez)Scaphoid nonunion with humpback deformity and a DISI pattern.Volar approach, resection of the fibrous nonunion, insertion of a structural volar wedge (tricortical iliac crest) graft to restore scaphoid length and correct the flexion deformity, fixed with a headless compression screw.Correcting the humpback and restoring the radiolunate angle is the point — a screw across an uncorrected nonunion leaves a shortened, flexed scaphoid and persistent DISI. Assess vascularity: a vascularised graft (1,2-ICSRA or medial femoral condyle) is indicated for proximal pole AVN.
Glenoid osteotomy (posterior opening wedge)Recurrent posterior shoulder instability with excessive glenoid RETROVERSION (>20°) and failed soft-tissue stabilisation.Posterior approach, opening-wedge osteotomy of the glenoid neck with a bone graft to reduce retroversion.Technically demanding with a real risk of intra-articular fracture, AVN of the glenoid and over-correction into anterior instability. Reserved for genuine bony retroversion; most posterior instability is treated with capsulolabral repair or a posterior bone block.
SPINE
Smith-Petersen osteotomy (SPO) / Ponte osteotomySmooth, multi-level sagittal deformity with MOBILE anterior discs — flatback syndrome, Scheuermann kyphosis, adolescent deformity correction.Posterior column resection: removal of the spinous process, inferior and superior facets and ligamentum flavum at the level, hinging on the posterior aspect of the disc, closed with compression.~10° of correction per level, and it is ADDITIVE — four levels give ~40°. Requires a mobile anterior column (a patent disc); it will not work across an ankylosed segment. Lower blood loss and neurologic risk per level than a PSO. A Ponte is the same manoeuvre described in the thoracic spine for Scheuermann and scoliosis.
Pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO)Fixed, sharp, angular sagittal imbalance requiring large correction at one level — ankylosing spondylitis, post-traumatic kyphosis, fixed flatback after a prior fusion where the discs are fused.A three-column wedge resection: bilateral pedicle removal and a wedge of the vertebral body, closed posteriorly to hinge on the anterior cortex, over long instrumentation.~30° of correction at a single level, usually performed at L3 or L4. Substantially greater blood loss, longer operative time, and higher neurologic and pseudarthrosis risk than SPOs. When multiple mobile discs are available, several SPOs achieve the same correction with lower morbidity — the classic 'least invasive option' answer. Use dual/accessory rods across the osteotomy to reduce rod fracture.
Vertebral column resection (VCR)Severe, rigid, angular deformity — congenital kyphoscoliosis, post-traumatic or post-infective gibbus, hemivertebra with severe deformity, resection of a tumour with deformity.Complete removal of one or more vertebral bodies with the adjacent discs, anterior column reconstruction with a cage, over long segmental instrumentation. Can be posterior-only (PVCR) or combined.The most powerful correction available and the most morbid — the highest neurologic risk of any deformity procedure. Neuromonitoring is mandatory, cord shortening rather than lengthening is the safe direction, and the spinal column is transiently completely destabilised.
Hemivertebra excisionProgressive congenital scoliosis from a fully segmented hemivertebra, particularly at the lumbosacral junction or thoracolumbar region causing trunk imbalance.Posterior (or combined) resection of the hemivertebra and both adjacent discs with short-segment instrumented fusion of the two adjacent vertebrae.Best performed EARLY (age 2–5 years), when the curve is short and flexible — a short fusion then beats a long fusion later. Definitive correction of the deformity at its source. Neuromonitoring essential; image the neuraxis first, as 30–40% have intraspinal anomalies.
Kyphectomy (vertebral resection for myelomeningocele)Rigid congenital kyphosis (gibbus) in myelomeningocele causing recurrent skin ulceration over the apex (the commonest indication), impaired sitting balance and restrictive lung disease.Resection of the apical vertebrae with long posterior instrumentation to the pelvis; the dural sac may be ligated below the neurologic level, since function below is already absent.High complication rate — wound breakdown, infection and hardware failure. All care must be LATEX-FREE (latex allergy is present in up to 20–70% of these patients and can cause intraoperative anaphylaxis).
Cervicothoracic (C7) osteotomy for ankylosing spondylitisFixed cervical flexion (chin-on-chest) deformity in ankylosing spondylitis, preventing horizontal gaze, eating and personal care.Posterior extension osteotomy at C7–T1 (below the vertebral artery's entry into the transverse foramen at C6), classically performed awake under local anaesthesia with the head in a halo so the neurologic status can be monitored during correction.Performed at C7 specifically because the vertebral artery lies outside the transverse foramen at that level. The characteristic complication is bilateral C8 nerve root palsy — numbness and pain along the ulnar border of both hands with intrinsic weakness — from foraminal narrowing during closure; it usually improves with time.
LIMB RECONSTRUCTION
Corticotomy with distraction osteogenesis (Ilizarov)Limb-length discrepancy, segmental bone defect, nonunion with shortening, deformity correction, congenital shortening.A low-energy, periosteum-preserving corticotomy (traditionally metaphyseal, where the blood supply and osteogenic potential are greatest), followed by a latency period of 5–7 days, then distraction at ~1 mm/day divided into four increments (0.25 mm × 4), then a consolidation phase.Rate and rhythm are the essential numbers: too fast → poor regenerate and nerve/vessel injury; too slow → premature consolidation. The distraction-consolidation index is typically 30–45 days per cm. Pin-site infection is near-universal; nerve palsy, joint contracture and subluxation, and axial deviation are the other complications. Protect the adjacent joints with physiotherapy and bracing.
Guided growth (hemiepiphysiodesis, tension-band plating)Angular deformity around the knee or ankle in a child with SUBSTANTIAL REMAINING GROWTH — genu varum/valgum, ankle valgus, deformity in rickets and skeletal dysplasia.An extraperiosteal tension-band plate with two screws is placed across the physis on the convex side, tethering that side while the concave side continues to grow, gradually correcting the deformity.Requires sufficient remaining growth — it fails with a closed or closing physis, an established physeal bar, or an intra-articular/bone-loss deformity. Over-correction happens quickly, especially in the young — monitor closely and remove the implant promptly. Rebound deformity after removal is well described in the very young. Implant failure is more common in the obese adolescent.
Physeal bar resection with interpositionA partial physeal arrest causing progressive angular deformity or shortening, where the bar is <50% of the physeal cross-sectional area and there are >2 years (or >2 cm) of growth remaining.The bar is mapped on CT or MRI, resected under image guidance or through a metaphyseal window, and the defect filled with interposition material — fat graft, cranioplast/PMMA or silicone to prevent recurrence; concurrent osteotomy corrects existing deformity.If the bar is >50% or minimal growth remains, do NOT resect — complete the epiphysiodesis and treat the deformity and length separately (contralateral epiphysiodesis, osteotomy or lengthening). Peripheral bars are easier to resect and have better outcomes than central ones.
