Named views and eponyms — how each is positioned, what it demonstrates, and where it fails. The performance column carries the sensitivity and specificity points that get tested.

View / eponymHow it is takenWhat it showsPerformance & pearls
SHOULDER & CHEST WALL
Grashey view (true AP of the glenohumeral joint)Patient rotated 30–45° toward the affected side (or the beam angled 30–45° medial to lateral) so the beam runs parallel to the glenoid face and perpendicular to the scapular plane.The true glenohumeral joint space, free of overlap between the humeral head and glenoid.A standard AP superimposes the head on the glenoid and cannot exclude a posterior dislocation or assess joint space. Loss of joint space on a Grashey is the plain-film hallmark of glenohumeral OA.
Axillary lateralArm abducted 70–90°, cassette superior to the shoulder, beam directed from inferior (axilla) to superior.Direction of dislocation, glenoid rim fractures, humeral head position, os acromiale.The single most important second view of the shoulder — a posterior dislocation is missed on AP alone in up to 50–79% of cases. If the patient cannot abduct, use a Velpeau.
Velpeau axillaryArm remains in the sling; patient leans backward ~30° over the cassette placed on the table; beam directed vertically from above downward.The same information as an axillary lateral without requiring abduction.The answer for the acutely painful or unstable shoulder that cannot be abducted — obtain this rather than accepting an AP alone.
West Point axillary (Rockwood modification)Patient PRONE with the arm abducted 90° and hanging over the table edge; beam angled 25° downward from horizontal and 25° medial, entering the axilla.The anteroinferior glenoid rim — bony Bankart lesions and glenoid rim erosion.The best plain film for anteroinferior glenoid bone loss, though CT with 3D reconstruction and humeral head subtraction is the modern gold standard for quantifying loss.
Stryker notch viewPatient supine, palm of the affected hand on top of the head with the elbow pointing straight up; beam angled 10° cephalad centred on the coracoid.The posterolateral humeral head — Hill-Sachs lesion.Pairs with the West Point view: Stryker for the humeral side, West Point for the glenoid side.
Garth view (apical oblique)Patient rotated 45° toward the affected side (as for a Grashey) with the beam angled 45° caudad.Both the anteroinferior glenoid rim and the Hill-Sachs lesion, plus glenohumeral dislocation.A useful single view in the emergency setting when patient positioning is limited.
Scapular Y (trans-scapular lateral)Patient rotated 45–60° anterior oblique so the scapular body is edge-on; beam parallel to the scapular spine.Direction of dislocation (head anterior or posterior to the glenoid at the centre of the Y), scapular body fractures.Less reliable than an axillary for subtle posterior subluxation, and easily malpositioned.
Supraspinatus outlet viewA scapular Y with the beam angled 10–15° caudad.Acromial morphology (Bigliani type I flat, II curved, III hooked) and the subacromial outlet; os acromiale.Acromial morphology is now considered a weak predictor of cuff tearing; the critical shoulder angle and acromial index have largely supplanted it.
Zanca viewAP of the AC joint with 10–15° cephalic tilt and reduced (about 50%) penetration.The acromioclavicular joint — distal clavicle osteolysis, AC separation, distal clavicle fracture.A standard AP shoulder over-penetrates the AC joint; the Zanca is the dedicated view. Bilateral Zanca views allow side-to-side comparison of the coracoclavicular distance.
Serendipity viewSupine, beam angled 40° cephalad, centred on the manubrium; both clavicles on one film.The sternoclavicular joints — anterior vs posterior dislocation (the affected medial clavicle appears displaced superiorly if anterior, inferiorly if posterior).CT with angiography is required for a suspected posterior SC dislocation — the plain film is only a screen, and posterior dislocation threatens the great vessels, trachea and oesophagus.
Bernageau (glenoid profile) viewProne oblique with the arm elevated, beam tangential to the glenoid face.The anteroinferior glenoid profile for erosion.Used mainly in European practice as a pre-Latarjet assessment.
ELBOW & FOREARM
Radiocapitellar (Greenspan) viewLateral elbow with the beam angled 45° cephalad toward the radial head.The radial head free of overlap from the ulna and coronoid.Reveals occult radial head fractures not visible on standard AP and lateral.
Jones viewAP taken with the elbow fully flexed, forearm on the cassette.The distal humerus and olecranon when the elbow cannot be extended.Useful in acute trauma where extension is impossible.
Anterior fat pad / posterior fat pad signStandard true lateral in 90° flexion.An elevated anterior ('sail') fat pad or any visible posterior fat pad indicates an intra-articular effusion.A posterior fat pad sign in an adult implies an occult radial head fracture in ~75–90%; in a child it suggests a supracondylar fracture. A normal anterior fat pad may be seen; the posterior fat pad is always pathological.
WRIST & HAND
PA in neutral rotation (zero-rotation view)Shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed 90°, forearm and wrist flat in neutral rotation, beam perpendicular.The reference view for ulnar variance and carpal alignment.Ulnar variance changes with rotation and grip — pronation and grip make the ulna appear more positive. Standardised positioning is essential before diagnosing ulnar impaction.
Scaphoid viewPA with the wrist in ulnar deviation, ± beam angled 20–30° cephalad, which extends the scaphoid.The scaphoid waist elongated and free of foreshortening.Initial radiographs miss up to 25–40% of scaphoid fractures. If clinically suspected with normal films, immobilise and repeat at 10–14 days, or obtain MRI (the most sensitive early test) or CT.
Clenched-fist (stress) PA viewPA with the patient making a tight fist, which drives the capitate proximally.Dynamic scapholunate widening — the Terry Thomas / David Letterman sign.Reveals dynamic instability invisible on a static PA; compare with the contralateral side.
Carpal tunnel view (Gaynor-Hart)Wrist maximally dorsiflexed with the beam angled 25–30° to the long axis of the hand.The hook of hamate, pisiform, and the trapezial ridge.CT is more reliable for a suspected hook of hamate fracture in a golfer or racquet athlete; the plain view is often equivocal.
Robert's view (true AP of the thumb)Forearm hyperpronated so the dorsum of the thumb lies against the cassette.The thumb carpometacarpal joint and the base of the first metacarpal.Essential for assessing Bennett and Rolando fractures and basal joint arthritis.
Bett's viewHand pronated 20–30° with the beam angled 15° proximal to distal.A true lateral of the thumb CMC joint.Pairs with Robert's view for basal thumb assessment.
Brewerton viewMCP joints flexed 65° with the dorsum of the fingers on the cassette, beam angled 15° from the ulnar side.The metacarpal heads and MCP collateral recesses — early erosions and collateral ligament avulsions.Useful for early inflammatory arthritis and for occult metacarpal head lesions.
HIP & PELVIS
AP pelvisSupine, legs internally rotated 15° to compensate for femoral anteversion; coccyx centred 1–3 cm above the symphysis, obturator foramina symmetric.Femoral head sphericity, joint space, acetabular coverage, Shenton's line, version and coverage indices.Pelvic tilt and rotation change every measurement made on this film. Standing AP pelvis is increasingly preferred for arthroplasty and dysplasia planning because it captures the functional pelvic position.
Frog-leg lateral (Lauenstein)Supine with hips flexed and abducted 45°, knees flexed, soles together.The anterior femoral head-neck junction; the classic view for SCFE and for femoral head AVN.Do NOT obtain a frog-leg lateral in a suspected femoral neck fracture or an unstable SCFE — the positioning can displace the fracture. Use a cross-table lateral.
Cross-table (Danelius-Miller) lateralContralateral hip flexed out of the field; beam horizontal, angled 45° to the affected femoral neck; cassette against the ipsilateral pelvis.A safe lateral of the femoral neck in acute trauma and the standard view for measuring acetabular anteversion after THA.The safe alternative to the frog-leg lateral when a fracture or unstable slip is suspected.
Dunn view (45° or 90°)Supine, hip flexed 45° (or 90°), abducted 20°, neutral rotation, beam perpendicular.The anterosuperior head-neck junction — the standard view for cam morphology and the alpha angle.The 45° Dunn is the most sensitive plain view for cam deformity. A standard AP and cross-table lateral together miss a substantial proportion of cam lesions.
False profile view (Lequesne)Standing, pelvis rotated 65° to the cassette with the ipsilateral foot parallel to the cassette; beam centred on the femoral head.ANTERIOR acetabular coverage — the anterior centre-edge angle; also the posteroinferior joint space.The dedicated view for anterior coverage, which the AP cannot assess. Essential in the work-up for periacetabular osteotomy.
Judet obliquesPatient rolled 45°. Obturator oblique = rolled toward the unaffected side, raising the affected hemipelvis. Iliac oblique = rolled onto the affected side.Obturator oblique: ANTERIOR column + POSTERIOR wall (and the obturator foramen 'en face'). Iliac oblique: POSTERIOR column + ANTERIOR wall (and the iliac wing 'en face').The spur sign, pathognomonic of an associated both-column fracture, is seen on the OBTURATOR OBLIQUE. CT has largely replaced the Judet views for classification but they remain the language of the classification.
Inlet and outlet pelvic viewsInlet: beam angled 40° caudad (toward the feet). Outlet: beam angled 40° cephalad.Inlet shows anterior/posterior displacement and internal rotation of the hemipelvis. Outlet shows superior/inferior (vertical) displacement, sacral foraminal lines and flexion/extension.Mnemonic: outlet for 'up', inlet for 'in'. Both are needed to define a pelvic ring injury and to guide percutaneous iliosacral screws.
Von Rosen viewSupine with hips abducted 45° and fully internally rotated.A line along the femoral shaft should intersect the acetabulum; in DDH it points superolateral to the acetabulum.Largely historical — ultrasound is the standard until the femoral head ossifies at ~4–6 months, after which an AP pelvis is used.
KNEE
Rosenberg view (45° flexion PA weightbearing)Standing, knees flexed 45°, PA projection, beam angled 10° caudad.Posterior femoral condyle cartilage wear and true joint space narrowing.More sensitive than a standing extension AP for detecting joint space narrowing — it loads the part of the condyle that wears first. The answer when an extension AP looks normal but the patient has clinical arthritis.
Merchant viewSupine, knees flexed 45° over the table edge, beam angled 30° caudad from horizontal, cassette perpendicular to the beam.The patellofemoral joint — congruence angle, patellar tilt, trochlear morphology.The Laurin view (20° flexion) is more sensitive for subtle lateral tilt and subluxation, since instability is greatest near extension. The 'sunrise/skyline' terminology is used loosely — specify the flexion angle.
Notch / tunnel viewPA with the knee flexed 40–50°, beam angled caudad (or the Rosenberg).The intercondylar notch — osteochondritis dissecans of the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle, loose bodies, notch osteophytes, ACL tunnel position.The dedicated view for OCD, which is easily hidden by the condyles on a standard AP.
Long-leg standing alignment (hip-to-ankle)Standing, full-length cassette or stitched images, patellae forward.The mechanical axis from the femoral head centre to the ankle centre, and its relationship to the knee.Mandatory before osteotomy or complex arthroplasty. Rotation of the limb markedly alters the measured alignment — the patellae must face forward.
Segond fracture (on AP)Standard AP knee.A small vertical avulsion of the lateral tibial plateau (anterolateral ligament / lateral capsular attachment).Pathognomonic of an ACL tear (~75–100% association) — and also associated with lateral meniscal tears. A 'reverse Segond' (medial plateau) suggests a PCL and medial meniscal injury.
FOOT & ANKLE
Ankle mortise viewAnkle internally rotated 15–20° so the intermalleolar axis is parallel to the cassette; beam perpendicular.The medial, superior and lateral clear spaces, tibiofibular clear space and overlap, talar tilt.All syndesmotic measurements are made 1 cm above the plafond. Note that tibiofibular overlap thresholds differ between the AP (>6 mm) and the mortise (>1 mm) — a classic trap.
Gravity / external rotation stress viewLeg supported with the foot unsupported (gravity), or the examiner applies external rotation stress to the foot.Widening of the medial clear space, indicating deep deltoid incompetence in a supination-external rotation fracture.A gravity stress view is as accurate as a manual stress view and is far better tolerated. A weightbearing view is an alternative that assesses functional stability.
Broden viewFoot in neutral dorsiflexion, leg internally rotated 30–40°, beam angled 10°, 20°, 30° and 40° cephalad in sequence.The posterior facet of the subtalar joint — reduction of a calcaneal fracture.Largely replaced by intra-operative 3D fluoroscopy or CT, but still the classic intra-operative check of posterior facet reduction.
Harris (axial) heel viewFoot maximally dorsiflexed, beam angled 45° cephalad through the heel.The calcaneal tuberosity — width, varus malalignment, and the sustentaculum.Detects the varus malunion and lateral wall blowout that cause peroneal impingement after a calcaneal fracture.
Canale viewFoot in maximum equinus, pronated 15°, beam angled 75° cephalad from horizontal.The talar neck, free of overlap — the view for assessing varus malalignment after talar neck fixation.Talar neck varus malunion is the commonest malreduction and produces a stiff, adducted foot; this view is how you avoid it.
Saltzman (hindfoot alignment) viewStanding, cassette behind the heel angled 20° from vertical, beam angled 20° from horizontal posteroanteriorly.The hindfoot moment arm — the relationship of the calcaneal tuberosity to the weightbearing axis of the tibia.The standard measure of hindfoot varus or valgus, essential in planning calcaneal osteotomy and total ankle replacement.
45° internal oblique footFoot oblique, 45° internal rotation.Calcaneonavicular coalition — the 'anteater nose' sign (elongated anterior process of the calcaneus).The dedicated view for the commonest tarsal coalition, which ossifies at 8–12 years.
Lateral foot — coalition signsStandard weightbearing lateral.'C sign' (continuous arc from the talar dome to the sustentaculum) and talar beaking suggest a talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalition, which ossifies at 12–16 years.CT is the best study for a talocalcaneal coalition; MRI is used for a fibrous or cartilaginous (non-ossified) coalition.
Weightbearing foot seriesStanding AP, lateral and oblique.True alignment for hallux valgus angles, Meary's angle, calcaneal pitch, Lisfranc alignment.Non-weightbearing films systematically underestimate deformity and can miss a subtle Lisfranc injury — always weightbear (or stress) when assessing alignment. Compare with the contralateral foot.
SPINE
Open-mouth odontoid viewAP with the mouth held open, beam centred through the open mouth.The dens, C1 lateral masses and the C1–C2 articulations.Rule of Spence: combined lateral mass overhang >6.9 mm implies transverse ligament disruption. In children, apparent lateral mass asymmetry is common and often positional — do not over-call it. CT is the definitive study.
Swimmer's viewLateral with the ipsilateral arm abducted overhead and the contralateral shoulder depressed.The cervicothoracic junction (C7–T1), obscured by the shoulders on a standard lateral.A lateral cervical film that does not show the top of T1 is inadequate. CT has largely replaced this in trauma.
Flexion-extension lateralsActive, patient-controlled flexion and extension, never passive or forced.Dynamic instability — translation and angulation between segments.Contraindicated in the acutely injured or obtunded patient (muscle spasm masks instability and forced motion is dangerous). Mandatory before anaesthesia in rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome, Morquio, SED and pseudoachondroplasia.
Cervical oblique views45° oblique.The neuroforamina and the facet joints.Superseded by MRI and CT for foraminal stenosis, but occasionally used to assess bony foraminal narrowing.
Lumbar oblique ('Scotty dog') view45° oblique lumbar.The pars interarticularis — a 'collar' on the Scotty dog indicates a pars defect (spondylolysis).Low sensitivity — SPECT or thin-slice CT is far more sensitive for an acute pars stress lesion, and MRI with STIR shows the marrow oedema of an active lesion that can still heal. Oblique views also roughly double the radiation dose.
Ferguson viewAP with the beam angled 30–35° cephalad.The L5–S1 disc space and the sacral ala en face.Useful for transitional anatomy and for assessing lumbosacral spondylolisthesis.
Standing full-length (36-inch) scoliosis seriesStanding PA (to reduce breast and thyroid dose) and lateral, arms on supports or on the clavicles for the lateral.Cobb angle, coronal and sagittal balance, spinopelvic parameters, Risser stage.PA rather than AP reduces breast dose substantially. EOS low-dose biplanar imaging further reduces exposure and is preferred where available for serial follow-up in a growing child.
Side-bending / fulcrum-bending / traction filmsSupine maximal lateral bending; fulcrum bending uses a radiolucent bolster under the curve apex; traction films apply axial pull.Curve FLEXIBILITY — the structural versus compensatory nature of each curve.Flexibility, not the standing Cobb angle, predicts the correction achievable. Fulcrum-bending correlates best for main thoracic curves; traction films are more useful in neuromuscular and very large curves.
Stagnara oblique (derotated) viewPatient rotated until the apical vertebra is seen in true profile.The true magnitude of the deformity and the apical vertebral anatomy in a severely rotated curve.Used for severe, rigid curves where standard projections distort the anatomy.
