Distal radius fracture: Indications, fixation & outcomes

The single most tested hand topic — acceptable parameters, who actually benefits from surgery, the technical traps of volar plating, and the named complications.

8 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2021 (x3), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024S (x3), 2024F (x3), 2025 (x6)

  • Normal radiographic parameters — know the normals before the acceptable limits:
    • Radial inclination ~22° (range 21–25°) on the PA view.
    • Radial height/length ~11–13 mm.
    • Volar (palmar) tilt ~11–12° on the lateral.
    • Ulnar variance ~0 (neutral), measured on a neutral-rotation PA with the shoulder abducted 90° and elbow flexed 90°.
    • Teardrop angle ~70° and the anteroposterior distance — both describe the volar lunate facet and are the best markers of a rotated volar rim fragment.
  • Acceptable non-operative parameters in an adult:
    • Radial inclination ≥15° (i.e. loss of no more than ~5°).
    • Radial height/length within 2–3 mm of the contralateral side.
    • Volar tilt from neutral (0°) to no more than ~20° DORSAL tilt — some sources use 10–15°.
    • Articular step-off <2 mm.
    • No DRUJ instability.
    • Loss of radial inclination and height shifts load to the ulnar side (ulnocarpal impaction) and reduces grip strength; loss of volar tilt shifts load dorsally and causes midcarpal instability.
  • Predictors of secondary displacement after a good closed reduction:
    • Age >60–65 — the strongest single predictor.
    • Dorsal metaphyseal comminution.
    • Initial dorsal tilt >20°.
    • Initial radial shortening and the degree of initial displacement.
    • Intra-articular extension.
    • An associated ulnar fracture.
    • Age >40 alone is NOT a strong predictor — the standard distractor in the "all of these except" format.
    • Lafontaine's original criteria (≥3 predicts instability) are still the framework, with age doing most of the work.
  • The elderly patient — the evidence that changed practice:
    • In patients over 65, multiple RCTs and the AAOS clinical practice guideline show that radiographic malalignment correlates poorly with function.
    • At one year, DASH and PRWE scores after cast treatment are equivalent to volar locking plate fixation.
    • Grip strength and early (3–6 month) function favour surgery, and the WRIST trial showed faster early recovery with plating without a 12-month difference.
    • Function and patient demand, not the radiograph, drive the decision in the low-demand elderly patient.
    • A malunion in this group is usually well tolerated; a malunion in a young manual worker is not.
  • Volar locking plate — the technical points that get tested:
    • Place the plate PROXIMAL to the watershed line. A plate distal to it abrades the flexor tendons and is the leading cause of flexor tendon rupture — FPL first (Soong grade 1–2 prominence predicts it).
    • Check a skyline/tangential view for dorsal screw penetration, which causes extensor tendon rupture (EPL and the fourth compartment). A standard lateral will miss it.
    • Restore volar tilt BEFORE locking the distal screws — the fixed-angle plate sets the reduction, it does not create it.
    • Distal locking screws should be subchondral and just short of the far cortex; length is judged on the pronated and supinated oblique views.
    • The FCR (Henry) approach is the workhorse. Its main technical difficulty is adequate exposure of the ulnar/volar-ulnar corner and the lunate facet, since the interval is radial to the radial artery and the pronator quadratus must be elevated; extending ulnarly risks the median nerve and the palmar cutaneous branch.
  • The volar lunate facet fragment — the critical fragment:
    • It is small, ulnar and volar, and it sits distal to the watershed line, so a standard volar plate does not reach it.
    • The short radiolunate ligament attaches to it, so if it is not captured the carpus subluxes volarly along with the fragment — the classic early post-operative failure, usually within 2 weeks.
    • Fixation requires a dedicated volar rim plate, a hook plate, or wire/suture (spring wire) fixation.
    • Suspect it when the teardrop angle is reduced or the AP distance is increased on the lateral.
  • The deforming forces — a directly asked item:
    • The brachioradialis is the deforming force on a radial styloid fragment, pulling it proximally and into radial deviation, and causing loss of radial height and inclination.
    • It is innervated by the RADIAL nerve.
    • Practical consequence: release or partially release the brachioradialis to reduce a shortened, radially deviated fracture, particularly in a comminuted or older fracture.
    • Pronator quadratus contributes to the volar-ulnar deforming pull; the extrinsic finger flexors and extensors cause axial shortening.
  • Bridge plating and external fixation:
    • Dorsal bridge (spanning) plate from the radius to the second or third metacarpal is used for highly comminuted intra-articular fractures, osteoporotic bone, and polytrauma.
    • Its distinctive advantage is that it permits immediate weight-bearing through the limb (crutches, walker, transfers) — the usual exam answer.
    • It is removed at 3–4 months; expect some loss of wrist motion, and the second-metacarpal construct passes between the second and third extensor compartments (ECRL/ECRB) with a risk of tendon irritation.
    • External fixation relies on LIGAMENTOTAXIS, which cannot reduce a depressed die-punch (central impaction) fragment — the volar and dorsal ligaments do not attach to it — and cannot restore volar tilt.
    • Over-distraction causes stiffness, finger contracture and CRPS; pin-site infection and radial sensory nerve injury are the other classic complications.
  • The DRUJ and the ulnar styloid:
    • Assess DRUJ stability intra-operatively after fixing the radius, in neutral, pronation and supination, comparing with the other side.
    • An ulnar styloid fracture per se does NOT need fixation and does not affect outcome — the well-established negative finding.
    • What matters is a large BASILAR ulnar styloid fracture with an unstable DRUJ, because the deep (foveal) fibres of the TFCC attach there.
    • If the DRUJ is reducible but unstable after radius fixation: immobilise in supination in a long-arm/Munster cast for ~6 weeks, or pin the DRUJ in supination; fix a large styloid fragment or repair the foveal TFCC if it is a small avulsion.
    • If the DRUJ is irreducible, suspect an interposed ECU tendon or an entrapped TFCC and open it.
  • EPL rupture — the counter-intuitive association:
    • It classically follows a MINIMALLY DISPLACED or NON-DISPLACED distal radius fracture treated NON-OPERATIVELY, not a badly displaced one.
    • The mechanism is a watershed of vascular supply in the third dorsal compartment at Lister's tubercle, with attrition and ischaemia inside an intact, tight, swollen compartment. A displaced fracture decompresses the compartment.
    • Onset is typically weeks to a few months after injury, with sudden loss of thumb retropulsion.
    • Treatment is EIP-to-EPL tendon transfer — direct repair is impossible because the tendon ends are attritional and frayed over a long segment.
    • After volar plating, the equivalent problem is dorsal screw prominence causing EPL or fourth-compartment rupture.
  • Median nerve problems — three distinct scenarios:
    • Acute carpal tunnel syndrome after a distal radius fracture — progressive, worsening median symptoms with a tense compartment: this is urgent carpal tunnel release, not observation. Distinguish it from a transient median contusion at the time of injury, which improves after reduction.
    • Persistent symptoms after a release that never worked → incomplete release of the distal transverse carpal ligament/distal antebrachial fascia.
    • Recurrent symptoms after an initially successful release, with a tender Tinel over the radial aspect of the distal wrist crease and normal digital two-point discrimination → palmar cutaneous branch neuroma from an incision placed too radially.
  • Other outcomes worth knowing:
    • Distal radius fracture is the injury classically associated with post-injury psychological distress and depression in the trauma literature, and with a disproportionate loss of independence in the elderly.
    • It is a sentinel fragility fracture in anyone over 50 — it mandates bone-density testing and typically precedes a hip fracture by 10–15 years.
    • CRPS is the classic non-union-related complication; vitamin C 500 mg daily for 50 days has trial support (though later meta-analyses are less convincing), and avoiding over-distraction and tight casts matters more.

Extensor tendon injuries: EPL rupture, sagittal band & deformities

The extensor apparatus made mechanical — what each structure does, the named deformities, and the tests that distinguish them.

7 of 10 sittings2018 (x3), 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x2), 2024F (x2)

  • Anatomy of the extensor apparatus — the four things to hold in mind:
    • The extrinsic extensors (EDC) extend the MCP joint; they contribute little to IP extension on their own.
    • The intrinsics (interossei and lumbricals) FLEX the MCP and EXTEND the IP joints, via the lateral bands. This is the key to every intrinsic/extrinsic question.
    • The central slip inserts on the base of the middle phalanx and extends the PIP; the terminal tendon (conjoined lateral bands) extends the DIP.
    • The sagittal bands tether the EDC over the MCP joint and are the structure that extends the MCP by transmitting EDC pull to the proximal phalanx.
  • Sagittal band rupture ("boxer's knuckle"):
    • Traumatic subluxation/dislocation of the extensor tendon at the MCP joint.
    • Usually ULNAR subluxation of the tendon from a RADIAL sagittal band tear, most often the middle finger.
    • Presents with an inability to initiate MCP extension, which can be maintained once passively extended.
    • Acute (<2–3 weeks): a relative-motion extension splint or MCP extension splint for 4–6 weeks — a highly effective non-operative treatment.
    • Chronic: sagittal band reconstruction (a junctura or a slip of EDC/lumbrical) or direct repair if the tissue permits.
  • Mallet finger:
    • Terminal tendon disruption → DIP extensor lag.
    • Nearly all are treated with continuous DIP extension splinting for 6–8 weeks (then night splinting), including most bony mallets.
    • Operate for volar subluxation of the distal phalanx, or a fragment involving >30–50% of the articular surface with subluxation.
    • The commonest reason for failure is interrupting the splinting — the DIP must never be allowed to flex, even for a moment.
  • Swan-neck deformity = PIP HYPEREXTENSION + DIP FLEXION:
    • Mechanism: loss of the PIP volar restraint allows PIP hyperextension, which migrates the lateral bands DORSALLY, and the terminal tendon becomes slack → DIP droops.
    • Causes: a chronic mallet (DIP lag with proximal migration of the lateral bands); volar plate laxity at the PIP; intrinsic tightness; and FDS insufficiency (rupture or laceration).
    • In rheumatoid disease it is driven by MCP volar subluxation with intrinsic tightness.
    • Treatment depends on whether the PIP is supple: a figure-of-eight ("silver ring") splint or dermodesis/FDS tenodesis (Littler) for a supple joint; PIP fusion or arthroplasty for a fixed deformity. Correct the MCP first in rheumatoid disease.
  • Boutonnière deformity = PIP FLEXION + DIP HYPEREXTENSION:
    • Mechanism: central slip rupture with VOLAR migration of the lateral bands, which then act as PIP flexors and hyperextend the DIP.
    • Elson's test is the diagnostic manoeuvre: flex the PIP to 90° over the edge of a table and ask the patient to extend the PIP against resistance — a RIGID, extending DIP indicates a central slip rupture (the lateral bands are being recruited). A floppy DIP means the central slip is intact.
    • Acute treatment: PIP extension splinting, leaving the DIP FREE, for 6 weeks, with active DIP flexion exercises to draw the lateral bands back dorsally.
    • A volar PIP dislocation implies a central slip injury until proven otherwise — treat it as a boutonnière.
  • Oblique retinacular ligament (Landsmeer):
    • It runs from the flexor sheath at the proximal phalanx, passes VOLAR to the PIP axis and DORSAL to the DIP axis, and inserts on the terminal tendon.
    • Its function is to couple/coordinate DIP extension with PIP extension — extending the PIP tightens it and helps extend the DIP.
    • Tightness produces a DIP that is fixed in extension, tested with the Haines-Zancolli test: passive DIP flexion is limited with the PIP extended and improves with the PIP flexed.
  • Intrinsic versus extrinsic tightness — the Bunnell test:
    • INTRINSIC tightness: passive PIP flexion is LIMITED with the MCP held in EXTENSION and improves with the MCP FLEXED (flexing the MCP relaxes the intrinsics).
    • EXTRINSIC (extensor) tightness: the opposite — passive PIP flexion is LIMITED with the MCP FLEXED and improves with the MCP EXTENDED (extending the MCP relaxes the long extensors).
    • Mnemonic: the tight structure limits motion when it is stretched; work out which position stretches the intrinsics (MCP extended) versus the extrinsics (MCP flexed).
    • The active-motion version — "cannot extend the PIP with the MCP flexed but can with the MCP extended" — reflects the balance between the extrinsic extensor and the intrinsic system, and points to a central slip or intrinsic problem.
  • Vaughan-Jackson syndrome — the rheumatoid extensor rupture:
    • Attritional rupture of the extensor tendons over a prominent, dorsally subluxed distal ulna (caput ulnae) in rheumatoid arthritis.
    • It progresses ULNAR to RADIAL — the EDM and then EDC to the small finger rupture first (compartment 5, then compartment 4).
    • Treatment: address the bone first — Darrach resection or Sauvé-Kapandji ± dorsal synovectomy — plus tendon transfer (EIP) or side-to-side suturing to an adjacent intact tendon.
    • Direct end-to-end repair is not possible because of the length of attritional tendon.
    • The differential for a rheumatoid patient who cannot extend a finger: tendon rupture, MCP volar subluxation, sagittal band attenuation with extensor subluxation, and PIN palsy from elbow synovitis — test by passively extending the MCP and asking the patient to hold it (tenodesis and hold test).
  • Mannerfelt lesion — the flexor equivalent:
    • FPL rupture from attrition against a volar scaphoid/scaphotrapezial osteophyte at the volar radiocarpal joint in rheumatoid arthritis.
    • Treatment: excise the offending spur plus tendon graft, an FDS (ring) transfer, or thumb IP fusion.
  • Dorsal compartment syndromes — distinguish the two:
    • De Quervain tenosynovitis — the FIRST dorsal compartment (APL and EPB) at the radial styloid. Positive Finkelstein/Eichhoff test. A separate EPB subsheath is present in a large minority and is a cause of failed injection or failed release — it must be released.
    • Intersection syndrome — where the first compartment (APL/EPB) crosses OVER the second (ECRL/ECRB), producing pain and crepitus ~4–6 cm PROXIMAL to the wrist.
    • The six dorsal compartments: 1 APL/EPB; 2 ECRL/ECRB; 3 EPL (around Lister's tubercle); 4 EDC/EIP; 5 EDM; 6 ECU.
  • Extensor zones — the odd-numbered ones are over joints:
    • Zone I DIP (mallet), II middle phalanx, III PIP (central slip → boutonnière), IV proximal phalanx, V MCP (fight bite, sagittal band), VI metacarpal, VII wrist/retinaculum, VIII distal forearm.
    • Zone V is the fight-bite zone — assume joint penetration, never close primarily, and cover Eikenella corrodens.
    • Extensor repairs tolerate early relative-motion (yoke) splinting, which is now standard for zones IV–VII and allows earlier return to work.

Carpal tunnel syndrome & median nerve compression

Localising the lesion along the median nerve, the evidence for treatment, and the specific complications of release.

7 of 10 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • The carpal tunnel — contents and boundaries:
    • Ten structures: nine flexor tendons (4 FDS, 4 FDP, FPL) plus the median nerve.
    • Roof = transverse carpal ligament; floor and walls = the carpal bones; radial border = scaphoid tubercle and trapezium; ulnar border = hook of hamate and pisiform.
    • The recurrent motor branch is most often extraligamentous and recurrent (~50%), but may be subligamentous or, in ~20%, transligamentous — the anatomy that makes a blind release hazardous.
    • The palmar cutaneous branch arises ~5–6 cm proximal to the wrist crease and runs SUPERFICIAL to the transverse carpal ligament — it does not enter the tunnel.
  • Clinical diagnosis:
    • Night symptoms in the median distribution, relieved by shaking the hand (flick sign) — the most characteristic history.
    • Durkan's carpal compression test is the most sensitive and specific provocative test, then Phalen, then Tinel.
    • Thenar atrophy and weakness of palmar abduction indicate advanced disease.
    • Two-point discrimination is a LATE finding (it tests innervation density); Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (threshold) testing is more sensitive early.
    • Electrodiagnostics support but do not define the diagnosis — a false-negative rate of 10–15%; a normal study in a classic history does not exclude CTS.
  • Localising a proximal median lesion — the palmar cutaneous branch rule:
    • Because the palmar cutaneous branch bypasses the tunnel, it is SPARED in carpal tunnel syndrome.
    • Therefore numbness over the THENAR EMINENCE and central palm indicates a lesion PROXIMAL to the carpal tunnel.
    • Pronator syndrome also lacks prominent night symptoms, has a positive Tinel in the proximal forearm, and characteristically produces forearm aching worse with resisted pronation.
  • Compression sites in pronator syndrome — the four, proximal to distal:
    • Ligament of Struthers (from a supracondylar process to the medial epicondyle).
    • Lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis).
    • Between the two heads of pronator teres.
    • The proximal (fibrous) arch of FDS.
    • The carpal tunnel is NOT a site of pronator syndrome — the classic "all are compression sites except" answer.
    • Provocative tests localise the level: resisted pronation with the elbow extended (pronator teres), resisted elbow flexion with the forearm supinated (lacertus), resisted middle-finger PIP flexion (FDS arch).
  • Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome:
    • PURELY MOTOR — there is NO sensory loss, because the AIN carries no cutaneous sensory fibres (only wrist joint afferents).
    • Affected muscles: FPL, FDP to the index and middle fingers, and pronator quadratus.
    • The sign is inability to make an "OK" sign — the thumb IP and index DIP stay extended, producing a triangle instead of a circle.
    • Many cases are a neuritis (Parsonage-Turner variant) rather than true compression, so observation for 3–6 months is reasonable; the Gantzer muscle (accessory FPL head) is a described compressive cause.
  • Non-operative treatment:
    • Neutral wrist night splinting is first-line for mild-to-moderate disease; wrist flexion and extension both raise carpal tunnel pressure.
    • Corticosteroid injection gives reliable short-term relief and has predictive value — a good response predicts a good response to release.
    • Neither changes the natural history in severe disease. Thenar atrophy, constant numbness or denervation on EMG is an indication for release, not for splinting.
  • Endoscopic versus open release:
    • Endoscopic release gives a faster return to work and less scar/pillar pain in the first few weeks.
    • Outcomes are equivalent by 3–6 months — symptom relief and satisfaction do not differ.
    • The commonest complication of endoscopic release is TRANSIENT NEURAPRAXIA of the median or ulnar nerve or their branches.
    • The feared complications are incomplete release and irreversible nerve or vessel laceration; conversion to open is the correct response to a poor endoscopic view.
    • Open release avoids the palmar cutaneous branch by keeping the incision in line with the radial border of the ring finger, ulnar to the thenar crease.
  • Persistent versus recurrent symptoms after release — a distinction that gets tested:
    • PERSISTENT (never got better): the commonest cause is incomplete release of the distal transverse carpal ligament and distal antebrachial fascia. Also reconsider the diagnosis — cervical radiculopathy, proximal compression, polyneuropathy.
    • RECURRENT (better, then worse): perineural scarring/tethering, or new pathology. Revision with neurolysis and a vascularised flap (hypothenar fat pad, synovial flap) is the usual approach.
    • NEW pain in a different place: a tender Tinel over the RADIAL aspect of the distal wrist crease with NORMAL digital two-point discrimination = palmar cutaneous branch neuroma from an incision placed too radially.
    • Injury to the recurrent motor branch causes thenar weakness with no change in sensation.
  • Secondary causes worth screening for:
    • Bilateral CTS in an older patient with cardiac disease or heart failure should prompt consideration of transthyretin (ATTR) AMYLOIDOSIS — send the tenosynovium for Congo red staining (apple-green birefringence under polarised light) at the time of release.
    • Bilateral CTS often precedes the cardiac diagnosis by 5–10 years, so recognising it allows disease-modifying treatment (tafamidis) before cardiomyopathy is advanced. This is the highest-value screening opportunity in hand surgery.
    • Other secondary causes: diabetes, hypothyroidism, pregnancy, dialysis-related β2-microglobulin amyloid, rheumatoid tenosynovitis, acromegaly, gout, and a distal radius malunion.
    • Pregnancy-related CTS usually resolves post-partum — splint, do not operate.

Metacarpal & phalangeal fractures

Acceptable deformity by digit, what actually indicates surgery, and why stiffness — not malunion — is the enemy.

6 of 10 sittings2017, 2018, 2020, 2021 (x2), 2024S (x2), 2024F

  • Acceptable apex-dorsal angulation for METACARPAL NECK fractures — increases radial to ulnar:
    • Index ~10°.
    • Middle ~20°.
    • Ring ~30–40°.
    • Small finger ~50–60°.
    • The reason is CMC mobility: the fourth and especially the fifth CMC joints have 20–30° of flexion-extension, which compensates for the deformity; the index and middle CMC joints are essentially rigid.
    • For metacarpal SHAFT fractures the tolerances are roughly half these (10–20° in the ring/small, <10° in the index/middle).
  • Rotation is the one thing never accepted:
    • NO rotational deformity is acceptable in any digit.
    • 5° of malrotation produces ~1.5 cm of digital overlap at the fingertip.
    • Assess it with the fingers in composite flexion (all should point toward the scaphoid tubercle) and by comparing the plane of the nail plates, not on the radiograph.
    • A scissoring digit is a functional problem the patient will not tolerate.
  • Indications for open reduction of a metacarpal fracture:
    • Malrotation.
    • Multiple metacarpal fractures (loss of the buttressing effect of the neighbours and the intermetacarpal ligaments).
    • Open fracture or associated soft-tissue/tendon injury.
    • Angulation beyond the acceptable range for that digit.
    • Shortening >5 mm (pseudoclawing and extensor lag).
    • Displaced intra-articular fracture.
    • Angulation within the acceptable range in an isolated small-finger neck fracture is not an indication — the classic boxer's fracture is treated with buddy taping or an ulnar gutter splint and early motion.
  • The shortening numbers:
    • Each 2 mm of metacarpal shortening produces roughly 7° of extensor lag (the extrinsic extensor is relatively lengthened).
    • Each 2 mm also costs roughly 8% of grip strength.
    • In practice, up to 5 mm of shortening in the ring and small finger is well tolerated functionally, because the intrinsics compensate.
    • The combination usually presented — a few degrees of dorsal angulation with 2 mm of shortening in a border digit — is acceptable.
  • Phalangeal fractures — the dominant complication is STIFFNESS:
    • The commonest complication of non-operative treatment is loss of motion from adhesions, NOT malunion or nonunion.
    • The reason is anatomical: the extensor mechanism dorsally and the flexor sheath volarly are intimately adherent to the phalanx, so any callus or scar tethers them.
    • Early protected motion is the priority — immobilise for as short a time as the fracture allows, in the intrinsic-plus (safe) position: wrist 20–30° extension, MCP 70–90° flexion, IP joints straight.
    • The same logic drives the choice of fixation: pick the construct that permits the earliest motion, and prefer percutaneous K-wires or a lag screw over extensive plating, which adds soft-tissue dissection and adhesion.
    • Proximal phalanx fractures angulate APEX VOLAR (interossei flex the proximal fragment, central slip extends the distal fragment).
  • Tuft fractures:
    • Treat the nail bed and the soft tissue, not the bone.
    • Options: splinting for comfort, nail bed repair if there is a laceration, and trephination for a painful subungual haematoma.
    • Trephination alone is appropriate even for a large haematoma when the nail and its margins are intact.
    • Formal ORIF with a plate is NOT a treatment for a tuft fracture — the usual "all are potential methods except" answer.
    • Nonunion is common radiographically and almost always asymptomatic; persistent hypersensitivity is the real problem.
  • The "fight bite" (tooth strike over the MCP, extensor zone V):
    • Assume joint penetration and extensor tendon injury until proven otherwise — the tendon laceration is at the MCP in flexion but retracts proximally when the hand is examined in extension, so the skin wound and tendon injury are not aligned.
    • Requires formal irrigation and débridement of the MCP joint in theatre, extending the wound.
    • Antibiotics covering Eikenella corrodens — amoxicillin-clavulanate (or ampicillin-sulbactam IV); Eikenella is resistant to first-generation cephalosporins and clindamycin.
    • Radiographs for retained tooth fragments and for an osteochondral defect in the metacarpal head.
    • Never close primarily. Also address tetanus, hepatitis and HIV exposure.

PIP and MCP joint injuries & fracture-dislocations

Stability judged by the percentage of the volar base involved, the reconstructive ladder, and why a complex MCP dislocation must not be pulled on.

7 of 10 sittings2017, 2018, 2019 (x2), 2020 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Why the volar base of the middle phalanx determines stability:
    • The volar plate and the collateral ligaments attach to the volar base of the middle phalanx.
    • A fracture that takes that base removes the restraint to dorsal subluxation as well as the bony buttress.
    • So the whole management algorithm is driven by the percentage of the volar articular surface involved, assessed on a true lateral.
    • The "V" sign (dorsal subluxation with a divergent joint line) on the lateral means the joint is not concentric, regardless of the fragment size.
  • Dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation — the algorithm:
    • <30–40% of the volar base — STABLE. Extension-block splinting and early active motion.
    • 30–50% — TENUOUS. Extension-block pinning, or ORIF if there is a single large fragment.
    • >50% — UNSTABLE. Requires reconstruction of the volar buttress: hemi-hamate autograft arthroplasty, volar plate arthroplasty (Eaton), dynamic external fixation (a "pins and rubber bands" or Suzuki frame), or ORIF if the fragment is a single reconstructible piece.
    • The recurring stem — a dorsal PIP fracture-dislocation with ~50% of the volar lip of the middle phalanx involved → hemi-hamate arthroplasty. Dorsal-block splinting alone is inadequate at this degree of instability.
  • Hemi-hamate arthroplasty — why the hamate:
    • The dorsal-distal articular surface of the hamate (between the ridge separating its fourth and fifth metacarpal facets) reproduces the concave, ridged articular surface of the middle phalanx base.
    • It restores both the articular surface and the volar buttress, and allows early motion.
    • Results give reliable stability with roughly 70–80° of PIP motion; some radiographic degeneration is common but usually asymptomatic.
    • Volar plate arthroplasty is the older alternative, better suited to comminution without a reconstructible surface.
  • Volar PIP dislocation:
    • It implies a CENTRAL SLIP injury until proven otherwise.
    • Treat with PIP extension splinting for 6 weeks, leaving the DIP FREE — or the patient develops a boutonnière.
    • An irreducible volar dislocation is blocked by the lateral band buttonholed around the condyle, or by an interposed central slip.
    • Rotatory volar dislocations are classically irreducible by closed means and need open reduction; the radiographic clue is a true lateral of one phalanx with an oblique view of the other.
  • Irreducible (complex) dorsal MCP dislocation:
    • Usually the index finger (and the thumb), because they are border digits.
    • The block to reduction is the interposed VOLAR PLATE, avulsed from its proximal attachment and flipped dorsally into the joint.
    • The metacarpal head is buttonholed between the lumbrical (radially) and the flexor tendons (ulnarly), with the natatory and superficial transverse metacarpal ligaments tightening around the neck as a noose.
    • Radiographic clue: a SESAMOID within the joint space (pathognomonic, since the sesamoid lies within the volar plate); the digit also sits in slight extension with skin puckering over the volar MCP, and the joint is not grossly deformed — a "simple-looking" dislocation.
  • Reduction technique — the manoeuvre that gets tested:
    • Do NOT apply longitudinal traction with hyperextension. That converts a simple dislocation into a complex one by drawing the volar plate into the joint.
    • Instead: flex the wrist (to relax the extrinsic flexors), keep the MCP flexed, and apply a volar and distally directed pressure on the base of the proximal phalanx to slide it over the metacarpal head.
    • If it does not reduce, open reduction is required — through a volar approach (direct access to the volar plate, but the digital nerves are draped superficially over the prominent metacarpal head and are at risk) or a dorsal approach (safer for the nerves, and the volar plate can be split longitudinally and reduced).
  • Collateral ligament and volar plate injuries at the PIP:
    • The proper collateral ligament is tight in flexion; the accessory collateral and volar plate are tight in extension.
    • An isolated volar plate avulsion of the middle phalanx base with <25% involvement and a congruent joint is stable — buddy tape and immediate motion.
    • The commonest sequel of any PIP injury is a fusiform swollen, stiff joint with a flexion contracture — warn the patient it takes 6–12 months to settle.
    • Prolonged immobilisation is the enemy at both the PIP and MCP; if immobilisation is unavoidable, use the safe position.

Scapholunate instability, SLAC wrist & carpal collapse

The ligament anatomy, the DISI/VISI distinction, the predictable arthritic pattern, and choosing between the salvage operations.

5 of 10 sittings2018, 2019 (x4), 2020 (x3), 2023, 2025

  • The ligament anatomy — which part matters:
    • The scapholunate interosseous ligament is a C-shaped structure with dorsal, proximal (membranous) and volar components.
    • The DORSAL component is the strongest and the most important stabiliser (roughly 300 N) — the volar component is weaker and the proximal membranous portion is essentially non-structural and degenerates with age.
    • Secondary stabilisers: the scaphotrapezial-trapezoid (STT) ligament, the radioscaphocapitate, the long radiolunate, and the dorsal intercarpal (DIC) ligament.
    • Isolated SLIL section does not produce a static deformity — static DISI requires failure of the secondary stabilisers too. That is the reason for the dynamic-to-static progression.
  • Radiographic signs of scapholunate dissociation:
    • SL gap >3 mm on the PA view — the "Terry Thomas" (or David Letterman) sign. Compare with the contralateral wrist.
    • Cortical ring sign — the flexed scaphoid seen end-on, with a scaphoid ring-to-proximal-pole distance <7 mm.
    • Scapholunate angle >70° on the lateral (normal 30–60°).
    • Capitolunate angle >15° and loss of collinearity of the radius–lunate–capitate axis.
    • Stress views (clenched-fist PA, or PA in ulnar deviation) unmask a dynamic lesion that is invisible on static films.
  • DISI versus VISI — the distinction that recurs:
    • DISI (dorsal intercalated segment instability): the lunate EXTENDS (dorsiflexes), SL angle >70°.
    • DISI is caused by scapholunate dissociation and by scaphoid nonunion — in both cases the lunate loses its flexion tether (the scaphoid) and follows the triquetrum into extension.
    • VISI (volar intercalated segment instability): the lunate FLEXES, SL angle <30°.
    • The commonest cause of VISI is LUNOTRIQUETRAL ligament injury / ulnar-sided carpal instability — the lunate loses its extension tether (the triquetrum) and follows the scaphoid into flexion.
    • Mnemonic for the mechanism: the lunate goes with whichever neighbour it is still attached to.
  • The progression to SLAC:
    • Dynamic instability (abnormal only on stress views) → static instability (abnormal on plain films) → SLAC (scapholunate advanced collapse).
    • Stage I — arthritis at the radial styloid–scaphoid articulation.
    • Stage II — the entire radioscaphoid joint.
    • Stage III — capitolunate/midcarpal arthritis, with proximal migration of the capitate.
    • Stage IV — pancarpal, including the radiolunate joint.
    • The RADIOLUNATE joint is characteristically SPARED until very late, because the lunate facet is congruent and spherical and the lunate remains well seated. This is the anatomical fact that makes proximal row carpectomy and four-corner fusion possible.
    • SNAC (scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse) follows an identical pattern, beginning at the radial styloid–distal scaphoid fragment.
  • Treatment by stage:
    • Acute, repairable ligament (<4–6 weeks): direct repair with suture anchors ± dorsal capsulodesis and temporary K-wire fixation.
    • Chronic, reducible deformity without arthritis: ligament reconstruction — three-ligament tenodesis (Garcia-Elias), SLIC screw, RASL, or a bone–ligament–bone graft. Results are inconsistent and deteriorate over time.
    • Chronic, irreducible without arthritis: consider partial (STT or scaphocapitate) fusion.
    • SLAC I: radial styloidectomy ± denervation (posterior interosseous nerve neurectomy).
    • SLAC/SNAC II–III: proximal row carpectomy (PRC) or scaphoid excision with four-corner fusion (4CF).
    • SLAC IV: total wrist fusion (or arthroplasty in a low-demand patient).
    • Wrist denervation (PIN and AIN neurectomy) is a motion-preserving option for pain at any stage, and a test injection predicts the response.
  • PRC versus four-corner fusion — the comparison that gets asked:
    • PRC requires an INTACT capitate head and lunate fossa — it converts the wrist to a capitate-on-lunate-fossa articulation.
    • Therefore PRC is CONTRAINDICATED when the capitolunate joint is arthritic (SLAC/SNAC III), unless combined with a capsular interposition or resurfacing.
    • PRC preserves more motion, is technically simpler, has no nonunion risk and a faster recovery.
    • 4CF preserves more grip strength and carpal height, and can be used when the capitate head is worn.
    • 4CF risks nonunion (historically ~5–10%, lower with modern plates/screws) and dorsal impingement from an over-dorsiflexed lunate.
    • In patients under 35 with high demands, 4CF is often favoured — though long-term series show the two converge and this remains debated.
    • Both accept a ~60–70% loss of motion and ~80% of contralateral grip; both are pain-relieving, motion-sacrificing operations.
  • Related radiographic signs on the same films:
    • The "piece of pie" sign — a triangular lunate on the PA view — and a disrupted Gilula arc with an overlapping lunate and capitate on the lateral indicate a perilunate or lunate dislocation, a missed emergency, not a chronic SLAC.
    • Carpal height ratio (carpal height ÷ third metacarpal length, normal ~0.54) falls with carpal collapse.
    • An ulnar translocation of the carpus points to rheumatoid disease or a radiocarpal ligament injury rather than SLAC.

Dupuytren disease

Cord anatomy and why the nerve is at risk, the diathesis that predicts recurrence, and the treatment trade-off between recovery speed and durability.

6 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • Pathology:
    • A fibroproliferative disorder of the palmar and digital fascia, driven by MYOFIBROBLASTS (the contractile cell, positive for α-smooth muscle actin).
    • The matrix shifts from type I to an increased proportion of TYPE III collagen.
    • Luck's three histologic stages: proliferative (nodule, high myofibroblast count) → involutional (cord formation, aligned cells) → residual (acellular, collagen-rich cord).
    • Nodules are the active, proliferative lesion; cords are the mature, contractile one. Nodules can be tender; cords cause contracture.
    • Northern European ancestry, autosomal dominant with variable penetrance, male predominance, associations with diabetes, epilepsy/anticonvulsants, alcohol and smoking. Wnt signalling pathway variants are implicated.
  • Cords are named for the normal structures they replace:
    • Pretendinous cord — from the pretendinous band; causes the MCP contracture.
    • Central cord — a distal continuation of the pretendinous cord; causes PIP contracture; does not displace the neurovascular bundle.
    • Lateral cord — from the lateral digital sheet; contributes to PIP contracture and can displace the bundle.
    • Spiral cord — the dangerous one (see below).
    • Natatory cord — from the natatory ligament; limits finger ABDUCTION (web-space contracture).
    • Retrovascular cord (Thomine) — dorsal to the neurovascular bundle; a cause of residual/recurrent DIP contracture.
    • Grayson's ligament (volar to the bundle) is involved; Cleland's ligament (dorsal to the bundle) is NOT involved — a classic exam fact.
  • The spiral cord — why the digital nerve gets cut:
    • It is formed by the pretendinous band, spiral band, lateral digital sheet and Grayson's ligament.
    • As it contracts, it displaces the neurovascular bundle CENTRALLY (toward the midline), SUPERFICIALLY and PROXIMALLY.
    • So the nerve ends up directly under the skin in the midline at the level of the PIP, exactly where the surgeon expects to find cord.
    • The clinical warning sign is a PIP contracture with a soft, fluctuant swelling at the base of the digit (the displaced bundle plus fat).
    • Identify the neurovascular bundle proximally in the palm, where it is normal, and trace it distally — never start the dissection at the PIP.
  • Dupuytren diathesis — predicts aggressive disease and recurrence:
    • Ectopic (extra-palmar) disease — Ledderhose (plantar fibromatosis), Peyronie disease, Garrod knuckle pads.
    • Bilateral disease.
    • Positive family history.
    • Early age of onset (<50).
    • Male sex, and radial-sided (thumb/index) involvement.
    • Ectopic disease and bilaterality are the strongest indicators — the usual "most suggestive of aggressive disease" answer. The Abe/Hindocha scoring systems formalise this.
  • Indications for treatment:
    • MCP contracture ≥30°.
    • ANY fixed PIP contracture — the PIP tolerates contracture far less well and recovers less completely.
    • A positive tabletop (Hueston) test — the patient cannot lay the palm flat on a table — with functional impairment.
    • A nodule alone, without contracture, is NOT an indication. Nor is the presence of a cord.
    • MCP contractures correct better and more durably than PIP contractures, because the PIP develops secondary volar plate, check-rein ligament and collateral contracture, and the joint itself becomes stiff.
  • Treatment options — a trade-off between recovery and recurrence:
    • Collagenase clostridium histolyticum injection and percutaneous needle aponeurotomy — minimally invasive, fast recovery, higher recurrence. Best for an isolated, palpable, well-defined MCP cord. (Collagenase has been withdrawn from some markets, including Canada.)
    • Open limited/segmental fasciectomy — the workhorse; lower recurrence, longer recovery.
    • Dermofasciectomy with a full-thickness skin graft — the lowest recurrence; reserved for recurrent disease and diathesis-positive patients, because the involved skin is excised with the fascia.
    • Adjuncts for a fixed PIP: check-rein release, and occasionally PIP arthrodesis or amputation for a severe recurrent small-finger contracture.
    • There is no role for steroid injection into a cord, though it can settle a painful nodule; radiotherapy is used in some centres for early nodular disease.
  • Complications of open fasciectomy:
    • The commonest complication is a WOUND-HEALING problem — haematoma and skin-flap necrosis (particularly at the apices of Bruner flaps).
    • Digital nerve injury (~2–5%) is the most feared, and is highest with a spiral cord and in revision surgery.
    • Digital artery injury — and correcting a long-standing severe PIP contracture can cause vascular insufficiency of the digit; if the finger blanches, flex it back and wait.
    • Complex regional pain syndrome, flare reaction (more common in women) and stiffness.
    • Recurrence is the rule rather than the exception with long follow-up — counsel the patient that this is disease control, not cure.

Flexor tendon injury, pulleys & repair

Pulley biomechanics and modern venting, what makes a repair strong enough for early motion, and the named tensioning errors.

5 of 10 sittings2018, 2019 (x3), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2025

  • The pulley system:
    • Five annular pulleys (A1–A5) and three cruciate pulleys (C1–C3), in the order A1-A2-C1-A3-C2-A4-C3-A5.
    • Odd-numbered annular pulleys arise from the volar plates (A1 at the MCP, A3 at the PIP, A5 at the DIP); even-numbered arise from bone (A2 from the proximal phalanx, A4 from the middle phalanx).
    • A2 and A4 are the most biomechanically important — they are the ones that keep the tendon against the bone through the arc of flexion.
    • The thumb has A1, oblique and A2 pulleys; the OBLIQUE pulley is the critical one in the thumb.
  • What pulley loss actually does:
    • Loss causes BOWSTRINGING — the tendon lifts away from the bone.
    • That INCREASES the moment arm but reduces the efficiency of excursion: more tendon travel is needed for the same arc, so the patient loses the terminal flexion arc and grip strength, and the work of flexion rises.
    • The clinical result is a finger that cannot fully make a fist, with a visible/palpable tendon lifting off in the palm or digit.
  • Venting — the modern position:
    • Venting (partial release) of A4, and of up to the DISTAL HALF of A2, is acceptable and allows the repair to pass and glide without a significant biomechanical penalty.
    • Complete A2 release causes measurable bowstringing, increased work of flexion, loss of the flexion arc and reduced PIP/DIP motion — avoid it.
    • The practical rule: release only as much as is needed for the repair to glide, and prefer venting a pulley to leaving a repair that catches (a catching repair ruptures or scars).
    • Wide-awake (WALANT) surgery, developed largely in Canada, allows the surgeon to test active glide intra-operatively and correct a gap or catch before closing — a major practical advance.
  • Repair strength:
    • Strength is proportional to the number of CORE STRANDS crossing the repair; a 4- or 6-strand core repair with an epitendinous suture permits early ACTIVE motion protocols. A 2-strand repair only tolerates passive protocols.
    • The epitendinous (peripheral) suture adds ~20–50% strength and tidies the repair for gliding.
    • Aim for ~1 cm of purchase from the cut end, and place locking rather than grasping loops.
    • A gap >3 mm predicts rupture and adhesion — gapping, not frank rupture, is the usual mode of clinical failure.
    • The repair is weakest at days 5–21 as the tendon softens before remodelling — the highest-risk window.
  • Two named tensioning errors:
    • QUADRIGA — from over-shortening (over-tightening) an FDP, typically by >1 cm at repair or by suturing the FDP stump to the distal phalanx after amputation. Because the FDP tendons share a common muscle belly, the shortened tendon reaches full excursion first and limits the excursion of the ADJACENT digits → weak grip and incomplete flexion of the neighbouring fingers.
    • LUMBRICAL-PLUS finger — from over-LENGTHENING the FDP (a graft that is too long, or a distal FDP amputation/avulsion). The lumbrical originates from the FDP, so pulling on a slack FDP tensions the lumbrical and paradoxically EXTENDS the PIP when the patient tries to flex. Treated by lumbrical release.
  • Jersey finger (FDP avulsion):
    • The RING finger is by far the commonest (it is the most prominent in grip and its FDP insertion is weakest).
    • Leddy-Packer classification by the level of retraction, which is determined by the vincula:
    • Type I — retracts to the PALM; both vincula are ruptured, so the tendon is avascular. Must be repaired within 7–10 days.
    • Type II — retracts to the PIP; the long vinculum is intact. Repair within a few weeks.
    • Type III — a large bony fragment caught at the A4 pulley; least retraction, can be repaired later.
    • Type IV — a bony fragment plus tendon avulsion off the fragment (a "double" injury) — must be recognised or the tendon is left unrepaired.
    • Late presentation with a supple finger: DIP arthrodesis or a staged tendon graft; do not attempt a delayed primary repair of a type I.
  • Suture anchor technique in the distal phalanx:
    • Pull-out strength is greatest when the anchor is angled AWAY from the direction of pull — the "deadman angle", classically ~45° to the cortex.
    • Insert at the base of the distal phalanx, in good subchondral bone, avoiding the germinal matrix of the nail (the sterile matrix begins immediately distal).
    • The alternative is a pull-out suture over a dorsal button, which has a higher rate of nail deformity and infection.
  • Zones and rehabilitation:
    • Zone I — distal to the FDS insertion (FDP only). Zone II ("no-man's land") — from the A1 pulley to the FDS insertion; both tendons in a tight sheath, the greatest risk of adhesion. Zone III — palm (lumbrical origin). Zone IV — carpal tunnel. Zone V — proximal to the carpal tunnel ("spaghetti wrist").
    • Early controlled motion is essential — Duran (passive), Kleinert (dynamic extension with rubber-band flexion), or a modern early active motion protocol with a strong repair.
    • Immobilisation reliably produces adhesions; tenolysis is not considered before 3–6 months, once the wound is supple and passive motion exceeds active.

Perilunate dislocation & lesser arc injury

The Mayfield sequence, which ligaments define a lesser arc injury, the radiographic signs that get missed, and why this is an urgent operation.

4 of 10 sittings2021, 2023, 2024F, 2025

  • Mayfield stages of progressive perilunar instability — the injury propagates radial to ulnar around the lunate:
    • Stage I — scapholunate dissociation (the scapholunate ligament fails).
    • Stage II — capitolunate dislocation (a perilunate dislocation; the capitate displaces dorsally, the lunate stays put).
    • Stage III — lunotriquetral disruption, so the whole carpus is now separated from the lunate.
    • Stage IV — lunate dislocation — the lunate is extruded VOLARLY into the carpal tunnel, rotating on the intact volar short radiolunate ligament: the "spilled teacup" sign.
    • Mechanism is hyperextension, ulnar deviation and intercarpal supination — a high-energy fall or motor-vehicle crash.
  • Lesser arc versus greater arc — the defining distinction:
    • LESSER ARC injuries are PURELY LIGAMENTOUS and travel around the lunate: scapholunate, capitolunate / radioscaphocapitate, lunotriquetral, and the dorsal radiocarpal (radiotriquetral) ligament.
    • GREATER ARC injuries pass THROUGH BONE: trans-scaphoid, trans-capitate, trans-triquetral, trans-styloid perilunate fracture-dislocations, in any combination.
    • A trans-scaphoid perilunate dislocation is the commonest greater-arc pattern and the commonest perilunate injury overall (~60%).
    • The exam typically asks which ligament is NOT injured in a lesser arc injury — the answer is usually the short radiolunate / volar radiolunate (which stays attached and is the lunate's remaining tether in stage IV), or the radioscaphoid/ulnolunate.
  • Why it is missed — up to 25% on first presentation:
    • The wrist is swollen and painful after high-energy trauma, and attention is on other injuries.
    • The PA view shows a triangular "piece of pie" lunate, loss of the normal quadrilateral lunate shape, carpal crowding/overlap, and disruption of Gilula's arcs.
    • Gilula's three arcs: arc I along the proximal convexity of the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum; arc II along their distal concavity; arc III along the proximal capitate and hamate. Any step or break in a smooth arc means a carpal injury.
    • The LATERAL view is the key film: loss of collinearity of the radius–lunate–capitate axis. In a perilunate dislocation the capitate sits dorsal to a normally aligned lunate; in a lunate dislocation the lunate is tipped volarly and the capitate has settled onto the radius.
    • Always examine and document median nerve function — acute carpal tunnel syndrome is common with a volar lunate dislocation.
  • Treatment:
    • Urgent closed reduction (Tavernier manoeuvre: traction, extension, then flexion over a volar-directed thumb on the lunate) to decompress the median nerve and reduce the soft-tissue insult.
    • Definitive treatment is OPEN reduction and ligament repair with K-wire or screw fixation — usually through a combined dorsal and volar approach. The dorsal approach allows reduction and repair of the scapholunate and lunotriquetral ligaments; the volar approach allows repair of the space of Poirier/volar capsular rent and carpal tunnel release.
    • Fix any scaphoid fracture with a screw in a trans-scaphoid pattern.
    • Carpal tunnel release if there is acute median neuropathy (and it is done routinely with a volar approach).
    • Closed treatment alone gives poor results — the ligaments do not heal and the carpus redisplaces.
    • K-wires are typically retained for 8–10 weeks.
  • Outcomes and late presentation:
    • Even with prompt anatomic treatment, post-traumatic arthritis and stiffness are common — expect roughly 60–70% of motion and grip.
    • Delayed presentation (>6–8 weeks) is often better served by salvage — proximal row carpectomy or wrist fusion — than by attempted late reduction, because the cartilage and ligaments have deteriorated.
    • Complications include chronic median neuropathy, scaphoid nonunion, AVN of the lunate or proximal scaphoid, and progressive carpal collapse.
    • Counsel the patient at the outset that this is a career- or hobby-limiting injury in many cases.

Scaphoid fracture & nonunion

Retrograde vascularity and what it dictates, the cast-versus-screw evidence, and correcting the humpback deformity.

7 of 10 sittings2017 (x4), 2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2020, 2022, 2024S, 2025

  • Blood supply — the anatomical fact behind every scaphoid question:
    • The supply is RETROGRADE, from the dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery entering through the dorsal ridge.
    • It supplies ~70–80% of the scaphoid, INCLUDING the ENTIRE PROXIMAL POLE.
    • A smaller volar branch (superficial palmar arch) supplies the distal 20–30% via the tubercle.
    • Therefore the more PROXIMAL the fracture, the higher the rate of nonunion and AVN — proximal pole nonunion rates approach 30–40%, waist ~10%, tubercle almost none.
    • The scaphoid is also ~80% covered by articular cartilage, leaving little surface for vessels to enter.
  • Diagnosis of an occult fracture:
    • Radiographs may be normal for up to 2 weeks. Include a scaphoid (ulnar-deviated PA) view.
    • MRI is the most sensitive early test and is the most cost-effective strategy in the classic "snuffbox tenderness with normal radiographs" scenario — it avoids weeks of unnecessary immobilisation in the majority who have no fracture.
    • CT along the scaphoid axis (oblique sagittal, along the long axis) is the test that defines displacement, angulation and union — not MRI.
    • Historically: immobilise and repeat films at 2 weeks. That is still acceptable but is being replaced by early MRI where available.
  • Non-displaced waist fracture — the evidence:
    • Union rates ~90–95% with cast immobilisation.
    • Multiple RCTs, including SWIFFT, show no difference between a short-arm thumb spica and a short-arm cast that excludes the thumb, and no clear benefit from a long-arm cast.
    • SWIFFT also found no functional benefit of early screw fixation over cast treatment at 12 months, with more complications in the surgical group — but a higher nonunion rate in the cast group requiring later surgery.
    • Percutaneous screw fixation gives a faster return to work and sport with equivalent union rates — the reasonable choice for an athlete, a manual worker, or a patient who cannot tolerate 8–12 weeks in a cast.
    • Screw placement should be central in both planes and along the central axis; a headless compression screw, with the length chosen so both threads engage.
  • Indications for ORIF:
    • Displacement >1 mm (step or gap).
    • Lateral intrascaphoid angle >35° (normal ~30–35°) or an intrascaphoid angle indicating a humpback.
    • Height-to-length ratio >0.65.
    • Associated perilunate injury or DISI (radiolunate angle >15°).
    • Proximal pole fracture — treat surgically, usually through a dorsal approach.
    • Comminution, or a delayed presentation.
  • The humpback deformity and DISI:
    • In a waist nonunion the distal pole FLEXES and the proximal pole EXTENDS, producing a volar-apex "humpback" and shortening of the scaphoid.
    • Because the scaphoid normally tethers the proximal row into flexion, its failure lets the lunate EXTEND → DISI, and the carpus collapses.
    • Correction requires a STRUCTURAL (corticocancellous, volar wedge) bone graft with screw fixation to restore scaphoid length and volar height and thereby correct the DISI — via a volar approach.
    • A non-structural cancellous graft alone will NOT correct the deformity — the key exam point. Cancellous graft (with a dorsal approach) is appropriate only for a nonunion without collapse.
  • Proximal pole nonunion with AVN:
    • Suspect it with sclerosis of the proximal pole on radiographs, loss of signal on T1 MRI that fails to enhance with gadolinium, and absent punctate bleeding at surgery (the intra-operative gold standard).
    • Requires a VASCULARISED bone graft: the 1,2-intercompartmental supraretinacular artery (1,2-ICSRA) pedicled graft from the dorsal distal radius, or a free medial femoral condyle graft (better union rates for the avascular proximal pole, especially after a failed 1,2-ICSRA).
    • A pedicled volar graft based on the volar carpal artery is the alternative for a volar humpback with AVN.
    • Untreated nonunion progresses to SNAC wrist in a pattern analogous to SLAC: radial styloid → radioscaphoid → capitolunate → pancarpal, with the radiolunate joint spared until late.
  • When to stop trying to unite it:
    • Once there is established radioscaphoid arthritis (SNAC II or beyond), treat with SALVAGE — proximal row carpectomy or scaphoid excision with four-corner fusion — rather than attempting union.
    • SNAC I (radial styloid only) can still be treated with union plus a radial styloidectomy.
    • PRC requires an intact capitate head, which is lost in SNAC III.
    • A completely asymptomatic long-standing nonunion in an elderly patient can reasonably be left alone.

Kienböck disease

Lunate vascular anatomy, Lichtman staging, and choosing the operation from the stage and the ulnar variance.

5 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x2), 2019, 2021 (x2), 2022

  • What it is:
    • Osteonecrosis of the lunate, typically in men aged 20–40, often the dominant hand and often in manual workers.
    • The aetiology is multifactorial: vascular (a tenuous supply), mechanical (repetitive loading), and anatomical (ulnar variance, lunate morphology).
    • Presents with insidious dorsal central wrist pain, swelling, loss of grip and a restricted arc of motion — the classic "middle-aged manual worker with a stiff, painful wrist and no clear injury".
  • Vascular anatomy — the reason it happens:
    • The lunate is supplied by volar and dorsal branches arising from the radial artery, the anterior interosseous artery and the ulnar recurrent arteries, entering through the volar and dorsal poles.
    • The articular cartilage covers ~80% of the lunate, leaving only the volar and dorsal non-articular poles available for vessels to enter — the anatomical basis of the "all of these contribute to lunate vascularity EXCEPT" question, where the cartilage-covered surfaces are the answer.
    • Gelberman described three intraosseous patterns: Y, I and X.
    • Roughly 20% of lunates have a SINGLE (usually volar) nutrient vessel with no intraosseous anastomosis — these are the vulnerable ones.
    • The volar supply is the dominant one in most lunates, which is why volar dissection must be respectful.
  • Lichtman staging:
    • Stage I — normal radiographs; MRI shows diffuse oedema/loss of T1 signal. The diagnosis is made on MRI.
    • Stage II — sclerosis of the lunate WITHOUT collapse; normal lunate height and carpal alignment.
    • Stage IIIA — lunate COLLAPSE with NORMAL carpal alignment.
    • Stage IIIB — collapse PLUS fixed scaphoid flexion (cortical ring sign) and carpal collapse; radioscaphoid angle >60°, with proximal capitate migration.
    • Stage IIIC — coronal fracture/fragmentation of the lunate (added later).
    • Stage IV — pancarpal (radiocarpal and/or midcarpal) arthritis.
    • The IIIA-to-IIIB transition is the critical decision point, because carpal alignment determines whether a joint-levelling procedure can still work.
  • Ulnar variance:
    • NEGATIVE ulnar variance is associated with Kienböck — a shorter ulna transmits more load through the radiolunate joint and increases radiolunate shear.
    • The association is real but not causal in every case, and Kienböck occurs with neutral and positive variance too.
    • Measure it on a neutral-rotation PA with the shoulder abducted 90° and the elbow flexed 90° — forearm rotation alters apparent variance.
    • The variance determines the operation, which is why it must be measured properly.
  • Treatment for early disease (I–II and IIIA):
    • Negative ulnar variance → a joint-levelling RADIAL SHORTENING OSTEOTOMY — the most reliable and best-supported procedure; it offloads the lunate and may also produce a regional hyperaemic response.
    • Neutral or positive variance → CAPITATE SHORTENING osteotomy (with or without capitohamate fusion), a radial wedge/core decompression, or a vascularised bone graft.
    • The commonest vascularised graft is the 4+5 extracompartmental artery (4+5 ECA) pedicle from the dorsal distal radius; the 2,3-ICSRA is the alternative.
    • Immobilisation/observation remains appropriate for stage I, and is the mainstay in children and adolescents, where the natural history is far more favourable and spontaneous revascularisation occurs.
    • The evidence base is weak throughout — there is no randomised comparison of the major procedures.
  • Treatment for late disease:
    • Stage IIIB → proximal row carpectomy, or a limited intercarpal fusion (scaphocapitate or STT) to unload the lunate and correct the scaphoid flexion.
    • Stage IV → SALVAGE: PRC (only if the capitate head and lunate fossa are intact), total wrist fusion, or wrist arthroplasty in a low-demand patient.
    • Wrist denervation (PIN/AIN neurectomy) is a motion-preserving option for pain at any stage.
    • Lunate excision ALONE causes carpal collapse and is not recommended without a stabilising procedure (a fusion or a tendon-ball/spacer interposition).
  • Differential and pitfalls:
    • Ulnar impaction syndrome also produces central wrist pain with lunate signal change — but it has POSITIVE ulnar variance, the changes are on the proximal-ULNAR corner of the lunate, and there is often a TFCC tear and LT ligament attenuation. Treated with an ulnar shortening osteotomy or wafer procedure — the mirror image of Kienböck.
    • Also consider an occult fracture, intraosseous ganglion, and radiographic "ulnar-negative" wrist without disease.
    • Bilateral lunate signal change should prompt consideration of a systemic cause (steroids, lupus, sickle cell).

Rheumatoid hand & wrist

Why the wrist drives the hand deformity, the named attritional ruptures, and the order of reconstruction.

6 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x2), 2019, 2020, 2024S, 2025

  • The primary lesion:
    • Proliferative synovitis (pannus) that destroys capsule, ligament, tendon and finally cartilage and bone.
    • Driven by TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6, with RANKL-mediated periarticular erosion at the bare areas.
    • Tendon rupture in rheumatoid disease is usually ATTRITIONAL (over bone) or INVASIVE (by pannus) — not traumatic.
  • The central concept: the WRIST drives the HAND (zig-zag collapse):
    • Synovitis at the wrist causes radial deviation and supination of the carpus, with ulnar and volar translocation.
    • Radial deviation of the carpus swings the metacarpals radially.
    • To keep the fingers pointing forward, the digits compensate with ULNAR deviation at the MCP joints — ulnar drift.
    • This "zig-zag" (radial wrist, ulnar fingers) collapse is why the wrist must be corrected before or with the MCP joints — reconstructing the MCPs over an uncorrected radially deviated wrist guarantees recurrence.
  • Why the fingers drift ulnarly — the multifactorial answer:
    • Radial deviation of the metacarpals at the wrist (the primary driver).
    • Attenuation of the RADIAL sagittal bands, allowing ulnar and volar subluxation of the extensor tendons off the MCP heads, so the EDC becomes an ulnar deviator and MCP flexor.
    • The normal ulnar-directed pull of the flexor tendons as they exit the A1 pulleys obliquely.
    • Intrinsic (particularly ulnar intrinsic) tightness and contracture.
    • Volar plate and radial collateral ligament attenuation, with volar subluxation of the proximal phalanx.
    • The asymmetry of the metacarpal heads and the pull of the abductor digiti minimi on the small finger.
    • The answer is NOT "intrinsic tightness alone" — that is the classic incomplete distractor.
  • Caput ulnae syndrome:
    • Dorsal subluxation of the distal ulna with supination of the carpus away from it.
    • Produces a painful DRUJ, a dorsally prominent ulnar head, loss of forearm rotation, and the "piano key" sign.
    • The prominent, roughened ulnar head then causes attritional extensor tendon rupture.
  • Vaughan-Jackson syndrome:
    • Attritional extensor rupture over the caput ulnae, progressing ULNAR to RADIAL.
    • EDM and then EDC to the small finger go first, then ring, then middle.
    • Treatment must address the bone first — Darrach resection or Sauvé-Kapandji, plus dorsal tenosynovectomy — then side-to-side transfer to an adjacent intact tendon, or an EIP transfer.
    • Direct end-to-end repair is not possible because of the length of attritional tendon.
    • Before attributing a lost finger extension to rupture, exclude MCP volar subluxation, extensor subluxation from sagittal band attenuation (passively extend the MCP — if the patient can then hold it, the tendon is intact) and PIN palsy from elbow synovitis.
  • Mannerfelt lesion:
    • The flexor equivalent: FPL rupture from attrition against a volar scaphoid (or scaphotrapezial) osteophyte at the volar radiocarpal joint.
    • Treatment: excise the offending spur, plus an intercalary tendon graft, an FDS (ring) transfer, or thumb IP fusion.
    • The same volar synovitis causes carpal tunnel syndrome, which is common and often the presenting complaint.
  • The commonest carpal deformity:
    • Radial deviation of the carpus with carpal SUPINATION, and VOLAR and ULNAR TRANSLOCATION.
    • Accompanied by loss of carpal height, scapholunate dissociation and scaphoid flexion.
    • Ulnar translocation (the carpus slides ulnarly off the lunate facet) is characteristic of rheumatoid disease and is what a radiolunate fusion is designed to arrest.
  • Wrist surgery — matched to the stage:
    • Early, pain from synovitis with preserved joint: tenosynovectomy and synovectomy, with DRUJ reconstruction (Darrach in a low-demand patient, Sauvé-Kapandji in a younger or higher-demand patient because it preserves the ulnar buttress and prevents further ulnar translocation).
    • Radiocarpal destruction with ULNAR TRANSLOCATION but a preserved midcarpal joint: radiolunate (Chamay) partial fusion, which arrests translocation while keeping midcarpal motion.
    • Pancarpal destruction: TOTAL WRIST ARTHRODESIS — durable, reliably pain-relieving and the workhorse in a heavily deformed rheumatoid wrist. Position in neutral to slight extension (some fuse in slight flexion in a low-demand patient to aid perineal care).
    • Bilateral wrist fusion must be considered carefully — if both wrists are fused, position at least one to permit perineal care and personal hygiene; many surgeons will fuse one and arthroplasty or leave the other.
    • Total wrist arthroplasty is an option in a low-demand patient with good bone stock who needs motion (for example, one who already has an ipsilateral elbow or shoulder problem), but survivorship is inferior to fusion.
  • Order of reconstruction in a patient needing several operations:
    • Lower limb before upper limb if the patient needs crutches or a walker.
    • Proximal before distal in the upper limb: wrist before MCP before PIP.
    • Pain and function before appearance — ulnar drift alone is not an indication.
    • Stabilise before mobilise, and correct deformity before arthroplasty.
    • MCP silicone arthroplasty improves alignment, appearance and arc position (it does not reliably improve grip strength) and is combined with intrinsic release, ulnar sagittal band release and radial collateral reconstruction.
  • Perioperative medical management:
    • CONTINUE methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine through surgery.
    • HOLD biologics and schedule surgery at the END of the dosing interval; restart at ~14 days post-operatively once the wound is satisfactory.
    • Hold JAK inhibitors for 3 days.
    • Screen the CERVICAL SPINE before any general anaesthetic — atlantoaxial instability (anterior atlanto-dens interval >3–5 mm, or a posterior AADI <14 mm) and basilar invagination are common and can be catastrophic on intubation.

Ulnar nerve compression at the wrist (Guyon canal)

Localising an ulnar lesion by which branches are spared — the dorsal cutaneous branch rule — plus the zones, the causes and the classic signs.

3 of 10 sittings2022, 2024S, 2024F

  • Guyon's canal boundaries:
    • Floor — the transverse carpal ligament and the hypothenar muscles.
    • Roof — the volar carpal ligament and palmaris brevis.
    • Ulnar wall — the pisiform and FCU.
    • Radial wall — the hook of the hamate.
    • The ulnar NERVE lies ULNAR to the ulnar ARTERY within the canal.
    • Note that the roof of Guyon's canal is the volar carpal ligament while the floor includes the transverse carpal ligament — which is why a carpal tunnel release also decompresses Guyon's canal indirectly.
  • The three zones — each gives a distinct picture:
    • Zone I — proximal to the bifurcation: MIXED motor and sensory loss. Usually a ganglion or a hook of hamate fracture.
    • Zone II — the DEEP MOTOR branch, curving around the hook of the hamate: PURE MOTOR, with NO sensory loss. The classic hook of hamate nonunion or ganglion.
    • Zone III — the SUPERFICIAL SENSORY branch: PURE SENSORY to the volar small finger and the ulnar half of the ring finger. Usually an ulnar artery thrombosis/aneurysm.
    • Palmaris brevis is the only muscle innervated by the superficial branch, so its function is preserved in a zone II lesion.
  • The DORSAL CUTANEOUS BRANCH rule — the key localising principle:
    • The dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve arises ~5–8 cm PROXIMAL to the wrist and passes dorsally around the ulna, NOT through Guyon's canal.
    • Numbness on the DORSO-ULNAR hand = a lesion PROXIMAL to the wrist (distal forearm, cubital tunnel, or higher).
    • SPARING of the dorso-ulnar hand, with volar small-finger numbness = a lesion AT or DISTAL to Guyon's canal.
    • An ulnar-sided distal forearm laceration producing dorsal hand and small-finger numbness with NO motor deficit and NO volar sensory loss localises to the dorsal cutaneous branch alone, in the distal forearm proximal to Guyon's canal — an isolated, purely sensory injury.
    • The same logic in reverse identifies the palmar cutaneous branch for the median nerve.
  • Causes at the wrist:
    • Ganglion — the commonest, usually arising from the pisotriquetral or hamate-triquetral joint.
    • Hook of hamate fracture or nonunion — the deep motor branch curves directly around it.
    • Ulnar artery thrombosis or aneurysm — hypothenar hammer syndrome, from repetitive palm-strike trauma; look for digital ischaemia in the ring and small fingers and get an Allen test and angiography/duplex.
    • Repetitive external pressure — cyclist's palsy (handlebar palsy).
    • Anomalous muscle (accessory abductor digiti minimi) crossing the canal.
    • Because a compressive mass is so common, IMAGING (MRI or ultrasound) is warranted for a wrist-level ulnar neuropathy — unlike cubital tunnel syndrome, where it usually is not.
  • The three eponymous signs:
    • Froment sign — thumb IP FLEXION when pinching a piece of paper, because the patient recruits FPL (median/AIN) to compensate for a weak adductor pollicis (ulnar). It is a sign of adductor pollicis weakness.
    • Jeanne sign — compensatory thumb MCP HYPEREXTENSION during the same manoeuvre.
    • Wartenberg sign — persistent small-finger ABDUCTION, from unopposed EDM (radial) with weak third palmar interosseous (ulnar); the patient's small finger catches on pockets.
    • Also: clawing of the ring and small fingers (intrinsic-minus posture), and Masse sign (flattening of the palmar arch from hypothenar weakness).
  • Distinguishing a WRIST-level from an ELBOW-level (cubital tunnel) lesion:
    • Cubital tunnel additionally weakens FCU and the FDP to the ring and small fingers (which a wrist lesion cannot).
    • Cubital tunnel additionally has DORSO-ULNAR sensory loss (the dorsal cutaneous branch).
    • Those two features are the discriminators.
    • The "ulnar paradox": a PROXIMAL (elbow) lesion produces LESS clawing than a distal one, because the FDP to the ring and small fingers is also paralysed, so the DIPs cannot flex. As the FDP recovers, the claw worsens.
  • Compression sites at and around the elbow — proximal to distal:
    • Arcade of Struthers (~8–10 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle — a fascial band from the medial intermuscular septum to the medial head of triceps). Do not confuse it with the ligament of Struthers, which compresses the median nerve.
    • Medial intermuscular septum.
    • Medial epicondyle / cubital tunnel retinaculum (Osborne's ligament) — the commonest site.
    • Between the two heads of FCU (the FCU aponeurosis).
    • The deep flexor-pronator aponeurosis as the nerve exits distally.
    • After an anterior transposition, the commonest site of PERSISTENT compression is an incompletely released MEDIAL INTERMUSCULAR SEPTUM (or a residual fascial band creating a new kink) — the septum must be excised, not merely divided, when transposing.
  • Motor recovery after ulnar nerve injury:
    • Reinnervation is proximal to distal at ~1 mm/day, so after a mid-forearm laceration the FDP to the ring/small fingers recovers before the intrinsics.
    • The INTRINSICS (interossei and adductor pollicis) are the LAST to recover, and frequently never do — the motor end-plates degenerate irreversibly by roughly 18–24 months and the regeneration distance is long.
    • This is the rationale for early tendon transfer (for example an FDS or ECRL-based transfer for clawing and pinch) or a supercharged end-to-side AIN-to-ulnar motor nerve transfer to "babysit" the intrinsics while the primary repair regenerates.

Bennett fracture & thumb CMC injuries

Deforming forces and reduction manoeuvre.

3 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2024S

  • What a Bennett fracture is:
    • An intra-articular fracture-dislocation of the base of the FIRST metacarpal.
    • A volar-ulnar fragment stays reduced, held to the trapezium by the anterior oblique (volar beak) ligament.
    • Everything else — the metacarpal shaft — displaces. The fragment is not the problem; the shaft is.
  • Deforming forces on the metacarpal shaft:
    • Abductor pollicis longus pulls the shaft proximally, dorsally and radially — shortening and subluxation.
    • Adductor pollicis pulls the distal metacarpal into adduction and SUPINATION.
    • EPL and EPB add an extension force.
    • The volar-ulnar fragment stays put — so the deformity is entirely of the shaft relative to it.
  • Reduction manoeuvre — the components:
    • Longitudinal traction.
    • Palmar abduction.
    • PRONATION (to counter the adductor-induced supination).
    • Direct pressure over the base of the metacarpal.
    • Pronation, NOT supination, is the correct rotational component — a repeated "which is false" item.
  • Treatment:
    • Closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (metacarpal to trapezium and/or to the index metacarpal) is adequate for most fractures with a small fragment — the pin does not need to capture the fragment.
    • ORIF with a screw for fragments >15–20% of the articular surface or with >1–2 mm of displacement.
    • Anatomic reduction reduces but does NOT eliminate later CMC arthritis — a point worth making to the patient.
  • Rolando fracture:
    • The comminuted T- or Y-shaped intra-articular base fracture.
    • More difficult and with a worse prognosis than a Bennett.
    • Treated with ORIF, external fixation or pinning depending on the degree of comminution; restore length and the articular surface as far as possible.
  • Thumb ulnar collateral ligament injury ("skier's thumb"):
    • A STENER LESION — the adductor aponeurosis interposed between the retracted UCL and its insertion — prevents healing and mandates surgical repair.
    • Suspect it with >30° of laxity in 30° of MCP flexion, a >15–20° side-to-side difference, or a palpable mass at the ulnar MCP.
    • Test in both extension (accessory collateral/volar plate) and 30° flexion (proper collateral); a local block makes the examination reliable.
    • Ultrasound or MRI confirms it when the examination is equivocal; get radiographs first to exclude a displaced bony avulsion.
    • Chronic UCL insufficiency causes weak key/pinch grip, and eventual MCP instability and arthritis — treated with reconstruction (free tendon graft) or MCP fusion.
    • The radial collateral equivalent ("gamekeeper's" is properly the chronic ulnar attritional injury) is less common and rarely has a Stener-type block.

Distal radioulnar joint instability & TFCC

Stabilisers of the DRUJ, ulnar styloid fractures, and ulnocarpal impaction.

  • What actually stabilises the DRUJ:
    • The DEEP fibres of the dorsal and volar radioulnar ligaments of the TFCC — the ligamentum subcruentum, inserting at the FOVEA — are the PRIMARY stabilisers.
    • The bony architecture contributes little: the sigmoid notch is shallow and its radius of curvature is much larger than the ulnar head's.
    • Secondary stabilisers: pronator quadratus, the ECU subsheath, the interosseous membrane (distal oblique bundle), and the ulnar capsule.
    • The superficial fibres insert on the styloid; the deep foveal fibres are the ones that matter.
  • The ulnar styloid fracture:
    • An ulnar styloid TIP fracture is usually irrelevant to DRUJ stability and to outcome — it does not need fixation.
    • A BASILAR (base) ulnar styloid fracture disrupts the foveal insertion of the TFCC and is associated with instability.
    • The decision to fix is based on the EXAMINATION of DRUJ stability after fixing the radius — not on the radiograph.
  • Algorithm after distal radius ORIF:
    • Reduce and fix the radius anatomically first — most DRUJ instability resolves once radial length, inclination and tilt are restored.
    • Then examine the DRUJ in neutral, pronation and supination, against the other side.
    • Stable → no further treatment.
    • Reducible but unstable → immobilise in SUPINATION in a long-arm/Muenster cast for ~6 weeks, or pin the ulna to the radius in supination; repair a foveal TFCC avulsion or fix a large basilar styloid fragment.
    • Irreducible → open the joint — an entrapped ECU tendon or an interposed TFCC is the usual block.
    • Fixing a small styloid tip fragment does not restore stability — a common wrong answer.
  • Ulnocarpal impaction (abutment):
    • Ulnar-sided wrist pain worse with grip, ulnar deviation and pronation, from POSITIVE ulnar variance.
    • Causes: congenital positive variance; acquired after a distal radius malunion with radial shortening; after radial head excision (Essex-Lopresti); and after premature distal radius physeal arrest.
    • Radiographs show cystic and sclerotic change in the ULNAR HEAD, the ulnar corner of the LUNATE and the TRIQUETRUM.
    • MRI shows a central TFCC perforation (degenerative, Palmer class 2) and lunate oedema; LT ligament attenuation follows.
    • Pronated grip view accentuates the positive variance and is the useful film.
  • Treatment of ulnocarpal impaction:
    • Activity modification and injection first.
    • Ulnar shortening osteotomy — the workhorse; it also tightens the ulnocarpal ligaments, which is an advantage when there is associated LT laxity.
    • Arthroscopic wafer procedure (resecting 2–3 mm of the ulnar dome through a central TFCC tear) — avoids a plate and nonunion, but contraindicated with significant DRUJ arthritis or with DRUJ instability, and it cannot correct more than ~2–3 mm of variance.
    • Address any distal radius malunion at the same time if that is the cause.
  • Palmer classification of TFCC tears:
    • Class 1 = TRAUMATIC.
    • 1A — central perforation.
    • 1B — ulnar/foveal avulsion ± ulnar styloid fracture — the one associated with DRUJ instability.
    • 1C — distal ulnocarpal ligament (ulnolunate/ulnotriquetral) avulsion.
    • 1D — radial avulsion from the sigmoid notch.
    • Class 2 = DEGENERATIVE — the ulnocarpal impaction spectrum (2A thinning → 2E arthritis).
    • Vascularity drives treatment: PERIPHERAL (1B, 1C) tears are vascular and are REPAIRED; CENTRAL (1A) tears are avascular and are DÉBRIDED. The TFCC receives blood only in its peripheral 10–40%.

Hand tumours & ganglion cysts

The commonest masses of the hand and their behaviour.

2 of 10 sittings2020, 2024S

  • Ganglion cyst — the commonest soft-tissue mass of the hand and wrist (60–70%):
    • Dorsal wrist (~70%) — arises from the scapholunate ligament; may be occult and only apparent in flexion.
    • Volar radial — from the radioscaphoid/STT joint, lying adjacent to the radial artery (do an Allen test before excision).
    • Flexor sheath (retinacular) cyst — a small, firm, tender pea at the A1 pulley.
    • Mucous cyst — at the DIP joint, associated with an osteophyte; often presents with a nail groove/deformity.
    • Transilluminates; imaging is only needed when the presentation is atypical.
  • Management of a ganglion:
    • Observation is entirely appropriate — many resolve spontaneously; reassurance is often all that is needed.
    • Aspiration has a high recurrence rate (~50%+) but is diagnostic and low-risk.
    • Excision requires removal of the STALK and a cuff of joint capsule — recurrence ~5–15% if done properly, much higher if the stalk is left.
    • For a MUCOUS cyst the associated DIP OSTEOPHYTE must be excised or it recurs; skin coverage may need a local flap.
    • Do not aspirate a mucous cyst with thin overlying skin — the risk is seeding the DIP joint.
  • Giant cell tumour of tendon sheath (localised TGCT/PVNS) — the second commonest hand mass:
    • A firm, painless, slow-growing nodule, typically volar/radial in a digit.
    • LOW signal on BOTH T1 and T2 from haemosiderin — the diagnostic MRI appearance.
    • Marginal excision; recurrence 10–20%, higher with satellite lesions or joint involvement.
  • Other common benign lesions:
    • Epidermal inclusion cyst — post-traumatic, at the fingertip or palm.
    • Lipoma — often deep and larger than it appears.
    • Glomus tumour — subungual, exquisitely painful, cold-sensitive, with pinpoint tenderness (Love's test) relieved by a proximal tourniquet (Hildreth's test); MRI shows a small, intensely enhancing T2-bright nodule.
    • Schwannoma — eccentric to the nerve and shellable; a neurofibroma is intraneural and cannot be excised without sacrificing fascicles.
  • Bone lesions:
    • ENCHONDROMA is the commonest primary bone tumour of the hand and the commonest bone tumour of the phalanges.
    • It frequently presents as a pathologic fracture of a proximal phalanx.
    • Let the fracture heal FIRST, then curette and graft if symptomatic — the standard answer.
    • Multiple enchondromas raise Ollier disease or Maffucci syndrome (with haemangiomas), which carry a real risk of chondrosarcoma.
  • Malignancy — rare, but do not miss it:
    • Squamous cell carcinoma of the skin or nail bed is the commonest hand malignancy — a chronic non-healing "paronychia" or nail bed lesion should be biopsied.
    • Epithelioid sarcoma is the classic mimic — it looks like a benign nodule or "ganglion" in a young adult, then recurs, ulcerates and spreads along tendon sheaths and to lymph nodes.
    • Melanoma (including subungual, presenting as a pigmented streak with Hutchinson's sign) and metastatic lung carcinoma (acrometastasis to the distal phalanx) also occur.
    • Any hand mass that recurs, ulcerates, grows or is painful must be biopsied — with a longitudinal incision that can be excised with the definitive specimen.

  • Who gets it:

    • Pain with resisted small-finger flexion (the flexor tendons pass around the hook).
    • Sometimes ulnar nerve symptoms (deep motor branch, Guyon zone II) or ulnar artery thrombosis.
    • It is very commonly diagnosed late, as a "wrist sprain" that never settles.

    • Plain radiographs are frequently NORMAL.

    • MRI shows marrow oedema early and is an alternative.

    • NONUNION is very common — a tenuous blood supply plus continuous shear from the transverse carpal and pisohamate ligaments.
    • Attritional rupture of the flexor tendons to the SMALL and RING fingers against the fracture edge.
    • Ulnar neuropathy (deep motor branch) and ulnar artery thrombosis.

    • A genuinely acute, non-displaced fracture may unite in a cast, but the union rate is poor and most athletes will not accept the immobilisation.

Distal radius malunion & corrective osteotomy

Indications and planning for corrective osteotomy.

1 of 10 sittings2021

  • The problem:
    • Malunion is the commonest complication of a distal radius fracture.
    • A symptomatic malunion causes pain, loss of grip strength, limited forearm rotation, DRUJ incongruity and midcarpal instability (adaptive carpal malalignment, with the lunate extending to compensate for dorsal tilt).
    • Radial shortening additionally produces ulnocarpal impaction.
  • The best indication for corrective osteotomy:
    • A SYMPTOMATIC malunion in a YOUNG, ACTIVE patient with preserved joint surfaces and good motion.
    • Supported by dorsal tilt >20°, radial shortening >5 mm, radial inclination <10°, or an incongruent DRUJ.
    • Malalignment ALONE, in an asymptomatic or low-demand elderly patient, is NOT an indication — the recurring distractor.
    • Established radiocarpal arthritis is a CONTRAINDICATION — salvage instead.
  • Timing:
    • Correct EARLY where possible — within 3–6 months.
    • Early osteotomy is technically easier, the soft tissues are more compliant (so the correction can be held), and adaptive carpal changes are still reversible.
    • Waiting for "the fracture to fully remodel" is not a reason to delay in an adult.
  • Planning and technique:
    • Bilateral wrist radiographs and CT to quantify the deformity in all three planes against the normal side.
    • 3D planning and patient-specific cutting guides improve accuracy, particularly for multiplanar and intra-articular deformity.
    • Extra-articular osteotomy: an opening wedge through a volar approach with a structural graft or a volar locking plate (restores length as well as tilt), or a closing wedge (simpler, but shortens further).
    • Intra-articular osteotomy is far more demanding and requires arthroscopic or open joint visualisation.
    • Concurrent carpal tunnel release is often required and should be discussed.
  • Do not forget the ulnar side:
    • An isolated ULNAR SHORTENING osteotomy may suffice when the dominant problem is ulnocarpal impaction from radial shortening without significant tilt deformity — a simpler operation with a reliable result.
    • In an older patient with DRUJ arthritis, consider a Darrach or Sauvé-Kapandji rather than a radial osteotomy.
  • Outcomes:
    • Good for pain, grip strength and forearm rotation.
    • Full restoration of motion is uncommon — set expectations accordingly.
    • Nonunion at the osteotomy is uncommon with a locking plate and graft; loss of correction is the more usual failure.

Tendon transfers in the hand

Principles of transfer and the standard transfers for each palsy.

4 of 10 sittings2017, 2018, 2019, 2021

  • Principles of tendon transfer — the checklist:
    • Correct the deformity first — the joints must be supple and passively correctable; a transfer cannot move a stiff joint.
    • An adequate, supple soft-tissue bed — no scar in the path of the transfer.
    • An expendable donor, whose loss the patient will not miss.
    • ONE TENDON, ONE FUNCTION.
    • A synergistic donor (wrist extensors with finger flexors, wrist flexors with finger extensors) — easier to retrain.
    • A straight line of pull.
    • Adequate EXCURSION — wrist ~33 mm, finger extensors ~50 mm, finger flexors ~70 mm (the "3-5-7" rule).
    • Adequate STRENGTH — a transferred tendon loses roughly one grade of power, so the donor should be at least grade 4+.
  • Low ulnar nerve palsy (claw hand):
    • The deformity is MCP HYPEREXTENSION of the ring and small fingers with PIP/DIP FLEXION, from loss of the intrinsics (which normally flex the MCP and extend the IP joints).
    • Bouvier test: if blocking MCP hyperextension restores IP extension, the extensor mechanism is intact and a static MCP block (Zancolli capsulodesis) will suffice.
    • Bouvier-negative (IP extension not restored) needs a dynamic transfer into the lateral bands: modified Stiles-Bunnell (FDS of the ring finger to the lateral bands) or Brand (ECRB or ECRL extended with a graft).
    • Restore key pinch with an ADDUCTORPLASTY (ECRB or FDS to adductor pollicis) when Froment's sign is positive.
    • Correct Wartenberg sign (persistent small-finger abduction) by transferring the EDM ulnar slip to the A1/A2 pulley or the radial lateral band.
    • The presentation of thumb weakness, difficulty grasping, thumb MCP hyperextension and clawing of the ring and small fingers is a LOW ULNAR NERVE PALSY — the reconstruction must restore thumb adduction/key pinch and correct the claw.
  • Radial nerve palsy — the classic triad of transfers:
    • Pronator teres → ECRB for wrist extension.
    • FCU (Jones) or FCR (Brand) → EDC for finger extension.
    • Palmaris longus → EPL for thumb extension (or FDS ring if PL is absent).
    • FCR-based transfers preserve wrist ulnar deviation balance and leave the FCU as a wrist flexor — increasingly preferred over the classic FCU transfer.
    • Remember to wait: most closed radial nerve palsies with a humeral shaft fracture recover, and brachioradialis is the first muscle to return.
  • Low median nerve palsy (loss of opposition) — the opponensplasties:
    • EIP (Burkhalter) — expendable, good excursion, no need for a pulley construction.
    • FDS of the ring finger (Bunnell) — strong, but avoid in a low-median-and-ulnar palsy (risk of swan-neck) .
    • Abductor digiti minimi (Huber) — favoured in children and congenital hypoplasia, because it also adds bulk to a flat thenar eminence.
    • Palmaris longus with a palmar aponeurosis extension (Camitz) — the transfer of choice in advanced carpal tunnel syndrome, done at the time of release; it restores palmar abduction rather than true opposition.
  • Technical execution:
    • The Pulvertaft weave is the strongest tendon-to-tendon junction and is the standard for transfers.
    • Set the tension with the wrist and digits in the resting posture, and check it with the tenodesis effect (passive wrist extension should flex the digits and vice versa).
    • Err on the side of slightly tight — transfers stretch out, they do not shorten.
    • Post-operative re-education matters; a synergistic transfer needs less of it.

Treatment options for basal thumb arthritis, including denervation.

  • Presentation and staging:
    • Thumb CMC (trapeziometacarpal) arthritis is common in women over 50; the joint is a biconcave saddle with high shear loads.
    • Eaton-Littler stage I — joint space widening from synovitis/laxity, normal contours.
    • Stage II — slight narrowing with debris/osteophytes <2 mm.
    • Stage III — significant narrowing with osteophytes >2 mm, sclerosis and cystic change.
    • Stage IV — PANTRAPEZIAL, with the STT joint involved. This is the stage that changes the operation.
  • Examination and first-line treatment:
    • Positive GRIND test (axial load plus rotation) and tenderness directly at the CMC joint.
    • Advanced disease shows an adduction contracture of the first metacarpal with compensatory MCP hyperextension and a "shoulder" sign at the base.
    • Get a true (Robert's) AP view of the thumb and a stress view to assess subluxation and the STT joint.
    • First line: a thumb spica orthosis, NSAIDs and a corticosteroid injection — effective and often durable for a year or more, especially in earlier stages.
  • Surgical options:
    • Trapeziectomy alone.
    • Trapeziectomy with ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition (LRTI), usually with FCR.
    • Suspensionplasty / suture-button suspension — avoids harvesting a tendon and allows earlier motion.
    • CMC ARTHRODESIS — for the young, high-demand labourer: durable and strong, but loses motion (difficulty flattening the hand), risks nonunion, and transfers load to the STT joint.
    • Volar ligament reconstruction (Eaton-Littler) — for stage I instability in a young patient before arthritis develops.
    • Prosthetic arthroplasty — increasingly used in Europe with good early motion, but with implant-specific failure modes.
  • The evidence point:
    • Systematic reviews and RCTs, including Cochrane, show NO clear superiority of LRTI over simple trapeziectomy in pain, function or strength.
    • LRTI has more complications and a longer operative time.
    • This is a frequently tested "which statement is true" item — simple trapeziectomy remains a fully defensible operation.
  • CMC denervation:
    • Excision of the articular branches supplying the joint — from the superficial radial nerve, the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, and the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve.
    • It is MOTION-PRESERVING and JOINT-PRESERVING — it relieves pain without altering the biomechanics of the thumb.
    • Attractive in high-demand patients, and it does not preclude later definitive surgery.
    • The trade-off: pain relief is less predictable and less durable, and a diagnostic local block should be used to select patients.
  • Do not forget the MCP joint:
    • MCP hyperextension >30° must be addressed at the time of surgery — with a volar plate capsulodesis, sesamoidesis or MCP fusion.
    • If it is not corrected, the reconstruction will fail, because the hyperextended MCP recreates the adduction/subluxation force at the base.
    • An adduction contracture of the first web may also need release.

  • The Stener lesion — the reason this injury needs surgery:
    • Present in roughly 60–80% of complete tears.
    • The torn UCL retracts proximally and becomes trapped SUPERFICIAL to the adductor aponeurosis, which then interposes between the ligament and its insertion on the proximal phalanx.
    • Healing is impossible in that position, so surgical repair is required.
    • A palpable tender mass just proximal to the MCP joint suggests it; ultrasound or MRI confirms it.
  • Treatment:
    • Partial (stable) tears heal well in a thumb spica cast or orthosis for 4–6 weeks.
    • Repair with a suture anchor for: a complete tear, a Stener lesion, or a displaced/rotated avulsion fragment (>2–3 mm of displacement, or involving >20% of the articular surface).
    • Protect the dorsal sensory branch of the radial nerve during the approach.
    • Immobilise for 4–6 weeks post-repair, then a protective orthosis for sport for 3 months.
  • Consequences of leaving it:
    • Chronic pain and weakness of key and pinch grip — the thumb collapses under load.
    • MCP instability, then post-traumatic MCP arthritis.
    • Chronic cases are treated with reconstruction using a free tendon graft (palmaris or a slip of ECRL) or, with established arthritis, MCP arthrodesis.
  • The radial collateral ligament:
    • Less common than UCL injury (roughly 1:4).
    • Tends to tear more distally.
    • Rarely forms a Stener-equivalent lesion, because there is no adductor aponeurosis on the radial side — so more of these are managed non-operatively.
    • It presents with volar-ulnar subluxation of the proximal phalanx and pain on the radial side.

Trigger finger & stenosing tenosynovitis

Pathophysiology and evidence-based treatment.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2019, 2021

  • What it is:
    • Stenosing tenosynovitis at the A1 pulley — a size mismatch between a thickened flexor tendon/sheath and the pulley.
    • Causes catching, locking and pain, often referred to the PIP joint rather than the palm.
    • Most often the RING finger and the THUMB.
    • It is a TENDON-SHEATH problem, not a tendon problem — histology shows fibrocartilaginous metaplasia of the pulley, not inflammation of the tendon.
  • Associations:
    • DIABETES MELLITUS — the strongest association; diabetic patients have more severe, multi-digit disease and respond less well to injection.
    • Thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, amyloidosis, gout, and carpal tunnel syndrome (they frequently coexist).
    • Renal dialysis (β2-microglobulin amyloid) and mucopolysaccharidoses in children.
  • Corticosteroid injection is first-line:
    • Success of roughly 60–90% in non-diabetics after one or two injections.
    • Success is materially lower in diabetics (~50%), and in long-standing disease, multi-digit involvement and younger patients.
    • Warn diabetics of a transient rise in blood glucose for several days after injection.
    • Injection into the sheath and into the subcutaneous tissue over the A1 pulley are similarly effective; repeated injections risk fat atrophy, depigmentation and tendon attenuation.
  • Surgical release:
    • Open or percutaneous release of the A1 pulley has >95% success.
    • In the THUMB, protect the RADIAL digital nerve — it crosses obliquely and superficially at the level of the A1 pulley and is the structure injured in a percutaneous release.
    • In RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, do NOT release the A1 pulley — it accelerates ulnar drift by removing the radial restraint on the flexor tendons. Perform a flexor tenosynovectomy and/or resect one FDS slip instead.
    • Releasing the A1 pulley alone does not cause bowstringing, because A2 is preserved.
  • Paediatric trigger thumb — a distinct entity:
    • A fixed IP FLEXION deformity, not intermittent triggering, with a palpable Notta's node.
    • Usually presents at 1–3 years; it is not congenital (it is essentially never present at birth).
    • A proportion resolve spontaneously, so observation for 6–12 months is reasonable.
    • Release is highly successful if needed, and results are good even when delayed.
    • Paediatric trigger finger (as opposed to thumb) is different — often anomalous FDS anatomy, and simple A1 release alone frequently fails.
  • Persistent triggering after release — think through the causes:
    • Incomplete release of the A1 pulley.
    • A retained FDS slip or a nodule catching at the FDS decussation.
    • Triggering at another level — the A3 pulley, or at the palmar aponeurosis pulley.
    • A locked PIP from a different cause (volar plate, sagittal band, an intra-articular loose body).

Wrist arthroscopy & ulnar-sided wrist pain

A structured approach to the ulnar-sided wrist.

1 of 10 sittings2021

  • The differential — ulnar-sided wrist pain is "the low back pain of the wrist":
    • TFCC tear.
    • Ulnocarpal impaction.
    • DRUJ instability or arthritis.
    • Lunotriquetral ligament injury.
    • ECU tendinitis or subluxation (a torn ECU subsheath).
    • Pisotriquetral arthritis.
    • Hook of hamate fracture.
    • Ulnar artery thrombosis (hypothenar hammer) and ulnar nerve compression.
  • Localise by anatomy first:
    • Dorsal-ulnar → ECU, DRUJ, dorsal TFCC.
    • Volar-ulnar → pisotriquetral joint, FCU, ulnar artery and nerve, hook of hamate, LT ligament.
    • Central/deep → TFCC and ulnocarpal impaction.
  • Provocative tests:
    • Fovea sign — tenderness in the soft spot between the ulnar styloid and FCU; highly sensitive for foveal TFCC and LT pathology.
    • Ulnocarpal stress test — axial load with ulnar deviation and forearm rotation.
    • Piano-key and ballottement tests — DRUJ instability.
    • ECU synergy test — resisted thumb radial abduction with the forearm supinated reproduces ECU tendinitis pain.
    • Shear (Kleinman) test for the LT ligament; pisotriquetral grind for that joint.
  • Working up a persistent case:
    • Start with a neutral-rotation PA (for ulnar variance) and a pronated grip view.
    • Then MRI, ideally arthrogram, for the TFCC and interosseous ligaments.
    • CT for a suspected hook of hamate fracture — plain films will miss it.
    • In an athlete with volar-ulnar pain and a negative ECU examination, the hook of hamate and the pisotriquetral joint are the lesions to chase.
    • Wrist arthroscopy is the diagnostic gold standard for intra-articular pathology when imaging is equivocal.
  • Wrist arthroscopy — what it shows and the portal anatomy:
    • TFCC: the "trampoline" test (loss of resilience when probed) and the "hook" test (the TFCC lifts up radially) indicate foveal detachment.
    • SL and LT interosseous ligaments: Geissler grading I–IV (I attenuation, II step with a probe-passable gap, III a probe passes between the bones, IV an arthroscope passes through — "drive-through" sign).
    • Radiocarpal portals are named for their relationship to the extensor compartments: 3-4 (the workhorse viewing portal, just distal to Lister's tubercle), 4-5, 6R (radial to ECU) and 6U (ulnar to ECU).
    • The 6U portal risks the DORSAL SENSORY BRANCH of the ulnar nerve — use an open (nick-and-spread) technique.
    • Midcarpal portals (MCR, MCU) are needed to assess the midcarpal joint and to grade instability properly.
  • Treatment follows the diagnosis:
    • Peripheral (vascular) TFCC tears are REPAIRED; central (avascular) tears are DÉBRIDED.
    • Ulnocarpal impaction → ulnar shortening osteotomy (or a wafer procedure).
    • DRUJ instability → foveal repair, or ligament reconstruction if chronic.
    • ECU subluxation → subsheath reconstruction; pisotriquetral arthritis → pisiform excision (protecting the ulnar nerve).
