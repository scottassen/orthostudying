Amputation: Level selection, technique & outcomes

The energy cost of each level, what predicts a stump will heal, and the technical details that make each level work.

8 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2025 (x2)

  • Energy expenditure above baseline walking — a repeatedly tested list:
    • Syme ~15%.
    • Traumatic transtibial ~25%; dysvascular transtibial ~40%.
    • Through-knee ~50%.
    • Traumatic transfemoral ~65%; dysvascular transfemoral ~100%.
    • Bilateral transtibial ~40%; bilateral transfemoral >200%.
    • Note that bilateral transtibial costs LESS than a single transfemoral — the knee is worth more than the limb count.
    • Walking VELOCITY FALLS as the level ascends, but oxygen consumption PER UNIT DISTANCE rises — patients self-select a slower speed to keep oxygen consumption per MINUTE near normal. That is the physiological explanation behind the whole table.
  • Preserving the knee is the single most important functional goal:
    • A transtibial amputee uses far less energy and has far better prosthetic function than a transfemoral amputee.
    • Ideal transtibial length is 12–15 cm (a hand's breadth) below the joint line, with a long posterior myofasciocutaneous flap (the posterior compartment has the better blood supply and provides a durable, padded, sensate cover).
    • Bevel the anterior tibial crest, and cut the fibula 1–2 cm shorter than the tibia.
    • Perform a myodesis or myoplasty so the muscles are stabilised rather than sliding over the bone end.
  • Predicting healing in peripheral vascular disease:
    • Ankle-brachial index >0.5 — but unreliable if the vessels are calcified (diabetes, renal failure); use TOE PRESSURES instead.
    • Toe pressure >30–45 mmHg.
    • Transcutaneous oxygen tension (TcPO2) >30–40 mmHg — the best single predictor.
    • Serum albumin >3.0 g/dL.
    • Total lymphocyte count >1500/mm³.
    • Nutrition and immunocompetence are as important as perfusion — a well-perfused stump in a malnourished patient still fails.
  • Transfemoral amputation:
    • Healing rates are high (>90%) because of the excellent proximal blood supply.
    • But functional prosthetic ambulation in the dysvascular elderly is POOR — only roughly 25–40% become community ambulators.
    • Mortality at 1–2 years is high (30–50%) in this population — the amputation is a marker of systemic vascular disease.
    • Retain as much femoral length as possible, and perform a MYODESIS of the ADDUCTOR MAGNUS.
    • Without it, the unopposed abductors and hip flexors produce an ABDUCTION and FLEXION deformity that makes prosthetic fitting and gait very difficult.
  • Through-knee (knee disarticulation):
    • Preserves a long, END-BEARING lever arm with excellent muscle balance, and does not violate the medullary canal (less blood loss, less pain, no marrow contamination).
    • Particularly useful in non-ambulatory patients — better sitting balance and transfers.
    • And in children, because it preserves the distal femoral physis and avoids terminal overgrowth.
    • Drawbacks: a BULBOUS end, a cosmetically poor and functionally limited prosthetic knee (the prosthetic knee centre sits distal to the contralateral knee), and difficulty fitting.
  • Syme amputation (ankle disarticulation):
    • Preserves an END-BEARING residual limb, allowing short-distance walking WITHOUT a prosthesis — a real advantage for night-time and bathroom mobility.
    • Low energy cost (~15%).
    • Requires an intact, well-perfused HEEL PAD and secure fixation of the pad to the tibia.
    • Heel pad MIGRATION is the classic complication — the pad slides posteriorly and the stump becomes non-end-bearing.
    • It leaves a bulbous stump with cosmetic prosthetic limitations, which some patients decline for that reason.
  • Paediatric amputation — the rule and the reason:
    • DISARTICULATION is preferred over TRANSOSSEOUS amputation wherever possible.
    • Because a transosseous amputation in a growing child produces TERMINAL BONY OVERGROWTH — appositional new bone at the cut end, not physeal growth — requiring revision in up to 15–30%.
    • The humerus, fibula, tibia and femur are the commonest sites, in that order.
    • Children have excellent healing, adaptability and lower energy costs than adults, and rarely develop phantom pain.
    • Revision for OVERGROWTH — rather than for prosthetic fitting — is the commonest reason to reoperate in a child.
  • Upper extremity amputation:
    • Preserve EVERY MILLIMETRE of length.
    • Rejection rates for upper limb prostheses are high, and function is often better with a well-preserved partial hand than with a prosthesis.
    • Wrist disarticulation preserves forearm PRONATION and SUPINATION and is preferred to transradial where feasible.
    • Targeted muscle reinnervation at the time of amputation improves myoelectric control and markedly reduces neuroma and phantom pain.
  • The mangled extremity:
    • The MESS score has poor POSITIVE predictive value and should NOT be used alone to decide on amputation — it is better at predicting salvage than at predicting the need for amputation.
    • The LEAP study showed that 2-year functional outcomes were SIMILAR for reconstruction and amputation.
    • And that the strongest predictors of outcome were PSYCHOSOCIAL factors — self-efficacy, education, poverty, smoking and social support — not the injury characteristics.
    • Insensate plantar skin is NOT an absolute indication for amputation — sensation frequently recovers, and outcomes in the insensate group were no different.
    • Reconstruction costs more over a lifetime, takes more operations, and has more rehospitalisations — but that is a shared decision, not a formula.

Periprosthetic joint infection: Diagnosis & management

The criteria and the thresholds that differ from a native joint, and choosing between retention and removal.

9 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x2), 2019, 2021 (x2), 2022, 2023, 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Diagnostic criteria (MSIS / ICM 2018) — a MAJOR criterion is definitive:
    • TWO positive periprosthetic cultures with the SAME organism, or
    • A SINUS TRACT communicating with the prosthesis.
    • Either one alone confirms the diagnosis — no scoring needed.
  • The minor criteria are scored:
    • Elevated serum CRP and D-dimer, and elevated ESR.
    • Elevated synovial WBC count.
    • Elevated synovial polymorphonuclear percentage.
    • Positive alpha-defensin.
    • Elevated synovial CRP.
    • Positive histology (>5 neutrophils per high-power field) and intra-operative purulence.
    • A total score ≥6 is infected, 2–5 is inconclusive (needs intra-operative findings), and ≤1 is not infected.
  • Synovial fluid thresholds — and the trap:
    • ACUTE (<6 weeks): WBC >10,000/µL with >90% PMN.
    • CHRONIC: WBC >3,000/µL with >80% PMN.
    • These are FAR LOWER than the >50,000 used for a NATIVE joint.
    • Do NOT apply native-joint thresholds to a prosthesis — the single most common conceptual error, and it under-diagnoses infection.
  • Which test to rely on:
    • Synovial fluid ASPIRATION with cell count, differential and culture has the highest diagnostic value — the single most useful test.
    • ALPHA-DEFENSIN has high specificity, and is NOT affected by antibiotics or by inflammatory arthropathy — but it is expensive and has false positives with METALLOSIS.
    • Serum D-dimer is a newer adjunct with good sensitivity.
    • Next-generation sequencing is promising for culture-negative infection but is not yet a standalone criterion.
    • ESR and CRP are the appropriate screening tests — a normal CRP and ESR together make chronic PJI unlikely, with the important exception of low-virulence organisms such as C. acnes.
  • Getting the microbiology right:
    • HOLD antibiotics before aspiration and before intra-operative cultures — ideally ≥2 weeks — unless the patient is septic.
    • Take ≥3–5 tissue samples with clean instruments for each.
    • Hold cultures for 13–14 days to capture Cutibacterium acnes, especially in the shoulder.
    • SONICATION of the explanted implant increases yield in biofilm infection — it dislodges organisms from the biofilm that swab and tissue cultures miss.
    • Send tissue, not swabs; swab cultures are unreliable.
  • Who is at highest risk:
    • PREVIOUS PJI or revision surgery — the single strongest risk factor.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis and immunosuppression.
    • Obesity (BMI >40).
    • Poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c >7.5–8%).
    • Malnutrition and IV drug use.
    • Prolonged operative time, and a wound with persistent drainage.
    • Prior SEPTIC ARTHRITIS of the same joint is a major risk.
  • DAIR — when it is appropriate:
    • Débridement, Antibiotics and Implant Retention is appropriate for an ACUTE infection — <3–4 weeks from implantation, or <3 weeks of symptoms in an acute haematogenous infection.
    • With a WELL-FIXED implant, an intact soft-tissue envelope, NO sinus tract, and a susceptible organism.
    • It REQUIRES a MODULAR component (liner/head) EXCHANGE to be effective — a débridement without exchanging the modular parts leaves the biofilm behind.
    • Success rates are roughly 30–70%, and are WORST with S. aureus, delayed presentation and a sinus tract.
    • So the patient 3 weeks after a THA with fever, erythema and 24 hours of purulent drainage is a DAIR candidate — early, well-fixed, acute.
  • When the implant must come out:
    • A sinus tract, a chronic infection, a loose implant, or a resistant organism.
    • So a knee replacement 6 months post-operatively with a sinus tract requires TWO-STAGE revision with an antibiotic spacer — NOT DAIR.
    • Two-stage revision remains the gold standard in North America, with ~85–95% eradication.
    • Single-stage revision is increasingly used in Europe for selected patients with a known susceptible organism and good soft tissue, with comparable eradication in appropriate cases.
    • Chronic suppression is reserved for the patient unfit for revision, and resection arthroplasty or arthrodesis for the failed multiply-revised joint.
  • Dental prophylaxis:
    • Current AAOS/ADA guidance is that ROUTINE antibiotic prophylaxis is NOT indicated for most patients with a prosthetic joint before a dental procedure.
    • It is considered case-by-case in the immunocompromised, in poorly controlled diabetics, and in those with a prior PJI.
    • Treating the DENTAL INFECTION ITSELF is the priority — an infected tooth needing extraction is a bacteraemic source, and the extraction is the treatment.
    • Optimise dental health before elective arthroplasty rather than prophylaxing afterwards.

Hip arthroscopy, FAI & labral pathology

Cam versus pincer, reading acetabular version on a plain film, and who does badly after arthroscopy.

7 of 10 sittings2019, 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • The two morphologies:
    • CAM — an ASPHERICAL femoral head-neck junction, alpha angle >55°. More common in young athletic men, and thought to develop during the adolescent growth spurt in high-impact sport.
    • PINCER — ACETABULAR OVER-COVERAGE: global (coxa profunda, protrusio) or focal (anterior over-coverage / retroversion). More common in middle-aged women.
    • Most are MIXED.
    • Cam impingement produces an outside-in delamination of the anterosuperior acetabular cartilage with a relatively preserved labrum early; pincer produces a crushed labrum with a narrow rim of cartilage damage and a contrecoup posteroinferior lesion.
  • Reading acetabular retroversion on an AP pelvis:
    • CROSSOVER sign — the anterior wall line crosses the posterior wall line proximally.
    • POSTERIOR WALL sign — the posterior wall lies medial to the centre of the femoral head.
    • ISCHIAL SPINE sign — the ischial spine projects into the pelvic inlet.
    • The ISCHIAL SPINE SIGN is the most useful for distinguishing TRUE acetabular retroversion — a rotational abnormality of the WHOLE HEMIPELVIS — from focal anterior over-coverage, because it reflects the position of the entire hemipelvis.
    • A crossover sign ALONE can be produced by focal anterior over-coverage, or simply by pelvic TILT or ROTATION on the radiograph — check that the film is adequate (coccyx over the symphysis, symmetric obturator foramina) before believing it.
  • The evidence for arthroscopic FAI surgery:
    • The UK FASHIoN RCT and the Canadian and Australian trials show arthroscopic FAI surgery gives a MODEST but statistically significant improvement in hip-related quality of life over best conservative care at 12 months.
    • Though the difference may not exceed the MCID for all patients.
    • It is not a benign or universally successful procedure, and best conservative care is a legitimate alternative.
    • Set expectations honestly: most improve, a substantial minority do not, and return to elite sport is not guaranteed.
  • Who does badly — the selection point:
    • Outcomes for a labral tear in a patient over ~45–50, or with arthritis, are markedly worse.
    • Reoperation or conversion to THA is common — roughly 15–20% or more within 2 years in patients over 50.
    • Higher still with Tönnis grade ≥2 arthritis or a joint space <2 mm.
    • ARTHRITIS is the strongest predictor of failure and is a relative contraindication.
    • Other predictors of a poor result: a long duration of symptoms, workers' compensation, and generalised laxity.
  • Nerve injury — almost all from traction and the perineal post:
    • The PUDENDAL nerve is the most commonly injured — a neurapraxia from perineal post pressure, causing genital or perineal numbness (and, rarely, sexual dysfunction).
    • Then the LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS nerve (from the anterolateral portal).
    • Then the SCIATIC and FEMORAL nerves, from traction.
    • Most are transient neurapraxias that resolve.
    • Minimised by limiting traction TIME (<2 hours), using the least traction force necessary, a well-padded or POST-LESS table, and careful portal placement.
    • Take traction breaks during a long case.
  • Technical points that matter:
    • Labral REPAIR is preferred to débridement where the tissue is repairable — it restores the suction seal.
    • The capsule should be CLOSED or plicated — capsular deficiency is a recognised cause of IATROGENIC INSTABILITY and revision, and is one of the commonest findings at revision hip arthroscopy.
    • FEMORAL NECK FRACTURE after over-resection of the head-neck junction (>30% of the neck diameter) is a serious complication — protect weight-bearing and do not chase the last millimetre of cam.
    • Inadequate cam resection is the commonest reason for revision arthroscopy; over-resection is the most dangerous.
  • The one hip not to scope:
    • Do NOT perform arthroscopy in DYSPLASIA without addressing the coverage.
    • Isolated labral surgery in a dysplastic hip can WORSEN instability — the hypertrophied labrum is a secondary stabiliser doing necessary work.
    • A PERIACETABULAR OSTEOTOMY is the correct operation (with labral treatment as an adjunct).
    • Look for a lateral centre-edge angle <20°, an increased Tönnis (acetabular index) angle, and a break in Shenton's line before scoping any young hip.

THA bearing surfaces, fixation & implant survivorship

Why fixation choice changes with age, the two cemented stem philosophies, and what modern bearings have and have not solved.

6 of 10 sittings2017, 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2025

  • Why THAs fail, and when:
    • ASEPTIC LOOSENING remains the commonest LONG-TERM cause of failure overall.
    • But registry data show that in the first years, INSTABILITY/dislocation and INFECTION dominate EARLY revision.
    • And PERIPROSTHETIC FRACTURE is increasingly common in the elderly, now among the leading early revision causes with cementless stems.
    • In cemented stems, the classic failure sequence is DEBONDING at the STEM–CEMENT interface, then CEMENT MANTLE FRACTURE, then subsidence into varus with a distal pedestal.
  • Cemented versus cementless in the elderly:
    • Registry data — the UK NJR, the Canadian Joint Replacement Registry and the Nordic registries — consistently show that CEMENTED femoral fixation has a LOWER revision rate for PERIPROSTHETIC FRACTURE in patients over ~75, especially women.
    • Cementless stems have a HIGHER EARLY periprosthetic fracture risk in osteoporotic bone.
    • HYBRID fixation — a cementless cup with a cemented stem — has an excellent registry track record in the elderly and is the usual answer for a patient in their mid-eighties.
    • The Dorr classification of the proximal femur (A "champagne flute", B, C "stovepipe") guides the choice — a Dorr C femur in an elderly woman should be cemented.
  • The two cemented stem philosophies — a recurring exam point:
    • POLISHED, TAPERED, COLLARLESS ("force-closed" / taper-slip) stems are designed to SUBSIDE slightly within the cement mantle, converting shear into compressive HOOP STRESS in the cement and bone. They have excellent survivorship.
    • ROUGH / GRIT-BLASTED ("composite-beam") stems rely on bonding to the cement. If they debond, they generate ABRASIVE metal and cement debris, causing accelerated OSTEOLYSIS and higher failure rates.
    • So roughening a taper-slip design is catastrophic, and a grit-blasted stem that debonds does far worse than a polished one that subsides.
    • Both designs work when used as intended; the error is mixing the philosophies.
  • Improving cemented stem longevity — modern (3rd/4th generation) technique:
    • Pulsatile LAVAGE of the canal.
    • Distal canal PLUG.
    • RETROGRADE cement delivery with a gun.
    • PRESSURISATION.
    • VACUUM MIXING to reduce porosity.
    • A stem CENTRALISER, and a uniform mantle ≥2 mm.
    • Pressurisation and a clean, dry interface are the critical steps — everything else is secondary to interdigitation.
  • Bearing selection:
    • HIGHLY CROSS-LINKED POLYETHYLENE against a metal or ceramic head is the workhorse, and has dramatically reduced wear-related osteolysis — this is the single biggest advance in THA of the last 30 years.
    • CERAMIC-ON-CERAMIC has the lowest wear but risks fracture (rare with modern delta ceramic) and squeaking.
    • METAL-ON-METAL has been ABANDONED for THA because of ALTR and pseudotumour; metal-on-metal resurfacing is now used only in selected young men by experienced surgeons.
    • Volumetric wear increases with the SQUARE of the head radius; LINEAR wear does not.
    • Which is exactly why the wear advantage of highly cross-linked polyethylene is what permits the routine use of larger (36 mm) heads for stability — the two advances are linked.
  • Modularity — the trade-off:
    • It adds versatility — independent adjustment of offset, leg length and version.
    • But it introduces TAPER CORROSION (trunnionosis), and the risk of component dissociation and fretting.
    • Modular-NECK stems have a poor track record and several have been recalled.
    • Minimise unnecessary modularity; assemble tapers clean and dry, and avoid skirted or extra-long heads.
    • A practical aside patients ask about: modern implants are non-ferromagnetic and MRI-conditional, but they DO commonly trigger airport metal detectors — an implant card is not required and is not accepted as proof; reassure rather than document.
  • Uncemented hemiarthroplasty for a femoral neck fracture:
    • The commonest reason for revision is PERIPROSTHETIC FRACTURE.
    • Then acetabular erosion/protrusio, and infection.
    • This is a key argument for CEMENTED hemiarthroplasty in the elderly, supported by the WHiTE 5 randomised trial and by registry data.
    • The counter-argument — bone cement implantation syndrome — is real but is managed by technique, not by abandoning cement.

THA instability: Component position, spinopelvic motion & prevention

Why the safe zone is not safe, what a stiff spine does to a well-positioned cup, and the implant choices that actually buy stability.

5 of 10 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2025 (x2)

  • The classic "safe zone" — and why it is not enough:
    • Lewinnek: acetabular INCLINATION 40° ± 10° and ANTEVERSION 15° ± 10°.
    • Modern data show a substantial proportion of dislocating hips have components WITHIN this zone.
    • There is no single universal safe zone — target position must be individualised to the patient's spinopelvic mechanics.
    • Combined anteversion (cup plus stem, target ~25–50°) is a more useful concept than cup position alone.
  • Which malposition causes which direction of instability:
    • A RETROVERTED acetabular component predisposes to POSTERIOR instability — particularly in FLEXION: sitting, bending, rising from a chair — and to anterior impingement.
    • EXCESSIVE ANTEVERSION predisposes to ANTERIOR instability in EXTENSION and EXTERNAL ROTATION — the patient dislocates standing up and turning.
    • Excessive inclination (a "vertical" cup) reduces the jump distance and causes edge loading.
    • Femoral version matters equally — excessive femoral anteversion compounds a proverted cup.
  • SPINOPELVIC MECHANICS — the highest-yield modern concept:
    • Normally, when a patient SITS, the pelvis rotates POSTERIORLY (posterior pelvic tilt).
    • That functionally INCREASES acetabular anteversion and inclination, creating clearance for the flexing femur.
    • In a patient with a STIFF, degenerative or FUSED lumbar spine — especially a long fusion to the sacrum, or flatback deformity — the pelvis CANNOT rotate posteriorly when sitting.
    • So the cup remains relatively RETROVERTED / under-anteverted in flexion, the femoral neck impinges on the POSTERIOR rim, and the hip levers out POSTERIORLY.
  • The corollary, and how to measure it:
    • A stiff spine in a patient who STANDS with abnormal POSTERIOR pelvic tilt produces functional cup RETROVERSION in standing and ANTERIOR instability.
    • DECREASED SPINOPELVIC MOTION (a "stiff spine") is the key risk factor — patients with a lumbar fusion have a several-fold increased dislocation rate, rising with the number of levels fused.
    • Assess with STANDING and SEATED LATERAL radiographs, measuring pelvic incidence, sacral slope, and the CHANGE in sacral slope.
    • ΔSS <10° = a stiff spine. A pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis mismatch >10° identifies the deformed group.
    • In these patients, aim for more anteversion and inclination, and strongly consider dual mobility.
  • Jump distance:
    • Jump distance = the lateral displacement required for the head to dislocate.
    • It is a function of HEAD DIAMETER (a larger head = a greater jump distance) and of head coverage and cup design.
    • Increasing femoral HEAD SIZE is the single most effective IMPLANT-based strategy for increasing jump distance, range of motion before impingement, and stability.
    • Increasing OFFSET also improves soft-tissue tension WITHOUT increasing leg length — a critical distinction, because lengthening to gain stability is poorly tolerated.
    • A lateralised (offset) liner and a high-offset stem both act on tension rather than on jump distance.
  • Primary arc of motion:
    • The motion available before NECK-ON-LINER impingement.
    • Increased by a larger HEAD-TO-NECK RATIO — a bigger head, a thinner or trapezoidal neck, and a SKIRTLESS head.
    • A SKIRTED head REDUCES the head-neck ratio and REDUCES the arc — which is why skirted (extra-long) heads should be avoided, and why they also worsen trunnionosis.
    • Once the neck impinges, further motion levers the head out — impingement is the first step of every dislocation.
  • The rest of the prevention strategy:
    • Restore OFFSET and LEG LENGTH.
    • Avoid elevated-rim liners in the wrong orientation — they add stability in one direction and cause impingement in the other.
    • Repair the POSTERIOR CAPSULE and SHORT EXTERNAL ROTATORS in a posterior approach — this alone reduces dislocation SEVERAL-FOLD and is the highest-value technical step in that approach.
    • Use DUAL-MOBILITY or CONSTRAINED components in high-risk patients: neuromuscular disease, cognitive impairment, abductor deficiency, spinal fusion, and revision surgery.
    • Dual mobility is preferred over a constrained liner where possible — a constrained liner transfers the force to the bone-implant interface and loosens.
    • Intra-operative assessment: check stability in flexion/adduction/internal rotation and in extension/external rotation, and confirm there is no impingement through that arc.
  • Hip ankylosis converted to THA:
    • The commonest reason for later revision is INSTABILITY/dislocation and ABDUCTOR INSUFFICIENCY.
    • Because the abductors have been non-functional for years and do NOT recover.
    • Consider a DUAL-MOBILITY or constrained liner at the index procedure.
    • Counsel that pain relief is reliable but a limp is likely to persist.

THA approaches & perioperative considerations

What each approach trades away, and the modifiable patient factors that matter more than the approach does.

7 of 10 sittings2018, 2019 (x4), 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Direct anterior approach:
    • Internervous between SARTORIUS and RECTUS FEMORIS (femoral nerve) and TENSOR FASCIA LATA (superior gluteal nerve).
    • Advantages: a lower early dislocation rate, and faster early recovery in some series (the difference largely disappears by 3–6 months).
    • Disadvantages: LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS NERVE injury is very common — up to 15–80% report some numbness.
    • A steep learning curve, with higher early PERIPROSTHETIC FEMORAL FRACTURE and WOUND complication rates, particularly in the obese (the transverse skin crease is a moist, high-tension area).
    • Difficult femoral exposure, especially for a revision or a stiff hip.
    • It is NOT a "muscle-sparing" approach in the sense of avoiding all muscle damage — the TFL is frequently injured by retraction.
  • Posterior approach:
    • The workhorse — extensile, with excellent femoral exposure, and it spares the abductors entirely.
    • Historically higher dislocation rates — largely mitigated by POSTERIOR CAPSULAR and SHORT EXTERNAL ROTATOR REPAIR, which reduces dislocation several-fold.
    • The SCIATIC nerve is at risk — avoid excessive retraction on the posterior column and keep the hip extended and the knee flexed during retraction.
    • It is the approach of choice for most revisions and for complex primaries.
  • Direct lateral (Hardinge):
    • Lower dislocation rate than the classic posterior approach.
    • But a risk of SUPERIOR GLUTEAL NERVE injury and abductor deficiency with a TRENDELENBURG gait.
    • Keep the abductor split within 5 cm of the tip of the greater trochanter — the superior gluteal nerve runs transversely above that.
    • Persistent limp and abductor weakness are the characteristic long-term complaint.
  • Smoking — the single most important modifiable risk factor:
    • It increases wound complications, deep infection, periprosthetic joint infection, delayed union and nonunion, medical complications, and revision after both THA and TKA.
    • Cessation for at least 4–8 weeks pre-operatively measurably reduces complications.
    • This is one of the most valuable interventions a surgeon can make — and the counselling opportunity of a planned elective operation is unusually powerful.
    • Nicotine replacement is acceptable; the harm is from combustion products and nicotine-induced vasoconstriction, so cessation matters more than substitution.
  • Obesity:
    • BMI >40 is associated with higher rates of infection, wound complications, dislocation, component malposition and reoperation, and lower ABSOLUTE functional scores.
    • But obese patients still achieve SUBSTANTIAL IMPROVEMENT in pain and function — the change from baseline is comparable.
    • So obesity is NOT an absolute contraindication.
    • Most guidelines recommend optimisation and shared decision-making rather than an arbitrary BMI cut-off — a hard BMI threshold denies a real benefit to patients who would gain from it.
    • Optimise what is modifiable: glycaemic control, nutrition (obese patients are frequently malnourished), smoking, and S. aureus decolonisation.
  • Return to driving:
    • The dominant determinant is restoration of BRAKE REACTION TIME.
    • Influenced most by the SIDE operated on (right-sided procedures take longer), by pain, and by the use of OPIOID analgesia.
    • Most patients recover baseline brake reaction time by ~4 weeks after THA, and 4–6 weeks after a right TKA.
    • Left-sided procedures in an automatic vehicle allow earlier return — often within 1–2 weeks.
    • The patient must also be off opioids and able to perform an emergency stop; advise them to check their insurance.
  • The geriatric patient:
    • Age alone is NOT a contraindication to THA.
    • Elderly patients report excellent pain relief and satisfaction, and the operation is highly cost-effective even late in life.
    • The priorities are medical optimisation, fixation appropriate to bone quality (a CEMENTED stem), and minimising dislocation risk (a larger head or dual mobility).
    • Add delirium prevention, early mobilisation and a fracture-liaison referral if the indication was a fragility fracture.

TKA: Alignment philosophy & gap balancing

Mechanical versus kinematic alignment, measured resection versus gap balancing, and the grid for fixing an unbalanced knee.

6 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2024S (x2)

  • The alignment philosophies:
    • MECHANICAL alignment targets a neutral hip-knee-ankle axis (0° ± 3°), with the femoral component perpendicular to the femoral mechanical axis and the tibial cut perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis.
    • KINEMATIC alignment reproduces the patient's pre-arthritic joint lines and obliquity.
    • ANATOMIC alignment sets the joint line in ~3° of varus.
    • Evidence to date shows comparable or modestly better EARLY patient-reported outcomes with kinematic alignment, with NO proven difference in SURVIVORSHIP.
    • Mechanical alignment remains the reference standard with the longest track record, and outliers (>3° varus) are associated with higher failure in some series.
  • Measured resection versus gap balancing:
    • MEASURED RESECTION sets bone cuts from ANATOMIC LANDMARKS — the transepicondylar axis, the posterior condylar axis, and Whiteside's line — and then balances soft tissue.
    • GAP BALANCING sets FEMORAL ROTATION from the TENSION OF THE LIGAMENTS in flexion.
    • Measured resection better restores the JOINT LINE and PATELLOFEMORAL TRACKING.
    • Gap balancing more reliably produces a rectangular, balanced FLEXION GAP — but risks JOINT LINE ELEVATION and femoral component MALROTATION if the ligaments are unbalanced at the time rotation is set.
    • Most surgeons use a hybrid: landmarks to set rotation, then check and adjust with tensioned gaps.
  • The gap-imbalance grid — memorise this:
    • TIGHT in FLEXION, LOOSE in EXTENSION → the femoral component is too anterior or too small, or the distal femoral cut is excessive. Correct by UPSIZING the femoral component and/or translating it POSTERIORLY, or by augmenting the distal femur.
    • TIGHT in EXTENSION, LOOSE in FLEXION → resect MORE DISTAL FEMUR (this raises the joint line) and/or downsize / translate the femur ANTERIORLY; release posterior osteophytes and the posterior capsule.
    • TIGHT in BOTH → cut more TIBIA — a tibial cut affects both gaps equally.
    • LOOSE in BOTH → increase POLYETHYLENE thickness — a thicker insert also affects both gaps equally.
    • BALANCED in extension, LOOSE in FLEXION → UPSIZE the femoral component and translate it POSTERIORLY (or use a posterior-augmented femur). Increasing the polyethylene thickness alone would OVER-TIGHTEN extension, and increasing tibial slope would WORSEN flexion laxity.
    • The organising principle: the TIBIA and the POLY affect BOTH gaps; the FEMUR affects them SEPARATELY — distal femur for extension, posterior femur (size and translation) for flexion.
  • Which structure controls which gap:
    • Release of the POPLITEUS TENDON significantly increases the LATERAL FLEXION GAP — the classic "which structure controls the lateral flexion gap" answer.
    • The LCL controls the LATERAL gap in BOTH flexion and extension.
    • The posterolateral capsule and iliotibial band control the LATERAL EXTENSION gap.
    • The SUPERFICIAL MCL controls the MEDIAL gap in BOTH flexion and extension.
    • The posteromedial capsule and posterior oblique ligament control the MEDIAL EXTENSION gap.
    • Corollary: to open the lateral gap in flexion only, release popliteus; to open it in both, release the LCL.
  • Tibial slope:
    • Increasing tibial slope OPENS the FLEXION gap and increases posterior femoral rollback.
    • Excessive slope in a CRUCIATE-RETAINING knee causes FLEXION INSTABILITY and anterior tibial subluxation.
    • It has essentially no effect on the extension gap — which is what makes it a flexion-gap-only tool.
    • In a posterior-stabilised knee, excess slope risks cam-post dislodgement; too little slope limits flexion.
  • The varus knee — the release sequence:
    • OSTEOPHYTE removal FIRST — osteophytes are frequently the cause of the apparent contracture, and removing them alone often balances the knee.
    • Then the deep MCL and anteromedial capsule.
    • Then the posteromedial capsule and semimembranosus — for EXTENSION tightness.
    • Then the superficial MCL — POSTERIOR fibres for extension, ANTERIOR fibres for flexion.
    • Release incrementally and re-check; a complete superficial MCL release destabilises the knee and commits you to a constrained implant.
  • The valgus knee — the mirror problem:
    • Release sequence: osteophytes, then the posterolateral capsule and iliotibial band (for extension tightness), then popliteus and the LCL (for flexion tightness).
    • Beware the hypoplastic lateral femoral condyle, which causes internal rotation of the femoral component if posterior referencing is used — use the transepicondylar axis or Whiteside's line instead.
    • The peroneal nerve is at risk in the severe valgus knee with a flexion contracture; document its function post-operatively, and flex the knee if a palsy appears.

Periprosthetic fracture around hip & knee

Every decision turns on one question — is the implant loose? — and the commonest error is answering it wrong.

4 of 10 sittings2021, 2023, 2024S, 2024F (x2)

  • Vancouver classification (femur around a THA):
    • A — TROCHANTERIC: AG greater trochanter, AL lesser trochanter.
    • B — AROUND THE STEM: B1 well-fixed stem; B2 LOOSE stem with adequate bone stock; B3 LOOSE stem with poor or deficient bone stock.
    • C — DISTAL to the stem tip.
    • The classification is only useful because it maps directly onto treatment.
  • Treatment by type:
    • B1 → ORIF — a locking plate with cables ± a strut allograft.
    • B2 → REVISION to a long, DISTALLY FIXED stem — usually a tapered fluted modular stem — ± ORIF of the fracture.
    • B3 → REVISION with a PROXIMAL FEMORAL REPLACEMENT, an allograft-prosthetic composite, or an extensively porous-coated long stem in the younger patient.
    • C → ORIF with a plate SPANNING the stem — overlap the stem to avoid creating a stress riser between plate and implant.
    • A → usually non-operative, unless displaced with abductor dysfunction or associated with osteolysis (which needs addressing).
  • The single most important determination:
    • Is the STEM LOOSE?
    • A stem that appears well fixed on plain films may be loose — review pre-operative and serial radiographs for SUBSIDENCE and LUCENCY, and ask about pre-fracture thigh pain.
    • TEST THE STEM INTRA-OPERATIVELY if there is any doubt.
    • Misclassifying a B2 as a B1 and PLATING A LOOSE STEM is the classic error and leads to failure — always confirm stem fixation before choosing fixation over revision.
  • Periprosthetic fracture around a TKA — distal femur (Rorabeck):
    • Type I — undisplaced with an intact prosthesis.
    • Type II — displaced with an intact prosthesis.
    • Type III — fracture with a LOOSE or FAILED prosthesis.
    • Types I and II → ORIF with a lateral locking plate, or a RETROGRADE intramedullary nail — confirm the femoral component is "nail-compatible" with an OPEN BOX before planning a nail.
    • Type III → REVISION arthroplasty, usually with a distal femoral replacement in the elderly.
  • A terminology note:
    • "Vancouver B2" applied to a KNEE is a misuse of the classification, but it is used colloquially to mean a periprosthetic fracture with a LOOSE component.
    • The answer in that situation is the same regardless of the label: REVISION arthroplasty, not fixation.
  • The geriatric distal femur periprosthetic fracture — fix or replace:
    • Dual plating or a nail-plate combination versus a DISTAL FEMORAL REPLACEMENT (DFR).
    • DFR allows IMMEDIATE weight-bearing and avoids nonunion, and is favoured for a loose component, severe comminution or bone loss, or very poor bone.
    • But it has higher INFECTION and EXTENSOR MECHANISM complication rates, and is a bigger operation with more blood loss.
    • Fixation preserves bone stock — which matters in anyone likely to need a future revision.
    • Recent comparative data show similar mortality with faster mobilisation after DFR.
  • Periprosthetic patella fracture:
    • Most are treated NON-OPERATIVELY if the extensor mechanism is intact and the component is well fixed.
    • Because the results of fixation are poor, with high complication rates — the patella is small, osteopenic and devascularised by the arthroplasty.
    • Surgery is for EXTENSOR MECHANISM DISRUPTION, or a LOOSE patellar component.
    • And even then, treatment is usually component REMOVAL with patelloplasty rather than revision of the button.
  • Nonunion after periprosthetic ORIF:
    • A painful, mobile fracture at ~4 months with a negative infection work-up is a nonunion.
    • Treat with revision fixation plus BONE GRAFT in a patient with reasonable bone and demand.
    • Or conversion to a DISTAL FEMORAL REPLACEMENT in an elderly, low-demand patient, which converts a healing problem into a mechanical solution.
    • Always exclude infection before revising a nonunion — aspirate, and send tissue at the time of surgery.

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty & conversion to TKA

Selection criteria old and new, why mobile bearings fail early, and which conversion is the harder operation.

4 of 10 sittings2020, 2021 (x2), 2023, 2025

  • The classic indications (Kozinn & Scott) — and how they have been liberalised:
    • Isolated MEDIAL (or lateral) compartment arthritis.
    • An intact ACL.
    • A CORRECTABLE deformity <10–15° of varus.
    • A flexion contracture <10–15°, and a functional range of motion.
    • Modern practice (the Oxford group) has RELAXED the age, weight and patellofemoral criteria — ANTEROMEDIAL osteoarthritis with a functionally intact ACL is the key indication.
    • Inflammatory arthritis and ACL DEFICIENCY remain contraindications — ACL deficiency particularly for MOBILE-bearing designs, where it causes bearing dislocation and early failure.
  • Why "anteromedial osteoarthritis" is the phrase that matters:
    • It describes a specific pattern: full-thickness cartilage loss ANTEROMEDIALLY with preserved cartilage POSTERIORLY on the tibia and a preserved, functionally intact ACL and MCL.
    • Because the posterior cartilage is preserved, the deformity corrects fully in flexion and the MCL is not contracted — so it does not need to be released.
    • A fixed varus deformity that does not correct means the MCL is contracted and the pattern is wrong for a UKA.
    • Lateral compartment full-thickness loss on a valgus stress view is the other exclusion.
  • How UKAs fail — and it depends on the bearing:
    • The commonest cause of EARLY failure (<5 years) of a MOBILE-bearing MEDIAL UKA is BEARING DISLOCATION.
    • Then progression of arthritis in the other compartments, aseptic loosening, and unexplained pain.
    • In FIXED-bearing designs, early failure is more often ASEPTIC LOOSENING and progression of disease rather than dislocation.
    • LATER failures across designs are dominated by PROGRESSION OF ARTHRITIS in the lateral or patellofemoral compartment, and by ASEPTIC LOOSENING.
    • "Unexplained pain" is a genuine and frequent revision indication in UKA — and is part of why the revision rate is higher.
  • Converting a UKA to a TKA:
    • Results are generally WORSE than a primary TKA but BETTER than a revision TKA.
    • With the need for augments, stems or bone graft in ~25–75% of cases, depending on the failure mode.
    • The TIBIAL side is the problem — tibial bone loss beneath the component often requires a metal augment or graft and a stemmed component.
    • Plan for it: have augments and stems available for any UKA revision.
  • HTO-to-TKA versus UKA-to-TKA — a repeated comparison:
    • Conversion of a HIGH TIBIAL OSTEOTOMY to TKA is MORE technically demanding.
    • Because of PATELLA BAJA, altered proximal tibial anatomy and metaphyseal deformity, retained hardware, scarring, and a compromised lateral soft-tissue sleeve.
    • And it is associated with worse outcomes and higher complication rates than conversion of a UKA to TKA.
    • UKA is generally the EASIER conversion — which is a legitimate argument in the UKA-versus-HTO decision in a middle-aged patient.
  • What the registries show:
    • UKA has a HIGHER revision rate than TKA.
    • But BETTER function, faster recovery, lower perioperative morbidity and MORTALITY, and lower complication rates.
    • Much of the revision-rate difference is attributable to a LOWER THRESHOLD for revising a UKA — revising a UKA is an easier decision than revising a TKA, so it happens sooner.
    • High-volume surgeons achieve markedly better UKA survivorship — the "USAGE EFFECT": surgeons for whom UKA is >20% of their knee practice have substantially better results.
    • So the honest counselling is: quicker recovery and a better-feeling knee, at the cost of a higher chance of needing something further.
  • Lateral UKA:
    • Technically different — the lateral compartment kinematics differ (greater femoral rollback and translation).
    • A FIXED bearing is preferred, because of a high MOBILE-bearing dislocation rate laterally.
    • It is much less commonly performed, and is best done by surgeons with a UKA practice.
    • Valgus deformity from lateral compartment loss must also be passively correctable, with an intact medial sleeve.

THA complications: Nerve injury, HO & psoas impingement

The nerves and vessels at risk, the acetabular screw quadrants, and the causes of persistent groin pain after a technically sound hip.

3 of 10 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2024F

  • Nerve injury after THA:
    • Roughly 1% in primary THA, and higher in revision, DDH and lengthening.
    • The SCIATIC nerve is the most commonly injured, and the PERONEAL DIVISION is the most vulnerable.
    • Why: it is more LATERAL, more TETHERED at the sciatic notch and the fibular neck, and has LESS protective connective tissue and fewer, larger fascicles.
    • Risk factors: developmental dysplasia, revision surgery, posterior approach, lengthening >2–4 cm, female sex, and direct retractor pressure.
    • The FEMORAL nerve is injured with anterior retractors over the acetabular rim, and the LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS nerve with the direct anterior approach.
  • Managing a post-operative nerve palsy:
    • A palsy of unknown cause should be treated by FLEXING THE KNEE and EXTENDING THE HIP to detension the sciatic nerve, and OBSERVING.
    • Most are neurapraxic and recover at least partially — recovery of the peroneal division is less complete than the tibial.
    • Explore only if there is a suspected HAEMATOMA (an expanding, painful swelling, particularly in an anticoagulated patient) or a known intra-operative event.
    • If lengthening is the suspected cause and the palsy is complete, consider early revision to shorten — though evidence is limited.
    • Provide an ankle-foot orthosis, document serially, and obtain EMG at ~6 weeks for a baseline.
  • Acetabular screw safe zones (the Wasielewski quadrant system):
    • Divide the acetabulum by a line from the ASIS through the centre of the acetabulum, and a perpendicular line through the centre.
    • POSTEROSUPERIOR quadrant — the SAFEST: thickest bone, no major structures.
    • POSTEROINFERIOR — safe with SHORT screws; the sciatic nerve and inferior gluteal vessels lie beyond.
    • ANTEROSUPERIOR — the MOST DANGEROUS: the EXTERNAL ILIAC ARTERY and VEIN lie immediately adjacent. This is the answer to "what is at risk with a screw in the anterosuperior quadrant".
    • ANTEROINFERIOR — risks the OBTURATOR neurovascular bundle.
    • The practical rule: put screws in the posterior half, and keep them short anteriorly if you must.
  • Iliopsoas impingement / tendonitis after THA:
    • ANTERIOR GROIN PAIN worse with RESISTED HIP FLEXION, with an active straight-leg raise, and with rising from a chair or climbing stairs.
    • Presenting months to years after surgery in a hip that otherwise looks and feels well.
    • Radiographs show an anteriorly PROMINENT or UNCOVERED acetabular component rim — or a prominent screw or extruded cement.
    • Diagnosis is supported by a CT measurement of anterior cup prominence, and confirmed by an image-guided LOCAL ANAESTHETIC INJECTION into the iliopsoas sheath.
    • Always exclude infection and loosening first — groin pain after a THA is periprosthetic joint infection until proven otherwise.
  • Treating psoas impingement:
    • Physiotherapy and injection first — a proportion settle.
    • Then either arthroscopic or open ILIOPSOAS TENOTOMY/release — simpler, but causes measurable hip FLEXION WEAKNESS (usually recovering by a year, but not always).
    • Or ACETABULAR COMPONENT REVISION — it addresses the cause, and is preferred where the cup is malpositioned or the patient is young.
    • Tenotomy has good results when the cup is otherwise well positioned and the prominence is minor.
  • Isolated liner and head exchange for eccentric wear with osteolysis:
    • The shell is retained if it is well fixed and well positioned — which spares a difficult acetabular revision.
    • The most likely complication is INSTABILITY/dislocation.
    • Because the new liner may not restore jump distance and offset, the capsule has been re-opened, and soft-tissue tension may be altered.
    • Mitigate by upsizing the head where the shell allows, using a lipped liner appropriately, and repairing the capsule.
    • Also consider incomplete débridement of the osteolytic lesion (graft it through screw holes or a cortical window) and damage to the locking mechanism — have a full revision set available.
  • Heterotopic ossification after THA:
    • Common radiographically — up to 30% — but USUALLY ASYMPTOMATIC.
    • Only a minority (~5–10%) have functionally significant stiffness.
    • Risk factors: male sex, ankylosing spondylitis, DISH, previous HO, hypertrophic OA, and extensive muscle dissection.
    • Prophylaxis with single-dose radiation or indomethacin is reserved for HIGH-RISK patients, not used routinely.
    • It does NOT reliably resolve within 6 months — the classic FALSE statement. Mature HO persists; only excision removes it, and that must wait for maturity.
    • Graded by the Brooker classification (I islands of bone → IV apparent ankylosis).

Expectations, satisfaction and the influence of BMI.

  • Dissatisfaction after TKA — the number to know:
    • Approximately 15–20% of TKA patients are NOT satisfied with their outcome — a substantially higher dissatisfaction rate than THA.
    • The strongest predictors are UNMET EXPECTATIONS, pre-operative pain catastrophising and depression, MINIMAL radiographic arthritis, and persistent pain from a NON-KNEE source (hip, spine, neuropathy).
    • Managing expectations pre-operatively is the highest-yield intervention — higher yield than any technical or implant choice.
  • Obesity and TKA:
    • Obese patients have higher rates of infection, wound complications, medical complications, component malposition and revision, and lower ABSOLUTE post-operative scores.
    • But they achieve SIMILAR or GREATER ABSOLUTE IMPROVEMENT in pain and function, from a worse baseline.
    • So obesity is NOT an absolute contraindication — guidelines favour optimisation and shared decision-making rather than a hard BMI cut-off.
    • Bariatric surgery before TKA has NOT consistently improved arthroplasty outcomes — a useful negative finding, and a reason not to mandate it.
  • The practical case — a high BMI with a small varus deformity:
    • Proceed with a STANDARD PRIMARY TKA using careful soft-tissue balancing — the small varus is easily corrected.
    • The high BMI increases perioperative risk that requires OPTIMISATION: glycaemic control, smoking cessation, nutritional assessment, weight-loss counselling, and S. aureus decolonisation.
    • It does NOT call for a different implant — constraint is dictated by ligament status, not by body weight.
  • Radiographs versus symptoms:
    • Radiographic severity correlates POORLY with symptoms.
    • Operating on a patient with MINIMAL radiographic arthritis is one of the strongest predictors of a POOR outcome.
    • If the radiograph is nearly normal, look elsewhere for the pain generator before offering an arthroplasty.

    • Pain relief is excellent, but a "FORGOTTEN KNEE" is uncommon — most patients remain aware of the knee.
    • Kneeling is difficult for most.
    • Expected implant survivorship is >90–95% at 15 years.
    • Full recovery takes up to a year, with the biggest gains in the first 3 months.
    • Numbness lateral to the scar (infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve) is near-universal and permanent.
  • Extensor mechanism disruption after TKA:
    • A devastating complication, particularly in a rheumatoid patient or after multiple revisions.
    • PRIMARY REPAIR ALONE FAILS AT A HIGH RATE.
    • Reconstruction with an EXTENSOR MECHANISM ALLOGRAFT, or SYNTHETIC MESH with a GASTROCNEMIUS FLAP, is generally required.
    • Tension the graft in full extension — under-tensioning is the commonest cause of failure — and protect it in an extension brace for 6–12 weeks.

Hip osteoarthritis: Aetiology & natural history

What causes hip arthritis and what does not.

3 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019, 2022

  • "Primary" hip osteoarthritis is largely secondary:
    • It is now understood to be substantially SECONDARY to subtle MORPHOLOGIC abnormality.
    • CAM and PINCER femoroacetabular impingement, ACETABULAR DYSPLASIA, and POST-SLIP or PERTHES deformity account for the majority of "idiopathic" hip OA in younger patients.
    • Which is the entire rationale for hip preservation surgery — treat the morphology before the cartilage fails.
  • Established causes:
    • Developmental dysplasia of the hip.
    • FAI — cam more strongly than pincer.
    • SCFE and Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease.
    • Osteonecrosis.
    • Post-traumatic incongruity — particularly after an acetabular fracture.
    • Inflammatory arthropathy and septic arthritis.
    • Haemochromatosis (and ochronosis) and the epiphyseal dysplasias.
  • Risk factors:
    • Obesity, age and female sex — though the relationship of obesity with HIP OA is WEAKER than for KNEE OA.
    • Occupational heavy lifting and farming are recognised occupational associations.
    • Previous hip injury, and a family history (there is a real heritable component).
  • The odd one out among the classic pre-arthritic deformities:
    • RECREATIONAL RUNNING is NOT associated with an increased risk of hip osteoarthritis.
    • Several cohort studies suggest recreational runners have a LOWER prevalence than sedentary controls.
    • Elite or competitive running at very high volumes MAY increase risk — the distinction matters.
    • The likely explanation is cyclical loading maintaining cartilage health, plus lower BMI in runners.
  • Radiographs and symptoms:
    • Radiographic OA and symptoms correlate imperfectly.
    • The presence of hip PAIN with radiographic changes — not the radiograph alone — drives management.
    • Confirm the hip is the source: groin pain, a positive FADIR or log-roll, and relief with an intra-articular local anaesthetic injection.
  • Non-operative management — and one caution:
    • Activity modification, weight loss, physiotherapy, NSAIDs and intra-articular injection.
    • Intra-articular CORTICOSTEROID injection into the HIP has been associated in some series with RAPIDLY PROGRESSIVE OSTEOARTHRITIS and OSTEONECROSIS.
    • And with increased infection risk if arthroplasty follows within ~3 months.
    • Both are worth counselling before injecting a hip, and are a reason to space an injection well before planned surgery.

Inflammatory arthritis & the arthritic joint

Managing the inflamed joint in inflammatory arthropathy.

2 of 10 sittings2019, 2020

  • The persistently inflamed rheumatoid knee:
    • A patient with known rheumatoid arthritis and a persistently painful, swollen knee that has failed high-dose oral corticosteroids and intra-articular injection, with only mild radiographic changes, has PERSISTENT PROLIFERATIVE SYNOVITIS.
    • The next step is SYNOVECTOMY (arthroscopic or open) after optimising medical therapy.
    • And, critically, after EXCLUDING SEPTIC ARTHRITIS — which must always be ruled out in a single inflamed joint.
  • Always aspirate a single acutely inflamed joint:
    • Particularly in an immunosuppressed patient on biologics.
    • Send cell count, Gram stain, culture and crystals.
    • Inflammatory arthritis and infection can COEXIST.
    • Biologic therapy BLUNTS the systemic inflammatory response — the patient may be afebrile with a modest CRP and still be septic.
  • What synovectomy achieves — and does not:
    • It provides good short-to-medium-term pain relief and reduces synovitis.
    • But it does NOT alter the progression of joint destruction.
    • Symptoms recur in a substantial proportion by 5 years.
    • Radiosynovectomy is an alternative in some centres.
    • It is a TEMPORISING measure in a joint with preserved cartilage — not a definitive treatment.
  • Medical management dominates modern outcomes:
    • Early aggressive DMARD and biologic therapy has DRAMATICALLY REDUCED the need for rheumatoid joint surgery over the last two decades.
    • Perioperative management: CONTINUE conventional synthetic DMARDs.
    • HOLD biologics, scheduling surgery at the END of the dosing cycle, and restarting at ~14 days post-operatively once the wound is satisfactory.
    • Hold JAK inhibitors for 3 days; taper glucocorticoids to the lowest feasible dose.
  • Special considerations in inflammatory arthritis:
    • CERVICAL SPINE INSTABILITY — screen with flexion-extension radiographs before any general anaesthetic.
    • Poor bone quality and fragile skin.
    • Higher infection risk.
    • Multiple joint involvement requiring a STAGED surgical plan: generally LOWER limb before UPPER limb; HIP before KNEE; and address the joint causing the most disability first.
    • Also assess the feet — forefoot deformity is often the biggest limitation to mobility after a successful hip or knee.
  • Arthroplasty in inflammatory arthritis:
    • Outcomes are GOOD — pain relief is reliable and satisfaction is high.
    • But with higher rates of INFECTION, PERIPROSTHETIC FRACTURE, and — in the shoulder and elbow — component LOOSENING.
    • Plan for poor bone stock, and have augments and stems available.

Mechanical axis, limb alignment & deformity planning

Definitions of the mechanical and anatomic axes.

3 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2025

  • The mechanical axis of the FEMUR — the definition:
    • The line from the CENTRE OF THE FEMORAL HEAD to the CENTRE OF THE KNEE (the centre of the intercondylar notch / femoral condyles).
    • This is the standard definition and a recurring single-best-answer item.
    • It is NOT a line down the shaft, and it is NOT from the greater trochanter.
  • Mechanical versus anatomic axis:
    • The ANATOMIC axis of the femur is the line down the MID-DIAPHYSIS.
    • The angle between the mechanical and anatomic axes of the femur is ~5–7°.
    • Which is the basis for the 5–6° VALGUS distal femoral cut in TKA — you cut relative to the anatomic (intramedullary) axis to end up perpendicular to the mechanical axis.
    • In the TIBIA the mechanical and anatomic axes are essentially the SAME — which is why the tibial cut is made perpendicular to the shaft.
  • The overall limb axis:
    • The mechanical axis of the LIMB (hip–knee–ankle axis) runs from the CENTRE OF THE FEMORAL HEAD to the CENTRE OF THE ANKLE.
    • Normally it passes just MEDIAL to the centre of the knee (~1.2° of varus).
    • The MECHANICAL AXIS DEVIATION is measured from this line to the centre of the knee.
  • Joint orientation angles for deformity planning:
    • mLDFA (mechanical lateral distal femoral angle) ~85–90°.
    • MPTA (medial proximal tibial angle) ~85–90°.
    • LDTA (lateral distal tibial angle) ~86–92°.
    • Joint line convergence angle ~0–2°.
    • Deviations localise the deformity to the FEMUR, the TIBIA or the JOINT LINE — which is the whole point of measuring them, because you must osteotomise where the deformity is.
  • CORA — the planning concept:
    • The CENTRE OF ROTATION OF ANGULATION is the INTERSECTION of the proximal and distal axis lines.
    • An osteotomy performed AT the CORA corrects the angulation WITHOUT creating translation.
    • An osteotomy AWAY from the CORA requires TRANSLATION to avoid creating a secondary deformity — a zig-zag limb that is straight on paper but not anatomically.
    • Always identify the CORA before choosing the osteotomy level.
  • Getting the radiograph right:
    • Long-standing (hip-to-ankle) WEIGHT-BEARING radiographs are required for accurate assessment.
    • ROTATION and FLEXION of the limb substantially alter the apparent alignment — a flexed or externally rotated limb reads as false valgus or varus.
    • Position with the patellae facing forward, and check the film before measuring.
    • Assess rotational profile separately with CT if a torsional deformity is suspected.

Metal-on-metal bearings & adverse local tissue reaction

Investigating and managing the painful metal-on-metal hip.

1 of 10 sittings2023

  • The mechanism:
    • Metal-on-metal (MoM) bearings generate NANOMETRE-sized wear particles and COBALT/CHROMIUM IONS.
    • These produce an ADVERSE LOCAL TISSUE REACTION (ALTR/ALVAL) — a lymphocyte-dominated immune response with tissue NECROSIS, effusion and PSEUDOTUMOUR formation.
    • It can destroy the ABDUCTORS and bone, which is what makes it so damaging.
    • Volumetric wear is LOW but particle NUMBER is enormous — the mechanistic point, and the reason a "low-wear" bearing caused so much harm.
  • Risk factors for ALTR:
    • Small component/head size (<50 mm) in resurfacing.
    • High acetabular inclination (>50–55°) with EDGE LOADING.
    • Female sex and dysplasia.
    • Specific implant designs — large-head MoM THA in particular.
    • MODULAR TAPER CORROSION produces an IDENTICAL picture even with a NON-MoM bearing — so a ceramic-on-poly hip can present the same way.
  • Working up a painful MoM hip:
    • FIRST EXCLUDE INFECTION — ESR, CRP, and aspiration with cell count.
    • Note that a MoM effusion can give FALSELY ELEVATED AUTOMATED cell counts (metal debris is counted as cells), so a MANUAL count is needed.
    • Then SERUM COBALT and CHROMIUM levels.
    • MARS (metal artefact reduction sequence) MRI or ultrasound, to identify a pseudotumour and assess the abductors.
    • Plain films to assess component position and osteolysis.
  • Interpreting metal ion levels:
    • Thresholds are guidance rather than absolutes.
    • Cobalt or chromium >7 µg/L (ppb) is widely used as a concerning level (UK MHRA), with higher levels and RISING TRENDS more significant than a single value.
    • Metal levels ALONE do not mandate revision.
    • SYMPTOMS plus a SOFT-TISSUE REACTION do.
    • A Co:Cr ratio >2 suggests taper corrosion rather than bearing wear.
  • Indications for revision:
    • Pain with a pseudotumour or ALTR on imaging.
    • Progressive soft-tissue or bone destruction.
    • Abductor compromise.
    • Markedly elevated and RISING metal ions.
    • Component malposition.
    • Revision should NOT be delayed once soft-tissue destruction is evident — abductor loss produces a poor outcome and instability, and it does not recover.
  • At revision:
    • Excise the pseudotumour and necrotic tissue.
    • Convert to a CERAMIC-ON-POLYETHYLENE bearing.
    • Be prepared for ABDUCTOR DEFICIENCY — consider dual mobility or constrained components.
    • Outcomes are worse than a routine revision, with high rates of dislocation and persistent weakness.
    • Warn the patient before surgery that the pain may improve but the limp may not.
  • The asymptomatic patient:
    • Asymptomatic patients with MoM implants require ONGOING SURVEILLANCE.
    • That means symptoms, examination, radiographs and — for higher-risk designs — metal ions and cross-sectional imaging.
    • Health Canada, the MHRA and the FDA all publish surveillance guidance; the frequency depends on the implant and the ion trend.

Osteonecrosis & hip preservation

Femoral head osteonecrosis: causes, staging and treatment.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2019, 2025

  • Causes of osteonecrosis of the femoral head:
    • CORTICOSTEROIDS and ALCOHOL are the two commonest ATRAUMATIC causes.
    • Sickle cell disease and Gaucher disease.
    • Caisson disease / dysbarism.
    • Systemic lupus erythematosus.
    • HIV and antiretroviral therapy.
    • Chemotherapy and radiation.
    • Post-traumatic — femoral neck fracture, hip dislocation, SCFE.
    • A substantial proportion are idiopathic. Roughly half are bilateral, so image both hips.
  • Imaging:
    • MRI is the most sensitive and specific investigation, and detects disease long before radiographic change.
    • The classic finding is the "DOUBLE-LINE SIGN" on T2 — a hyperintense inner GRANULATION line and a hypointense outer SCLEROTIC line — with a serpiginous margin.
    • Radiographs are normal in early disease; a bone scan is less sensitive than MRI and cannot stage.
    • Image both hips even when only one is symptomatic.
  • Ficat / ARCO staging:
    • Stage I — normal radiograph, abnormal MRI.
    • Stage II — sclerosis and cysts WITHOUT collapse.
    • Stage III — SUBCHONDRAL COLLAPSE — the "CRESCENT SIGN" — with a preserved spherical head.
    • Stage IV — femoral head FLATTENING with acetabular involvement and arthritis.
    • The CRESCENT SIGN marks the transition to IRREVERSIBLE disease and is the key prognostic threshold — everything before it may be salvageable, everything after it is not.
    • Lesion size and location (the modified Kerboul angle; medial and small lesions do better) predict collapse as strongly as stage.
  • Pre-collapse treatment:
    • Stage I–II with a SMALL lesion → joint-preserving surgery, most commonly CORE DECOMPRESSION.
    • ± bone graft, bone marrow aspirate concentrate, or a vascularised fibular graft.
    • Results are best for SMALL, MEDIAL lesions in YOUNG patients; a large lateral lesion will collapse regardless.
    • Bisphosphonates and other pharmacologic therapies have NOT shown consistent benefit.
    • Protected weight-bearing alone does NOT prevent collapse.
    • Remove the cause where possible — steroid reduction and alcohol cessation.
  • Post-collapse treatment:
    • Stage III–IV → TOTAL HIP ARTHROPLASTY, which gives reliable results in modern series.
    • Historically worse in patients with sickle cell disease, renal transplant and ongoing steroid use — largely because of the underlying disease rather than the implant.
    • Hemiarthroplasty and resurfacing are generally AVOIDED in osteonecrosis, because the femoral HEAD is the diseased structure and will not support the implant.
    • Rotational (transtrochanteric) osteotomy is used in some centres for young patients with a small, well-localised lesion, but it complicates a later THA.
  • The differential for a painful hip with normal radiographs:
    • Early OSTEONECROSIS.
    • SUBCHONDRAL INSUFFICIENCY FRACTURE — in an older or osteoporotic patient, with a low-signal subchondral line parallel to the articular surface.
    • TRANSIENT OSTEOPOROSIS / BONE MARROW OEDEMA SYNDROME — self-limiting, with DIFFUSE marrow oedema WITHOUT a serpiginous line, classically in a middle-aged man or a woman in the third trimester; treated with protected weight-bearing.
    • MRI distinguishes them — and the distinction matters, because two of the three need nothing more than time.

Revision arthroplasty: Bone loss, extensile exposure & constraint

Exposure options and managing bone loss in revision.

2 of 10 sittings2019 (x2), 2024F

  • Extended trochanteric osteotomy (ETO) — the workhorse extensile exposure:
    • It gives access to a well-fixed cemented or extensively porous-coated stem and to the femoral canal, and it corrects varus remodelling.
    • Typically ~12–15 cm long, taken laterally and hinged on the vastus lateralis / abductor sleeve to preserve the blood supply.
    • Repaired with cables, and the stem must bypass the distal extent of the osteotomy by at least two cortical diameters.
    • Union rates are high (>90%) precisely because the muscle sleeve is preserved.
  • Trochanteric osteotomy complications:
    • NONUNION.
    • Trochanteric ESCAPE / MIGRATION with ABDUCTOR WEAKNESS.
    • Painful hardware — cable and wire irritation and breakage.
    • A standard (Charnley) trochanteric osteotomy has a HIGHER nonunion rate than an ETO or a trochanteric SLIDE, because the slide and ETO preserve the vastus lateralis in continuity, NEUTRALISING the proximal pull of the abductors.
    • A trochanteric osteotomy DOES compromise abductor function — non-union of the fragment or trochanteric escape produces a persistent limp and is the main reason the classic osteotomy has largely been replaced by the extended (ETO) and slide variants.
  • Femoral bone loss (Paprosky):
    • Types I–II → a proximally or extensively porous-coated stem.
    • Type IIIA (>4 cm of intact diaphysis) → a long extensively coated stem, or a MODULAR TAPERED FLUTED TITANIUM stem.
    • Type IIIB (<4 cm of diaphysis) and Type IV (non-supportive diaphysis) → modular tapered stems, impaction grafting, an allograft-prosthetic composite, or a PROXIMAL FEMORAL REPLACEMENT.
    • The organising question is the same as for periprosthetic fracture: how much supportive diaphysis is left to grip?
  • Acetabular bone loss (Paprosky):
    • Type I/II → a hemispherical porous cup ± screws — highly porous metals such as tantalum have improved results, provided there is a supportive rim.
    • Type IIIA ("up and out") → a jumbo cup, an augment, or a structural graft.
    • Type IIIB ("up and in") and pelvic DISCONTINUITY → a CUP-CAGE construct, a custom TRIFLANGE, or a DISTRACTION technique.
    • The key intra-operative judgement is whether >50% host bone contact with rim support can be achieved — if so, a hemispherical cup will work.
  • Constraint — the last resort:
    • A CONSTRAINED liner transmits HIGH STRESS to the bone–implant interface and has a HIGH FAILURE rate.
    • Correct the underlying cause first — component position, offset, and abductor function.
    • DUAL MOBILITY is the preferred first-line option for recurrent instability with FUNCTIONING abductors.
    • Reserve a constrained liner for the patient with an absent abductor mechanism or a neurologic cause who cannot be salvaged otherwise.
  • Before any revision for "aseptic" loosening:
    • ALWAYS EXCLUDE INFECTION.
    • ESR/CRP and aspiration are mandatory, and intra-operative frozen section and multiple cultures should be taken.
    • A proportion of presumed aseptic loosenings are low-grade infections, and revising one as aseptic guarantees failure.
