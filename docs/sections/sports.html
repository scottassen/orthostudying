ACL reconstruction: Graft choice, technique & outcomes

Graft selection, tunnel placement, LET, and the predictors of failure.

7 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Graft choice: strength at time zero is not the thing that fails.
    • Load to failure at time zero: QUADRUPLED HAMSTRING (~4,000 N) > QUADRICEPS TENDON (~2,350 N) > BONE-PATELLAR TENDON-BONE (~2,000 N) > native ACL (~2,160 N). All modern grafts exceed the native ACL, so time-zero strength is almost never the reason a reconstruction fails.
    • BTB — bone-to-bone healing in 6 weeks, historically the "gold standard" for the high-demand pivoting athlete; costs are anterior knee pain, kneeling pain, quadriceps weakness, and a small risk of patellar fracture or patellar tendon rupture.
    • HAMSTRING (semitendinosus ± gracilis) — less donor-site pain, but soft-tissue-to-bone healing takes 8–12 weeks, there is measurable deep-flexion and internal-rotation weakness, and it carries a higher failure rate in young pivoting athletes in registry data. Saphenous (infrapatellar and sartorial branch) neuropraxia is the characteristic complication.
    • QUADRICEPS TENDON — large cross-sectional area, good strength, lower donor-site morbidity than BTB, and increasingly used for revision and in the skeletally immature; it can be taken with or without a patellar bone block. Comparative evidence shows outcomes and failure rates comparable to BTB and hamstring, with less anterior knee pain than BTB.
    • ALLOGRAFT — markedly higher failure rate in patients under 25 and in high-demand athletes (some series 3–4 times autograft), and irradiation above about 2.5 Mrad and chemical processing further weaken it. It is appropriate for the older, lower-demand patient, for multi-ligament reconstruction, and for revision when autograft is exhausted.
    • The one-line summary: in a young athlete, use an AUTOGRAFT; which autograft matters far less than tunnel position, age, sport and rehabilitation.
  • Tunnel placement is the technical determinant of outcome.
    • Anatomic femoral tunnel — at the native footprint on the posteromedial wall of the lateral femoral condyle, posterior and proximal, bounded by the lateral intercondylar ridge (resident's ridge) anteriorly and the lateral bifurcate ridge between the AM and PL bundles.
    • A tunnel placed too ANTERIOR (the classic "vertical graft" of transtibial drilling) is the commonest technical error: the graft is tight in flexion and lax in extension, giving loss of flexion and persistent ROTATIONAL instability with a positive pivot shift despite a negative Lachman.
    • A tunnel placed too POSTERIOR risks posterior wall blowout and a graft that is lax in flexion.
    • Tibial tunnel — centred on the native footprint, posterior and lateral to the medial tibial spine, in line with the posterior border of the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus. To avoid roof (notch) impingement, the tunnel must be POSTERIOR ENOUGH that the graft lies parallel to and just behind BLUMENSAAT'S LINE in FULL EXTENSION — a tunnel that is too anterior causes impingement, loss of extension and graft attrition.
    • Independent femoral drilling (anteromedial portal or outside-in) is used to reach the anatomic footprint, because a transtibial trajectory cannot get there. Hyperflex the knee for AM portal drilling to avoid a short femoral tunnel and posterior wall blowout, and to move the drill away from the PERONEAL NERVE and the lateral structures.
  • Predictors of failure and re-rupture.
    • YOUNG AGE (under about 20–25) is the single strongest risk factor, followed by return to a high-level pivoting/cutting sport, ALLOGRAFT use in a young patient, non-anatomic tunnel position, a family history of ACL injury, increased POSTERIOR TIBIAL SLOPE (>12°), high-grade preoperative pivot shift, and generalised ligamentous laxity.
    • Returning to sport before 9 months roughly multiplies re-injury risk; each month of delay up to about 9 months substantially reduces it. Criterion-based (not time-based) return-to-sport testing — limb symmetry above 90% on strength and hop testing, plus psychological readiness (ACL-RSI) — is the current standard.
    • Female sex is a risk factor for the INDEX injury but is NOT consistently a risk factor for GRAFT re-rupture — the contralateral ACL is at comparable or greater risk than the graft in young athletes. This is the distinction most easily reversed.
    • Chondral and meniscal injury at the time of reconstruction predicts worse outcome, and revision ACL reconstructions have more patellofemoral and tibiofemoral chondrosis than primaries.
    • Delayed reconstruction increases the rate of secondary meniscal tears, particularly medial meniscus posterior horn tears, in a knee that continues to give way — though a short delay to regain motion and quiet the knee reduces arthrofibrosis and does not harm outcome.
  • Lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) and the anterolateral complex.
    • The STABILITY randomised trial (Canadian-led, Getgood and colleagues) randomised young (≤25) high-risk patients undergoing hamstring autograft ACL reconstruction to ± a modified Lemaire LET.
    • Adding LET SIGNIFICANTLY REDUCED GRAFT FAILURE and persistent rotatory laxity at 2 years (clinical failure roughly 25% down to 11%; graft rupture roughly 11% down to 4%), with no increase in complications and no difference in patient-reported outcomes.
    • Indications now generally accepted: age under 25, high-grade pivot shift, generalised ligamentous laxity, revision ACL reconstruction, return to a high-risk pivoting sport, and increased posterior tibial slope.
    • The anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction is an alternative that targets the same rotatory instability; comparative evidence between LET and ALL reconstruction is still developing, but LET has the higher-level trial support.
    • Over-tensioning a LET risks lateral compartment overconstraint; it is fixed with the knee in about 60° of flexion and neutral rotation.
  • The paediatric and adolescent ACL.
    • Non-operative treatment of a complete ACL tear in an active child produces high rates of instability, further meniscal tears and chondral damage — delaying reconstruction to skeletal maturity is no longer standard.
    • Physeal-sparing options in the very immature (Tanner 1–2): the all-epiphyseal reconstruction and the Micheli-Kocher combined intra/extra-articular iliotibial band technique.
    • Transphyseal reconstruction with a SOFT-TISSUE graft is used in the older child/adolescent — NEVER take a bone block across an open physis, avoid fixation hardware crossing the physis, use vertical rather than oblique tunnels, keep the tunnel small, and do not over-tension. Growth disturbance is uncommon but real (limb-length discrepancy and valgus from a lateral distal femoral physeal bar).
    • Graft failure in adolescents is driven by young age, return to pivoting sport, allograft use, non-anatomic tunnels and increased posterior tibial slope — not by the choice between a physeal-sparing and a transphyseal technique.
    • Adolescents have the highest re-rupture and contralateral injury rates of any group — up to roughly a third within a few years — which is the core counselling point.
  • Two intra-operative problems worth having an answer for.
    • GRAFT CONTAMINATION (the graft is dropped on the floor): the graft should be decontaminated and reused rather than discarded in most circumstances. The accepted protocol is a sequential wash — most commonly chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine followed by copious saline and antibiotic solution. Chlorhexidine gluconate has the best evidence for eradicating contamination; a saline-only wash is insufficient. Reported infection rates after protocol-based decontamination are very low, and harvesting a second autograft or switching to allograft is the alternative if the surgeon is not satisfied.
    • SEPTIC ARTHRITIS after ACL reconstruction (roughly 0.3–0.5%, most often coagulase-negative staphylococci, higher with hamstring autograft): treat with urgent arthroscopic irrigation and debridement plus culture-directed antibiotics, and RETAIN THE GRAFT. The graft is removed only if it is frankly necrotic, the infection persists after repeated debridement, or the organism is unusually virulent. Retained hardware is generally left unless infection persists.

Patellofemoral instability & MPFL reconstruction

MPFL anatomy, risk factors for recurrence, and the surgical algorithm.

7 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • MPFL anatomy: the primary restraint, and only in early flexion.
    • The MPFL provides 50–60% of the restraint to LATERAL patellar translation, and it does so between 0° and about 30° of flexion — beyond that the patella engages the trochlear groove and the bony architecture takes over.
    • Femoral origin: in the SADDLE between the ADDUCTOR TUBERCLE and the MEDIAL EPICONDYLE — on a true lateral this is SCHÖTTLE'S POINT: just ANTERIOR to the posterior cortical extension line, DISTAL to the posterior origin of the medial femoral condyle, and PROXIMAL to the level of the posterior point of Blumensaat's line.
    • Patellar insertion: the PROXIMAL TWO-THIRDS (roughly the superomedial half) of the medial patella, blending with the deep surface of vastus medialis obliquus.
    • Companion structures of the medial patellofemoral complex: the medial patellotibial ligament (MPTL) and the medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament (MQTFL), which are increasingly reconstructed alongside or instead of the MPFL in the hyperlax or hyperextending knee.
  • The femoral tunnel is the technical crux.
    • The graft is nearly ISOMETRIC only if the femoral origin is correct; the patellar side is far more forgiving.
    • A tunnel that is too PROXIMAL (or anterior) makes the graft TIGHT IN FLEXION — producing loss of flexion, medial patellofemoral overload and pain, and eventual graft stretch or medial patellar cartilage wear.
    • A tunnel too DISTAL makes the graft lax in flexion and tight in extension, and fails to control the patella where it matters.
    • Confirm the tunnel FLUOROSCOPICALLY on a true lateral before drilling, and tension the graft at 30° of flexion with the patella centred, using minimal tension — the MPFL is a checkrein, not a tether. Over-tensioning (or over-medialising) is the commonest cause of a painful, stiff knee after reconstruction.
    • The graft should never be used to pull a patella into a dysplastic trochlea — an isolated MPFL reconstruction fails when the underlying bony risk factors are ignored.
  • The four anatomic risk factors, and how they are measured.
    • TROCHLEAR DYSPLASIA — the strongest single risk factor. Graded by the Dejour classification (A–D) on a true lateral: the CROSSING SIGN (type A), the SUPRATROCHLEAR SPUR and DOUBLE CONTOUR (types B–D). A sulcus angle above 145° on axial imaging is abnormal.
    • PATELLA ALTA — Caton-Deschamps index >1.2 (or Insall-Salvati >1.2); the patella does not engage the trochlea until later in flexion.
    • INCREASED TT-TG DISTANCE — abnormal above 20 mm on CT/MRI (a TT-PCL distance >24 mm is the alternative that removes rotational error).
    • PATELLAR TILT / excessive lateral tilt (>20°) and, less consistently, increased femoral anteversion, external tibial torsion, genu valgum and generalised ligamentous laxity.
  • Predictors of RECURRENCE after a first dislocation.
    • SKELETAL IMMATURITY / YOUNG AGE — the strongest predictor; recurrence after a first dislocation is roughly 15–20% overall but above 50% in a skeletally immature patient with dysplasia.
    • TROCHLEAR DYSPLASIA, PATELLA ALTA, a history of CONTRALATERAL dislocation, and increased TT-TG.
    • The Patellar Instability Severity Score and the Recurrent Instability of the Patella Score (RIP) combine these into a usable risk estimate.
    • What does NOT predict recurrence: the presence of an osteochondral fragment, the size of the effusion, or whether the MPFL tore at the patellar or the femoral end — the bony morphology dominates.
  • Treatment algorithm.
    • A FIRST-TIME dislocation is treated NON-OPERATIVELY — brief immobilisation, then bracing with early motion and a VMO/hip-abductor/core rehabilitation programme.
    • The exception: an OSTEOCHONDRAL FRAGMENT or a loose body requires urgent arthroscopy with fixation or removal, and the MPFL is usually repaired or reconstructed at the same time. A displaced osteochondral fracture off the medial patellar facet or the lateral femoral condyle is the finding that converts a first dislocation into a surgical problem.
    • RECURRENT instability → MPFL RECONSTRUCTION (graft: gracilis, semitendinosus, quadriceps tendon or allograft). Reconstruction outperforms repair, which has an unacceptably high recurrence rate.
    • Add a TIBIAL TUBERCLE OSTEOTOMY when the TT-TG exceeds about 20 mm — medialising (Elmslie-Trillat) for a high TT-TG, ANTEROMEDIALISING (Fulkerson) when there is also DISTAL or LATERAL patellar chondral wear, and DISTALISING when there is significant patella alta. A tubercle osteotomy must NEVER be performed in a patient with an OPEN TIBIAL TUBERCLE APOPHYSIS — it causes genu recurvatum. In the skeletally immature, use an MPFL reconstruction (with a soft-tissue distal realignment such as Roux-Goldthwait or a Galeazzi procedure if needed) instead.
    • Trochleoplasty is reserved for high-grade (Dejour B/D) dysplasia with a supratrochlear spur in a skeletally mature patient, usually after a failed MPFL reconstruction; it is technically demanding and risks stiffness and chondral damage.
    • An ISOLATED LATERAL RELEASE IS NOT A TREATMENT FOR INSTABILITY — it is for isolated lateral patellar compression syndrome with a genuinely tight lateral retinaculum, and performing it for instability can cause iatrogenic MEDIAL patellar instability, a difficult problem to salvage. A lateral lengthening rather than release is preferred when the retinaculum is tight.
  • Why reconstructions fail.
    • Non-anatomic femoral tunnel (the commonest reason), over-tensioning, and failure to address uncorrected bony risk factors — particularly high-grade trochlear dysplasia, patella alta and an elevated TT-TG.
    • Other causes: graft failure or stretch, patellar fracture from an over-large or transverse patellar tunnel, and unrecognised rotational malalignment (femoral anteversion, external tibial torsion).
    • Work-up of a failed reconstruction: a true lateral and axial radiographs, a CT rotational profile, and MRI — and specifically re-measure the femoral tunnel against Schöttle's point.

Meniscus tears, root repair & allograft transplantation

Meniscal function, root tears and the indications for transplantation.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2022 (x3)

  • Structure and function: hoop stress is the whole story.
    • The meniscus is fibrocartilage, roughly 70% water, with type I collagen predominating, arranged CIRCUMFERENTIALLY to resist hoop stress, with radial "tie" fibres binding the bundles.
    • It transmits 50–70% of the load in extension and up to 85–90% in flexion, and converts axial load into circumferential (hoop) tension anchored by the anterior and posterior ROOTS.
    • Total meniscectomy raises peak contact pressure by roughly 235% and contact area falls by about 75% — the basis of Fairbank's changes and the reason meniscus preservation dominates modern practice.
    • Blood supply: the perimeniscal capillary plexus from the medial and lateral geniculate arteries penetrates only the peripheral 10–30% — the RED-RED zone (peripheral third, heals), RED-WHITE (middle), WHITE-WHITE (inner third, avascular, does not heal).
    • The LATERAL meniscus is more mobile (about 9–11 mm of excursion versus 2–5 mm medially), is not attached to the LCL, and is interrupted at the popliteal hiatus — which is why lateral meniscectomy is far less well tolerated than medial and produces faster degeneration.
  • Root tears: biomechanically equivalent to a total meniscectomy.
    • A complete radial tear within 1 cm of the root, or an avulsion of the root itself, DESTROYS the circumferential hoop-stress mechanism. The meniscus EXTRUDES medially and no longer transmits load.
    • The consequence: INCREASED tibiofemoral contact pressure AND INCREASED peak contact pressure, with DECREASED contact area — kinematically and biomechanically equivalent to a total meniscectomy. That specific pairing (both mean and peak pressure up, area down) is the examinable point.
    • MEDIAL posterior root tears are typically degenerative, in middle-aged patients, often after a minor squatting or twisting event, and are strongly associated with meniscal extrusion, a subchondral insufficiency fracture of the medial femoral condyle (formerly "SONK") and varus alignment.
    • LATERAL posterior root tears are typically traumatic and associated with ACL rupture (present in up to 10% of ACL tears) and must be looked for and repaired at the time of ACL reconstruction — they significantly increase anterior tibial translation and rotatory laxity.
    • Treatment: TRANSTIBIAL PULL-OUT REPAIR (or suture anchor repair) restores contact mechanics and slows degeneration. Repair outperforms meniscectomy and non-operative treatment for progression to arthroplasty.
    • Contraindications to root repair: advanced (Kellgren-Lawrence 3–4) arthritis, uncorrected malalignment (a varus knee needs a concurrent or staged HTO), and a chronically extruded, degenerate meniscus.
  • Repair versus resection for the rest.
    • Repair whenever possible, especially: peripheral (red-red/red-white) longitudinal tears, tears in young patients, tears concurrent with ACL reconstruction (the healing environment is markedly better), and BUCKET-HANDLE tears.
    • A locked bucket-handle tear is a semi-urgent problem — the displaced fragment blocks extension and must be reduced and repaired.
    • RAMP LESIONS (a tear at the meniscocapsular junction of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus) are present in roughly a quarter of ACL tears, are frequently missed on standard anterolateral viewing and on MRI, and require a posteromedial portal or viewing through the notch. They add anterior and rotatory laxity, and repair is recommended.
    • DEGENERATIVE horizontal cleavage tears in a middle-aged or older patient with arthritis should NOT be resected — multiple randomised trials (including the FIDELITY sham-controlled trial and the METEOR trial) show arthroscopic partial meniscectomy is no better than physiotherapy or a sham procedure. This is one of the most consistently tested evidence points in sports medicine.
    • When resection is unavoidable, preserve the peripheral rim — the rim is what preserves hoop stress.
  • Meniscal allograft transplantation: who qualifies.
    • The ideal candidate: YOUNG (under ~50), a symptomatic MENISCUS-DEFICIENT compartment after previous meniscectomy, pain LOCALISED to that compartment, a STABLE knee, NEUTRAL alignment, and INTACT (or nearly intact) articular cartilage (Outerbridge ≤2).
    • Contraindications: advanced arthritis (grade III–IV changes with joint space narrowing), uncorrected malalignment, uncorrected ligamentous instability, inflammatory arthropathy, obesity, and previous infection. Malalignment and instability are not absolute — they must be corrected concurrently or in a staged fashion (HTO, ACL reconstruction).
    • It is a PAIN-RELIEVING and potentially chondroprotective procedure, NOT a return-to-high-level-pivoting-sport procedure, and patients must be counselled accordingly.
    • Grafts are size-matched to within about 5% using plain radiographs (or MRI/CT), and are FRESH-FROZEN and NON-irradiated in current practice; they are not HLA-matched and immunosuppression is not used.
    • Bony fixation of the horns (bone plugs medially, a bone bridge laterally) is biomechanically superior to soft-tissue-only fixation for restoring hoop stress, because it anchors the roots. Survivorship is roughly 70% at 10 years.
  • Discoid lateral meniscus.
    • A congenital variant of the LATERAL meniscus, present in roughly 3–5% of knees (higher, up to 15%, in Asian populations) and bilateral in about 20%.
    • Watanabe classification: TYPE I complete, TYPE II incomplete, TYPE III (WRISBERG) — lacking the posterior meniscotibial (coronary) attachment, held only by the ligament of Wrisberg. Only the Wrisberg variant is inherently unstable, and it is the one that produces the classic "SNAPPING KNEE" in a child.
    • Presentation: a child with painless snapping, a clunk on extension, or a mechanical block; a torn discoid presents with pain, effusion and locking.
    • Radiographs may show a widened lateral joint space, squaring of the lateral femoral condyle, cupping of the lateral tibial plateau and a hypoplastic lateral tibial spine; MRI shows three or more contiguous 5 mm sagittal slices with meniscal continuity ("bow-tie sign").
    • Treatment: an ASYMPTOMATIC discoid meniscus is left alone. Symptomatic lesions are treated with arthroscopic SAUCERISATION (partial central resection to a stable 6–8 mm peripheral rim), plus REPAIR or peripheral STABILISATION if the rim is unstable. Total meniscectomy is avoided — it produces early lateral compartment degeneration in a child.

Multiligament knee injury & knee dislocation

Vascular assessment, the obese low-energy dislocation, and staged management.

4 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Recognise it: the knee that reduced itself.
    • A knee dislocation is defined by injury to at least TWO of the four ligamentous structures (ACL, PCL, MCL/PMC, LCL/PLC), whether or not it presents dislocated.
    • Up to half of knee dislocations SPONTANEOUSLY REDUCE before assessment — so a multiligament injury pattern IS a knee dislocation until proven otherwise, and must be worked up as one. A normal-looking radiograph is not reassurance.
    • Obesity and low-velocity/ultra-low-velocity mechanisms are an increasingly recognised group with a higher rate of vascular injury and wound complications than the classic high-energy pattern.
    • The SCHENCK (KD) classification: KD-I one cruciate + a collateral; KD-II both cruciates, collaterals intact; KD-III-M both cruciates + MCL; KD-III-L both cruciates + PLC (the pattern with the highest rate of peroneal nerve and vascular injury); KD-IV all four; KD-V with a periarticular fracture. Suffix C for arterial and N for nerve injury.
  • Vascular assessment is the priority, and pulses are not enough.
    • The POPLITEAL ARTERY is tethered proximally at the ADDUCTOR HIATUS and distally by the SOLEAL ARCH / fibrous tunnel, which is why it is injured in roughly 5–15% of knee dislocations (highest with KD-III-L and anterior/posterior dislocations).
    • Serial examination plus the ANKLE-BRACHIAL INDEX (ABI) is the accepted screening algorithm. ABI ≥0.9 with normal pulses → serial observation for 24–48 hours. ABI <0.9 → urgent CT ANGIOGRAPHY or immediate vascular surgical consultation.
    • Selective angiography based on ABI has replaced routine angiography for all, with a negative predictive value approaching 100% for a normal ABI and normal serial examination.
    • Hard signs (absent pulses, expanding haematoma, active haemorrhage, bruit or thrill, distal ischaemia) mandate IMMEDIATE theatre — do not delay for imaging.
    • The warm ischaemia window is roughly 6–8 hours; beyond it, amputation rates rise steeply. Vascular repair precedes ligament surgery, and a PROPHYLACTIC FOUR-COMPARTMENT FASCIOTOMY should be performed after revascularisation when ischaemia has been prolonged.
    • An intimal tear found on CTA in an otherwise perfused limb is usually managed with observation and antiplatelet therapy rather than intervention.
  • The peroneal nerve.
    • Injured in roughly 25% of knee dislocations overall, and up to 40–50% of KD-III-L (PLC) injuries — the nerve is stretched over the fibular neck as the posterolateral corner fails.
    • Prognosis is poor: only about a third of complete palsies recover useful function; incomplete palsies do considerably better.
    • Document the deficit before reduction and again after; a deficit that appears after reduction or after surgery demands immediate exploration.
    • Options for a persistent drop foot: an AFO, posterior tibial tendon transfer, or nerve grafting/transfer if explored early; nerve grafting over a defect longer than about 6 cm has poor results.
  • Acute management.
    • Urgent CLOSED REDUCTION under sedation, then reassess the neurovascular status and splint or SPAN with an external fixator.
    • An IRREDUCIBLE dislocation — classically the POSTEROLATERAL dislocation with the "DIMPLE SIGN" (puckering of the medial skin from invaginated medial capsule and the MFC buttonholing through it) — requires OPEN reduction, and closed attempts risk skin necrosis.
    • Indications for a spanning external fixator: vascular repair, an open injury, gross instability that will not hold reduced, obesity, or a polytrauma patient.
    • Get an MRI once the limb is stable to map the ligament injuries, and a CT if there is a periarticular fracture.
  • Ligament reconstruction: timing and priorities.
    • EARLY (within about 3 weeks) surgery gives better stability and functional outcomes than delayed reconstruction, at the cost of a higher rate of arthrofibrosis. The pragmatic window most surgeons use is 2–3 weeks — late enough for the capsule to seal (allowing arthroscopy without fluid extravasation) and early enough that tissue planes are still identifiable.
    • Some injuries are repaired truly acutely: the PLC and the peroneal nerve are best addressed within 2–3 weeks, because after that direct repair is impossible and reconstruction is required. An open injury, a vascular injury, an irreducible knee, and a displaced bony avulsion all force earlier surgery.
    • RECONSTRUCTION outperforms REPAIR for the PLC and for the cruciates; the exception is a bony avulsion (an arcuate fracture, a fibular styloid avulsion, a PCL tibial avulsion), which is fixed directly.
    • Operative treatment gives better outcomes than non-operative treatment for the multiligament knee; non-operative management is reserved for the medically unfit.
    • Sequence within a single stage: fix bony avulsions → place tunnels for all grafts before fixing any → fix the PCL first (it is the central pivot and reference point), then the ACL, then the collaterals/corners with the knee at 30° and in slight valgus/varus as appropriate. Rehabilitate in a hinged brace with the PCL protected.
    • Outcomes to counsel for: most patients regain a functional knee but do not return to pre-injury sport; stiffness requiring manipulation or arthroscopic lysis is common; post-traumatic arthritis is the long-term expectation.

Posterolateral corner injury & lateral-sided knee instability

PLC anatomy, examination and the consequences of missing it.

3 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2024F

  • The three PRIMARY (static) stabilisers — the answer to "what are the components of the PLC".
    • LATERAL (FIBULAR) COLLATERAL LIGAMENT — from just PROXIMAL AND POSTERIOR to the lateral epicondyle to the lateral aspect of the fibular head. The primary restraint to VARUS.
    • POPLITEUS TENDON — from the popliteal sulcus of the lateral femoral condyle, ANTERIOR AND DISTAL to the LCL origin (about 18 mm apart), running to the posteromedial tibia. The primary restraint to EXTERNAL ROTATION.
    • POPLITEOFIBULAR LIGAMENT — from the popliteus musculotendinous junction to the posteromedial fibular styloid; a co-restraint to external rotation and varus.
    • SECONDARY structures: the ARCUATE LIGAMENT, the FABELLOFIBULAR LIGAMENT, the LATERAL CAPSULE, the LATERAL GASTROCNEMIUS tendon, and the dynamic contributions of BICEPS FEMORIS and the ILIOTIBIAL BAND.
    • Structures that are NOT part of the posterolateral corner and appear as distractors: the POSTERIOR OBLIQUE LIGAMENT and the semimembranosus (both POSTEROMEDIAL), the meniscofemoral ligaments of Humphrey and Wrisberg, and the deep MCL.
  • Seebacher's three layers — the approach.
    • LAYER I — the ILIOTIBIAL TRACT and the superficial part of BICEPS FEMORIS. The COMMON PERONEAL NERVE lies deep to and just posterior to layer I, and is found posteromedial to the biceps tendon.
    • LAYER II — the PATELLAR RETINACULUM and the patellofemoral ligaments.
    • LAYER III — the LATERAL CAPSULAR layer, split into a superficial lamina (containing the LCL and the fabellofibular ligament) and a deep lamina (containing the coronary ligament, the popliteus tendon, the popliteofibular ligament and the arcuate ligament). The LATERAL INFERIOR GENICULATE ARTERY runs between the two laminae — a useful intra-operative landmark.
    • Identify and protect the PERONEAL NERVE FIRST, before any deeper dissection; a neurolysis is often performed as part of the exposure.
  • Examination: the tests, and what they mean at 30° versus 90°.
    • VARUS STRESS AT 30° — isolated LCL/PLC injury. VARUS LAXITY AT 0° (full extension) implies a COMBINED PLC + CRUCIATE injury, because the cruciates are secondary varus restraints in extension.
    • DIAL TEST (prone or supine, comparing external rotation of both feet at 30° and 90° of knee flexion):
      • >10–15° asymmetric external rotation at 30° ONLY = ISOLATED PLC injury.
      • Increased at BOTH 30° AND 90° = COMBINED PLC + PCL injury. This is the classic examination question.
      • Increased at 90° only is unusual and suggests an isolated PCL injury or a posteromedial problem.
    • EXTERNAL ROTATION RECURVATUM TEST — lifting the great toe with the patient supine produces hyperextension, varus and external rotation; positive in combined PLC and cruciate injury.
    • REVERSE PIVOT SHIFT and the POSTEROLATERAL DRAWER complete the set.
    • Do not forget the PERONEAL NERVE examination and the GAIT assessment for a VARUS THRUST.
  • Imaging.
    • Plain radiographs — look for the ARCUATE SIGN (an avulsion of the fibular styloid, pathognomonic of a PLC injury), a Segond fracture (ACL), and a widened lateral joint space.
    • VARUS STRESS RADIOGRAPHS quantify it: >2.7 mm of side-to-side lateral compartment gapping at 20° suggests an isolated LCL/complete PLC injury, and >4 mm indicates a complete PLC injury with cruciate involvement.
    • MRI within the first few weeks is the diagnostic standard, using thin coronal oblique sequences along the plane of the popliteofibular ligament; MRI sensitivity falls markedly after 12 weeks as the acute oedema resolves.
    • Get LONG-LEG ALIGNMENT FILMS in every chronic case.
  • Treatment.
    • GRADE I–II (partial, <10 mm lateral opening) → non-operative, with immobilisation in extension and protected weightbearing, then progressive rehabilitation.
    • GRADE III (complete) → OPERATIVE — the natural history of an untreated complete PLC injury is chronic instability, varus thrust and, critically, failure of any concurrent ACL or PCL reconstruction.
    • ACUTE (within 2–3 weeks): repair BONY AVULSIONS directly; RECONSTRUCT mid-substance ligament injuries. Direct repair of soft-tissue PLC injuries has a substantially higher failure rate than reconstruction (roughly 37% versus 9% in the Mayo comparative series), so reconstruction is preferred even acutely.
    • Anatomic reconstruction (LaPrade) recreates the LCL, popliteus tendon and popliteofibular ligament with two femoral tunnels and a fibular and tibial tunnel; the Larson (fibular sling) technique reconstructs the LCL and popliteofibular ligament with a single fibular tunnel and is simpler.
    • THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT PRINCIPLE IN THE CHRONIC CASE: CORRECT VARUS MALALIGNMENT FIRST. A ligament reconstruction performed into a varus-aligned knee with a varus thrust WILL FAIL — a lateral closing-wedge or medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (a proximal tibial osteotomy that also addresses increased posterior slope if the PCL is deficient) is done first, and roughly a third of patients need no further surgery afterwards.
    • Missing a PLC injury is the commonest reason a cruciate reconstruction fails — it should be actively excluded in every revision ACL and PCL work-up.

Articular cartilage restoration: OATS, ACI & microfracture

Choosing a cartilage procedure by lesion size and characteristics.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2023

  • Microfracture / marrow stimulation.
    • Best for SMALL (<2–2.5 cm²) contained lesions in lower-demand patients, as a first-line procedure.
    • It produces FIBROCARTILAGE (type I collagen), which is biomechanically inferior to hyaline cartilage and deteriorates after 2–5 years.
    • Technique: debride to a stable vertical rim, remove the CALCIFIED CARTILAGE LAYER without violating the subchondral plate, and space the awl holes 3–4 mm apart; deflate the tourniquet to confirm marrow egress.
    • Subchondral bone overgrowth and INTRALESIONAL OSTEOPHYTES compromise every later procedure — which is the main argument against using microfracture reflexively in a young athlete who may need ACI or an allograft later.
  • Osteochondral autograft transfer (OATS / mosaicplasty).
    • The ideal candidate: a SMALL-to-MEDIUM (1–4 cm²), FOCAL, CONTAINED, SYMPTOMATIC lesion, in a YOUNG patient (<40–50), with a STABLE and WELL-ALIGNED knee and INTACT OPPOSING cartilage.
    • It provides IMMEDIATE HYALINE cartilage on a bony base, which is why it gives the fastest and most reliable return to sport of any cartilage procedure.
    • Donor plugs are taken from the non-weightbearing periphery of the trochlea (superolateral or superomedial) or the intercondylar notch.
    • Limitations: DONOR-SITE MORBIDITY, a limited amount of graft, and difficulty matching surface contour and cartilage thickness for large lesions; plugs must be inserted flush and perpendicular, since a proud plug overloads and a recessed plug fails to share load.
  • Autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI / MACI).
    • A TWO-STAGE procedure: arthroscopic cartilage harvest from a non-weightbearing area, cell culture over several weeks, then implantation under a periosteal patch (first generation) or on a collagen membrane (MACI).
    • Indicated for LARGE (>4 cm²) symptomatic femoral condyle, trochlear or PATELLAR lesions in young patients, particularly after FAILED marrow stimulation — ACI is one of the few options that performs well on the patella.
    • It produces HYALINE-LIKE cartilage that improves in quality over time, and randomised comparisons show it outperforms microfracture for large lesions at medium-term follow-up.
    • It requires an INTACT SUBCHONDRAL BONE PLATE — a deep osseous defect (as in OCD) needs a "sandwich" technique with bone grafting, or an osteochondral allograft instead.
    • Drawbacks: two operations, cost, PROLONGED REHABILITATION with return to sport at 12–18 months, and the characteristic complications of graft HYPERTROPHY (mostly with the periosteal patch) and DELAMINATION.
  • Osteochondral allograft.
    • For LARGE (>4 cm²) lesions WITH BONE LOSS, revision cases after failed cartilage surgery, osteochondritis dissecans with a large osseous defect, and post-traumatic or steroid-associated osteonecrosis.
    • FRESH (hypothermically stored, NOT frozen) grafts are used, because chondrocyte viability falls sharply with freezing; the practical window is roughly 14–28 days from procurement, after a 14-day quarantine for microbiological screening.
    • The graft is size-matched but NOT HLA-matched, and immunosuppression is not used — the chondrocytes are protected from the immune system by the surrounding matrix. The BONE portion is the immunogenic part, so the bony plug is kept thin (about 6–8 mm) and pulse-lavaged to remove marrow elements.
    • Survivorship is roughly 75–85% at 10 years on the femoral condyle, with worse results on the patella and in bipolar (kissing) lesions.
  • The universal rule: fix the environment first.
    • CORRECT MALALIGNMENT, INSTABILITY AND MENISCAL DEFICIENCY, OR THE CARTILAGE PROCEDURE WILL FAIL. A concurrent or staged high tibial or distal femoral osteotomy, ligament reconstruction, or meniscal allograft transplantation is frequently required.
    • PATELLOFEMORAL lesions additionally require correction of maltracking — measure the TT-TG and add an anteromedialising (Fulkerson) tubercle osteotomy where indicated, which also offloads a distal or lateral patellar lesion.
    • A "kissing" bipolar lesion, inflammatory arthropathy, and generalised osteoarthritis are contraindications to all of these procedures.
  • Osteochondritis dissecans: prognosis and the specific answers.
    • GOOD prognostic factors: SKELETALLY IMMATURE with OPEN PHYSES (much the strongest favourable factor), a STABLE lesion, SMALLER size, the CLASSIC location (lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle), and INTACT overlying cartilage.
    • POOR prognostic factors: adult onset/closed physes, an UNSTABLE or detached lesion, larger size, an ATYPICAL or weightbearing/lateral condyle location, and a lesion with surrounding cyst formation or a high-signal rim on MRI.
    • Signs of INSTABILITY on MRI: a high-signal (fluid) line behind the fragment, fluid-filled cysts beneath it, a full-thickness articular cartilage defect, and a displaced fragment.
    • Treatment: a STABLE lesion in a SKELETALLY IMMATURE knee is treated NON-OPERATIVELY (activity modification ± unloading for 3–6 months), with roughly half to two-thirds healing. Failure to heal → drilling (transarticular or retroarticular). An unstable or hinged lesion → fixation with bone grafting; a detached, unsalvageable fragment → a restorative procedure (OATS or osteochondral allograft).
    • CAPITELLAR OCD follows the same logic: an open capitellar physis, a stable lesion with intact cartilage, a small lesion and preserved elbow motion predict a good outcome; poor prognostic signs are a closed physis, an unstable or fragmented lesion, LATERAL BUTTRESS INVOLVEMENT, a large lesion, and loss of motion or mechanical symptoms. It is distinguished from Panner's disease (a younger child, a self-limiting osteochondrosis of the entire ossific nucleus that resolves without surgery).

Posterior cruciate ligament injury

PCL anatomy, natural history and the indications for reconstruction.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2023

  • Anatomy and function.
    • The PCL is the primary restraint to POSTERIOR TIBIAL TRANSLATION, providing about 95% of the restraining force, and is the strongest and largest ligament in the knee (roughly twice the cross-sectional area and strength of the ACL).
    • Two bundles: the larger ANTEROLATERAL bundle, TIGHT IN FLEXION, and the smaller POSTEROMEDIAL bundle, TIGHT IN EXTENSION. Most single-bundle reconstructions reproduce the anterolateral bundle, which is why residual laxity in extension is common.
    • The meniscofemoral ligaments of HUMPHREY (ANTERIOR to the PCL) and WRISBERG (POSTERIOR to the PCL) run from the posterior horn of the lateral meniscus to the medial femoral condyle; at least one is present in the large majority of knees and they are secondary restraints.
    • The femoral origin is a broad footprint on the lateral wall of the MEDIAL femoral condyle; the tibial insertion is in the PCL facet on the posterior tibia, about 1–1.5 cm below the joint line.
  • Mechanism and examination.
    • Mechanism: a posteriorly directed force on the FLEXED tibia (the DASHBOARD injury), or a fall onto a flexed knee with a plantarflexed foot. Hyperextension tears the PCL as part of a multiligament pattern.
    • The POSTERIOR DRAWER TEST AT 90° IS THE MOST SENSITIVE AND SPECIFIC TEST. Grade by the medial tibial STEP-OFF (normally the plateau sits about 1 cm anterior to the femoral condyles):
      • GRADE I — the plateau remains ANTERIOR to the condyles (0–5 mm of translation).
      • GRADE II — the plateau is FLUSH with the condyles (5–10 mm).
      • GRADE III — the plateau translates POSTERIOR to the condyles (>10 mm), which implies an associated injury, usually the PLC.
    • Adjuncts: the QUADRICEPS ACTIVE TEST (the tibia reduces anteriorly on quadriceps contraction at 90°), the POSTERIOR SAG (Godfrey) sign, and the reverse pivot shift.
    • Always perform a DIAL TEST: increased external rotation at BOTH 30° and 90° means a combined PCL + PLC injury.
    • Imaging: MRI is highly sensitive acutely; POSTERIOR STRESS RADIOGRAPHS (kneeling or Telos) quantify chronic laxity — more than 8–12 mm of side-to-side difference indicates a complete tear, and more than 12 mm indicates a combined PCL/PLC injury. MRI sensitivity falls in the chronic setting because the PCL heals in continuity, so a chronically lax PCL may look normal on MRI — the specific trap.
  • Natural history.
    • Isolated GRADE I–II PCL injuries generally do well NON-OPERATIVELY with quadriceps-focused rehabilitation, and most athletes return to sport.
    • The PCL has an intrinsic capacity to HEAL IN CONTINUITY (unlike the ACL), because it is extrasynovial and has a better blood supply — though it often heals with residual laxity in a lengthened position.
    • Long-term follow-up nevertheless shows a significant incidence of MEDIAL COMPARTMENT and PATELLOFEMORAL degenerative change — the posteriorly subluxated tibia shifts contact and increases patellofemoral load.
  • Indications for surgery.
    • A BONY AVULSION of the PCL from the tibia — ORIF, and it is the one PCL injury with reliably excellent results.
    • GRADE III isolated injury (>10–12 mm of translation), and symptomatic instability despite a proper rehabilitation programme.
    • Any PCL injury as part of a MULTILIGAMENT pattern, and any combined PCL + PLC injury — both must be reconstructed.
    • Technique choices: TRANSTIBIAL versus TIBIAL INLAY. The transtibial tunnel creates the "KILLER TURN" — an acute angle at the posterior tibial tunnel aperture that abrades the graft and is implicated in residual laxity; the inlay technique avoids it by fixing a bone block directly into the posterior tibia, at the cost of a posterior approach and prone positioning. Single- versus double-bundle: double-bundle gives better biomechanical restoration of laxity but no consistent clinical advantage.
    • The POPLITEAL ARTERY is the structure at risk when drilling or reaming the posterior tibial tunnel — keep the knee flexed, use a posterior retractor, ream under direct arthroscopic and fluoroscopic control, and use a curette to protect the posterior cortex.
  • Why PCL reconstructions fail, and rehabilitation.
    • AN UNRECOGNISED POSTEROLATERAL CORNER INJURY IS THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF PCL RECONSTRUCTION FAILURE.
    • UNCORRECTED VARUS MALALIGNMENT and INCREASED POSTERIOR TIBIAL SLOPE are the other two. In the chronic varus knee, correct the alignment first — and an opening-wedge HTO that also DECREASES posterior slope reduces posterior tibial translation and is the specific osteotomy for PCL deficiency (the reverse of what is wanted in ACL deficiency, where reducing slope is protective and increasing it is harmful).
    • Rehabilitation: QUADRICEPS strengthening is the priority; AVOID isolated HAMSTRING exercises early, because the hamstrings pull the tibia POSTERIORLY and stress the graft.
    • Use a dynamic PCL brace and PRONE range-of-motion exercises to prevent posterior sag under gravity, and protect weightbearing in extension in a brace.

Proximal tibiofibular joint instability

The overlooked cause of lateral knee pain.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2024S

  • Anatomy of the joint.
    • A SYNOVIAL (diarthrodial) joint between the fibular head and the posterolateral tibial condyle, stabilised by the anterior and posterior proximal tibiofibular ligaments, the LCL, the biceps femoris tendon and the popliteus/fabellofibular complex.
    • It communicates with the knee joint in roughly 10% of people — which is why a knee effusion can decompress into it and produce a proximal tibiofibular ganglion.
    • Two morphologies (Ogden): HORIZONTAL — more stable, larger surface area, greater rotational tolerance; and OBLIQUE (inclination >20°) — LESS STABLE, smaller surface area, and more prone to instability and dislocation.
    • The COMMON PERONEAL NERVE wraps the fibular neck immediately distal to the joint — the structure at risk in every posteromedial dislocation, in fibular head fixation and in reconstruction.
  • Function.
    • It dissipates TORSIONAL stress transmitted from the ankle, transmits axial load, and resists lateral tibial bending.
    • The fibula bears roughly 6–17% of the axial load at the ankle (and the joint transmits about one sixth of the static load), which is why fibular head resection or arthrodesis of this joint transfers load and torsion to the ankle and causes ankle pain.
    • The fibula also externally rotates and translates proximally with ankle dorsiflexion — the reason instability here presents with pain on ankle motion.
  • Mechanism and classification (Ogden).
    • Type I — subluxation (atraumatic, in generalised laxity or Ehlers-Danlos, often in adolescent girls).
    • Type II — ANTEROLATERAL dislocation: the commonest by far (~85%), from a fall on a FLEXED, ADDUCTED knee with the foot INVERTED and PLANTARFLEXED. Classically described in parachutists, wrestlers, soccer and rugby players, and in horse riders.
    • Type III — POSTEROMEDIAL dislocation: rare, higher energy, and STRONGLY ASSOCIATED WITH PERONEAL NERVE INJURY.
    • Type IV — SUPERIOR dislocation, associated with a high-energy ankle injury and with a Maisonneuve-type mechanism.
  • Presentation and diagnosis — the great mimic.
    • Lateral knee pain with a prominent fibular head, worse with ankle motion (especially dorsiflexion) and direct pressure, and reproduced by knee flexion with ankle dorsiflexion and eversion.
    • It is frequently MISDIAGNOSED as a LATERAL MENISCAL TEAR or an LCL injury — the distinguishing features are the point of maximal tenderness over the fibular head, pain on ankle motion, and a normal meniscal examination.
    • Chronic instability presents with a sense of the fibular head "popping" and sometimes with transient peroneal symptoms.
    • Imaging: compare with the contralateral side. A true AP with about 45° of INTERNAL rotation of the knee profiles the joint best; CT is confirmatory and MRI shows associated soft-tissue injury and excludes the differential.
  • Treatment.
    • ACUTE DISLOCATION → CLOSED REDUCTION: flex the knee to 90° to RELAX the BICEPS FEMORIS and the LCL, evert and dorsiflex the foot, and apply direct pressure over the fibular head. Then immobilise for 2–3 weeks with protected weightbearing.
    • CHRONIC INSTABILITY → ANATOMIC LIGAMENT RECONSTRUCTION is preferred — a biceps femoris tenodesis or a free graft reconstruction of the anterior and posterior tibiofibular ligaments.
    • Historical options are now largely avoided: ARTHRODESIS of the joint causes ANKLE PAIN through loss of fibular rotation and load sharing, and FIBULAR HEAD RESECTION destabilises the LCL and biceps insertion and risks the peroneal nerve.
    • Type I atraumatic subluxation in a hypermobile adolescent is treated non-operatively with a supportive strap and physiotherapy; many settle with skeletal maturity.
  • The distractor to be aware of.
    • The proximal tibiofibular joint is NOT a stabiliser tested by VARUS STRESS at 20–30° of knee flexion — that test isolates the LCL and PLC.
    • Nor is the proximal tibiofibular joint part of the posterolateral corner proper; it appears in PLC component lists as a distractor, alongside the posterior oblique ligament (posteromedial) and the meniscofemoral ligaments.

Stingers, burners & cervical spine in athletes

The brachial plexus stinger and return-to-play decisions.

2 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S

  • What a stinger is, and its two mechanisms.
    • A transient UNILATERAL upper-limb burning dysaesthesia and weakness after a shoulder or neck impact, most often in football and rugby.
    • TRACTION on the upper trunk (C5–C6) — shoulder depression with contralateral neck side-bending. Typical in the YOUNGER athlete with a normal spine.
    • COMPRESSION of the exiting nerve root in the neuroforamen — neck EXTENSION with IPSILATERAL rotation and side-bending (a Spurling mechanism). Typical in the OLDER athlete with degenerative foraminal narrowing.
    • The distinction matters because the compression type recurs, is associated with structural narrowing, and warrants imaging.
  • The findings, and the red flag.
    • Symptoms are UNILATERAL and follow an UPPER TRUNK (C5–C6) pattern — deltoid, biceps, supraspinatus and infraspinatus weakness with lateral arm and thumb paraesthesia. A positive Spurling test supports the compressive mechanism.
    • BILATERAL symptoms, or ANY lower-limb involvement, is NOT a stinger. That is CERVICAL CORD NEURAPRAXIA / TRANSIENT QUADRIPARESIS and requires immediate cervical spine imaging and full work-up. This is the single most important discrimination.
    • Neck pain is NOT typical of a stinger — midline tenderness or restricted rotation should redirect you to the cervical spine.
    • Weakness may be delayed by hours to days, so an athlete cleared on the field must be re-examined.
  • Return to play after a stinger.
    • Same-game return is permitted ONLY if symptoms have COMPLETELY resolved, strength is FULL and SYMMETRIC, and the neck examination and range of motion are NORMAL.
    • Persistent symptoms, any weakness, or recurrent episodes mean removal from play and further evaluation: radiographs, MRI, and EMG if weakness persists beyond 2–3 weeks (EMG changes take about 3 weeks to appear).
    • Risk factors for recurrence: cervical FORAMINAL STENOSIS, a reduced TORG-PAVLOV RATIO (<0.8), cervical disc disease, previous stingers, and poor neck strength or tackling technique.
    • The Torg-Pavlov ratio is the sagittal canal diameter divided by the vertebral body diameter on a lateral radiograph; a ratio below 0.8 suggests developmental stenosis, but it has low positive predictive value in athletes with large vertebral bodies, so MRI (and specifically the presence of functional cerebrospinal fluid reserve around the cord) has largely replaced it for return-to-play decisions.
    • Recurrent stingers warrant MRI plus a change in technique, neck and periscapular strengthening, and protective equipment (a cervical roll or neck collar and properly fitted shoulder pads).
  • Cervical cord neurapraxia and transient quadriparesis.
    • BILATERAL sensory and/or motor symptoms in two or more limbs, lasting minutes to hours (occasionally up to 48 hours), typically after a hyperextension, hyperflexion or axial-load mechanism.
    • MRI is mandatory. CONTRAINDICATIONS to return to contact sport: congenital or acquired stenosis with loss of CSF reserve around the cord, CORD SIGNAL CHANGE (myelomalacia), instability, ligamentous injury, or more than one episode.
    • A single episode with a NORMAL MRI, no stenosis and full recovery MAY allow return after complete resolution — but the recurrence rate is meaningful and the decision is individualised and shared.
    • Klippel-Feil (multi-level congenital fusion), atlanto-axial instability, os odontoideum, and Down syndrome with C1–C2 instability are absolute contraindications to contact sport.
  • On-field management of the suspected cervical injury.
    • Any athlete with neck pain, midline tenderness, a neurological deficit or altered consciousness is managed with FULL SPINAL IMMOBILISATION and transfer.
    • LEAVE THE HELMET AND SHOULDER PADS ON in the field — removing one alone changes cervical alignment (the helmet alone extends the neck; the pads alone flex it). They are removed TOGETHER in a controlled setting with manual in-line stabilisation, unless the airway cannot be accessed, in which case the facemask is removed and the helmet retained.
    • Log-roll with in-line stabilisation onto a spine board, and transport to a centre with CT and MRI.

Biologics in sports medicine (PRP)

What platelet-rich plasma is and what the evidence shows.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • What PRP is — and what it is not.
    • Autologous plasma with a platelet concentration above baseline (typically 3–5×), delivering growth factors released from the platelet alpha granules: PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF, IGF-1, EGF and FGF.
    • PRP is NOT a stem cell therapy and contains very few (if any) mesenchymal stem cells — a common misconception and a standard distractor.
    • Nor is it "stem cell" bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) or adipose-derived cells; BMAC does contain a small MSC fraction, but even there the nucleated cell count is far higher than the true MSC count, and clinical evidence for both remains limited.
  • Preparations are not standardised — the reason the literature disagrees.
    • LEUKOCYTE-RICH (LR-PRP) versus LEUKOCYTE-POOR (LP-PRP), activated versus non-activated, and widely varying platelet concentration, red-cell content and volume.
    • Broadly: LP-PRP is favoured for INTRA-ARTICULAR (joint) injection, because leukocytes drive a catabolic and inflammatory synovial response; LR-PRP is favoured for TENDINOPATHY, where the inflammatory stimulus may be part of the mechanism.
    • Classification systems (PAW, DEPA, MARSPILL) exist precisely because trials that do not report preparation details cannot be compared or pooled. Any meta-analysis of PRP is limited by this heterogeneity — a legitimate methodological criticism to have ready.
  • What the evidence actually shows.
    • KNEE OSTEOARTHRITIS — the most consistent positive signal; several RCTs and meta-analyses show benefit over hyaluronic acid and saline at 6–12 months, although the largest and most rigorous recent trials (including the RESTORE trial) have been NEGATIVE. The honest position is that the effect, if real, is modest.
    • LATERAL EPICONDYLITIS and PATELLAR TENDINOPATHY — some positive studies, but inconsistent, and no clear superiority over placebo in the best-designed trials.
    • Weak or NEGATIVE: acute muscle injury, ACHILLES tendinopathy, and augmentation of ROTATOR CUFF repair or ACL reconstruction.
    • PRP does NOT improve healing in ACUTE ACHILLES RUPTURE — the PATH-2 randomised trial was clearly negative, and this is the most quotable single trial in the area.
  • Practical and ethical points.
    • Hold NSAIDs for roughly one week before and after injection — they inhibit platelet cyclo-oxygenase and therefore the growth-factor release the treatment depends on.
    • PRP is NOT covered by most public and private plans in Canada and is an out-of-pocket cost — which creates a real conflict-of-interest and informed-consent obligation when the treating surgeon also sells the injection.
    • Anti-doping: intramuscular PRP is currently PERMITTED under the WADA code (the prohibition was lifted in 2011), but formulations and adjuncts change, so check the current WADA Prohibited List for any competitive athlete.
    • Adverse events are rare — post-injection pain and flare, and (very rarely) infection.
    • Counsel honestly: a plausible biological rationale, low risk, unstandardised preparations, and modest and inconsistent effect sizes. Overselling an unproven autologous biologic to a paying patient is the specific professionalism trap.
  • The wider biologics landscape, briefly.
    • Corticosteroid — reliable short-term relief in OA and tendinopathy, but worse long-term outcomes in tendinopathy and, in the knee, repeated injections are associated with cartilage volume loss.
    • Hyaluronic acid — guideline bodies (including AAOS) do not recommend it routinely for knee OA; effect sizes are small.
    • BMAC and adipose-derived (SVF) therapies — marketed widely, but with sparse controlled evidence; Health Canada regulates minimally manipulated autologous cell products, and expanded-cell therapies require a clinical trial authorisation.
    • The professional obligation is the same across all of them: describe the evidence accurately, disclose cost and any financial interest, and do not present an experimental therapy as standard care.

Hamstring & pelvic apophyseal avulsion injuries

Avulsion injuries around the pelvis and hip.

1 of 7 sittings2021

  • Why the apophysis fails in the adolescent.
    • Before fusion, the APOPHYSIS is the WEAK LINK in the muscle-tendon-bone unit, so a violent eccentric contraction avulses bone rather than tearing tendon.
    • Classic sites and their muscles:
      • ISCHIAL TUBEROSITY — the HAMSTRINGS (the commonest and the most disabling).
      • ANTERIOR INFERIOR ILIAC SPINE (AIIS) — the DIRECT HEAD OF RECTUS FEMORIS.
      • ANTERIOR SUPERIOR ILIAC SPINE (ASIS) — SARTORIUS and TENSOR FASCIA LATA.
      • ILIAC CREST — the ABDOMINAL muscles.
      • LESSER TROCHANTER — ILIOPSOAS.
      • GREATER TROCHANTER — the ABDUCTORS.
    • Apophyses fuse in the late teens (ischial tuberosity last, around 20–25 years), which is why these injuries have an age window.
  • Working out the site from the mechanism — the actual examinable skill.
    • HIP FLEXED + KNEE EXTENDED maximally stretches the HAMSTRINGS → ISCHIAL TUBEROSITY (sprinting, hurdling, a cheerleading or gymnastics split).
    • HIP EXTENDED + KNEE FLEXED maximally stretches RECTUS FEMORIS → AIIS (kicking, the cocking phase of a kick).
    • SARTORIUS (ASIS) is the LEAST LIKELY to avulse with the hip FLEXED and the knee EXTENDED, because sartorius flexes the hip AND flexes the knee — that position SHORTENS it rather than lengthening it. This is the standard "least likely" answer.
    • Work through any such list by asking, for each muscle, whether the described position lengthens or shortens it.
  • The adult red flag.
    • A LESSER TROCHANTER AVULSION IN AN ADULT WITHOUT SIGNIFICANT TRAUMA IS A PATHOLOGICAL FRACTURE UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE — most often a metastasis.
    • Work-up: full-length femoral radiographs, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, bone scan, myeloma screen, and biopsy before any fixation.
    • The same logic applies to any low-energy avulsion around the pelvis in an adult with a skeletally mature apophysis.
  • Treatment of apophyseal avulsions.
    • Most are treated NON-OPERATIVELY — rest in a position of relaxation for the injured muscle, protected weightbearing, and graduated rehabilitation, with excellent results.
    • Operative fixation is considered for ISCHIAL TUBEROSITY avulsions displaced more than about 1.5–2 cm in an athlete, because non-union, a painful bony prominence when sitting, weakness, and SCIATIC NERVE irritation or tethering are recognised sequelae.
    • Symptomatic non-union or a hypertrophic callus mass can be excised and the hamstring reattached later, but the results are inferior to acute fixation.
  • Complete proximal hamstring avulsion in the adult.
    • The classic WATER-SKIING injury: sudden forced HIP FLEXION with the KNEE EXTENDED, giving a pop, posterior thigh ECCHYMOSIS, a palpable defect and a "cramping" weakness with sitting intolerance.
    • The proximal origin: the semimembranosus originates most laterally and anteriorly on the ischial tuberosity, with the conjoined semitendinosus/long head of biceps tendon posteromedial to it; the SCIATIC NERVE lies just LATERAL to the ischial tuberosity, about 1–2 cm away, and is the structure at risk in both the injury and the repair.
    • MRI defines the number of tendons involved and the retraction, which drive the decision.
    • SURGICAL REPAIR is recommended for COMPLETE two- or three-tendon avulsions with retraction (commonly >2 cm) in an active patient — it gives better strength, endurance and return to sport than non-operative treatment.
    • ACUTE repair (within about 4–6 weeks) is substantially better than CHRONIC repair: the chronic tendon is retracted, scarred to the sciatic nerve, and often needs neurolysis or graft augmentation.
    • Partial or one-tendon injuries and the low-demand patient are treated non-operatively.
  • Distinguish from the far commoner musculotendinous strain.
    • A hamstring musculotendinous strain (typically the biceps femoris long head at the proximal musculotendinous junction in a sprinter, or a semimembranosus "stretch-type" injury in a dancer) is treated with rehabilitation.
    • Eccentric strengthening — the NORDIC HAMSTRING programme — is the best-evidenced measure for preventing hamstring strain, roughly halving injury rates in team-sport athletes.
    • Prognostic features for a long recovery: proximal free-tendon involvement, a large cross-sectional area of injury, and a "stretch-type" mechanism. Previous injury is the strongest risk factor for recurrence.

Medial collateral ligament & posteromedial corner

The medial side of the knee.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • The three medial layers (Warren & Marshall).
    • LAYER I — the deep crural (sartorial) fascia, enveloping sartorius.
    • LAYER II — the SUPERFICIAL MCL, the medial patellofemoral ligament, and semimembranosus.
    • LAYER III — the deep capsular layer, containing the DEEP MCL (the meniscofemoral and meniscotibial ligaments) and the joint capsule.
    • The POSTERIOR OBLIQUE LIGAMENT (POL) lies at the junction of layers II and III posteriorly, where the two layers fuse.
    • Gracilis and semitendinosus run BETWEEN layers I and II — the plane in which they are harvested; the SAPHENOUS NERVE runs in the same plane, which is why its sartorial branch is injured during graft harvest.
  • What is — and is not — in the posteromedial corner.
    • The PMC comprises the POSTERIOR OBLIQUE LIGAMENT, the SEMIMEMBRANOSUS tendon and its five expansions, the OBLIQUE POPLITEAL LIGAMENT, the POSTEROMEDIAL CAPSULE, and the POSTERIOR HORN OF THE MEDIAL MENISCUS (with its meniscotibial attachment).
    • The SUPERFICIAL MCL is the primary valgus restraint but is conventionally considered SEPARATELY from the PMC proper.
    • NOT components of the posteromedial corner: the MEDIAL PATELLOFEMORAL LIGAMENT, the PES ANSERINUS (sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus), and the medial head of gastrocnemius — these three are the ones most often mistaken for PMC structures.
    • The POL is the key static structure; it is the primary restraint to internal rotation of the tibia and to valgus in full extension, and it is the structure whose loss produces anteromedial rotatory instability (AMRI).
  • Function and examination.
    • The SUPERFICIAL MCL is the primary restraint to VALGUS stress, tested at 30° of flexion, and it is also a secondary restraint to external rotation and anterior translation.
    • The POL and posteromedial capsule resist valgus in FULL EXTENSION and control anteromedial rotatory instability.
    • VALGUS LAXITY AT 0° (full extension) implies a COMBINED MCL + PMC injury, usually with a CRUCIATE injury — because in extension the cruciates and posterior capsule are secondary valgus restraints. Laxity at 30° alone is an isolated MCL injury.
    • Grading: I — tenderness, no laxity; II — 5–10 mm of opening with a firm endpoint; III — more than 10 mm with no endpoint.
    • ANTEROMEDIAL DRAWER (Slocum) test — anterior drawer with the foot externally rotated 15°; positive in AMRI.
    • Valgus stress radiographs quantify chronic laxity: more than about 3.2 mm of side-to-side medial gapping suggests a complete superficial MCL tear, and more than 9.8 mm a complete MCL + PMC injury.
  • Natural history — the most reliable non-operative result in orthopaedics.
    • ISOLATED MCL injuries — even GRADE III — heal reliably with NON-OPERATIVE treatment: a hinged brace, EARLY range of motion, and progressive weightbearing.
    • The MCL is extra-articular with an excellent blood supply, so it heals in a way the intra-articular ACL cannot.
    • Immobilisation is harmful — controlled motion improves collagen organisation and the strength of the healing ligament.
    • Expect some residual valgus laxity even in a well-healed grade III injury; laxity without symptoms does not require treatment.
  • When the MCL does need surgery.
    • A DISPLACED / ENTRAPPED distal MCL avulsion — the "STENER-LIKE" LESION, in which the torn distal MCL flips SUPERFICIAL to the PES ANSERINUS and cannot appose its footprint. This will not heal and requires open repair.
    • A BONY AVULSION from either the femur or tibia — fixed directly.
    • CHRONIC symptomatic valgus instability — reconstruction (an anatomic superficial MCL + POL reconstruction, or a semitendinosus-based technique).
    • A combined MULTILIGAMENT injury, and VALGUS LAXITY IN FULL EXTENSION with an associated PMC injury.
    • Correct VALGUS MALALIGNMENT (with a distal femoral varus-producing osteotomy) before or with a chronic medial-sided reconstruction, on exactly the same principle as varus and the PLC.
    • A femoral-sided MCL tear that heals with an ossified mass at the medial epicondyle is PELLEGRINI-STIEDA disease.
  • The combined ACL-MCL injury.
    • The standard approach: let the MCL HEAL FIRST with bracing and rehabilitation, then reconstruct the ACL at 4–6 weeks once valgus stability and full motion are restored.
    • This staged approach reduces ARTHROFIBROSIS compared with acute combined surgery, which is the specific reason for the delay.
    • Exceptions that force earlier medial surgery: a Stener-like lesion, a bony avulsion, or gross valgus laxity in full extension.
    • If the MCL has not stabilised by 6 weeks, address it at the time of ACL reconstruction rather than reconstructing the ACL into a valgus-lax knee.

Patellar tendinopathy & jumper's knee

Diagnosis and the evidence for treatment.

1 of 7 sittings2022

  • Presentation and examination.
    • Anterior knee pain localised to the INFERIOR POLE OF THE PATELLA, provoked by jumping, landing and deceleration — volleyball, basketball and high-jump athletes.
    • Tenderness is maximal at the DEEP POSTERIOR aspect of the PROXIMAL patellar tendon at the inferior pole, with the knee EXTENDED, and DECREASES with the knee FLEXED (BASSETT'S SIGN) — the flexed tendon tightens over the pole and the tender area becomes inaccessible.
    • The single-leg decline squat reproduces the pain and is used both diagnostically and as the rehabilitation exercise.
    • Blazina staging: I pain after activity only; II pain at the start of activity that resolves with warm-up and returns with fatigue; III pain during and after activity, impairing performance; IV tendon rupture.
  • It is a tendinosis.
    • The pathology is TENDINOSIS — angiofibroblastic degeneration with NEOVASCULARISATION, mucoid ground substance and disorganised collagen — NOT inflammation. Histology shows an absence of inflammatory cells.
    • This is why anti-inflammatory strategies underperform and mechanical loading strategies work.
    • Imaging (ultrasound or MRI) shows thickening and increased signal in the proximal DEEP fibres, sometimes with neovascularity on Doppler.
    • Imaging findings correlate POORLY with symptoms and are common in ASYMPTOMATIC athletes — treat the patient, not the scan.
  • Treatment: loading is the therapy.
    • ECCENTRIC strengthening — specifically a DECLINE-BOARD SINGLE-LEG SQUAT programme (25° decline) — has the best evidence and is the treatment of choice.
    • HEAVY SLOW RESISTANCE training is at least as effective and is better tolerated, and is increasingly the first-line prescription.
    • Both outperform concentric exercise, rest, ultrasound and most passive modalities. Rest alone makes it worse — the tendon needs load to remodel.
    • Expect a 12-week minimum programme; pain during the exercise up to a tolerable level is acceptable and expected.
  • What not to do, and second-line options.
    • CORTICOSTEROID INJECTION IS CONTRAINDICATED — it gives short-term relief but weakens the tendon, risks RUPTURE, and produces worse long-term outcomes. This is the same pattern as lateral epicondylitis and Achilles tendinopathy.
    • Second-line: extracorporeal shockwave therapy, sclerosing (polidocanol) injections, and PRP — all with inconsistent evidence and none clearly superior to a well-executed loading programme.
    • SURGERY — open or arthroscopic débridement of the degenerate tissue at the inferior pole, ± excision of the distal pole ± longitudinal tenotomy — is reserved for at least 6 months of FAILED STRUCTURED ECCENTRIC REHABILITATION. It gives roughly 80% good results, with a long (6–12 month) return to sport, and open and arthroscopic techniques give comparable outcomes.
  • Address the contributing factors, or it recurs.
    • Decreased ANKLE DORSIFLEXION (which increases patellar tendon load on landing), quadriceps and hamstring inflexibility, poor landing mechanics (a stiff, knee-dominant landing), training volume and frequency, and a hard playing surface.
    • Intrinsic factors: greater jump height and landing force (paradoxically, the better jumpers get it), higher body mass, and patella alta.
    • Rehabilitation must include hip and trunk control and landing retraining, not just the tendon itself.
  • The differential to keep in mind.
    • SINDING-LARSEN-JOHANSSON disease — a traction apophysitis of the INFERIOR pole of the patella in a child (10–13), self-limiting.
    • OSGOOD-SCHLATTER disease — traction apophysitis of the TIBIAL TUBERCLE (11–15), also self-limiting; a persistent painful ossicle in an adult may be excised.
    • Fat pad (Hoffa's) impingement, patellofemoral pain, a bipartite patella (usually superolateral, type III), and quadriceps tendinopathy at the superior pole.
    • PATELLAR TENDON RUPTURE — a palpable defect, patella alta on the lateral radiograph, and an inability to straight-leg raise; it requires urgent repair, and is more common in the tendinopathic, previously injected or previously operated tendon.

  • The rest of the racquet-sport injury list.
    • Shoulder — rotator cuff tendinopathy and INTERNAL IMPINGEMENT from serving; the late-cocking position of maximal abduction and external rotation is the provocative phase, with the same GIRD/SICK-scapula spectrum as throwers.
    • Elbow — lateral and medial epicondylitis; in adolescents, medial epicondyle apophysitis.
    • Wrist and hand — ECU TENDINOPATHY and ECU SUBLUXATION (the two-handed backhand and the western grip), and the HOOK OF HAMATE FRACTURE from the butt of the racquet, which presents with ulnar-sided palmar pain, is missed on standard radiographs and needs a CARPAL TUNNEL VIEW or CT, threatens FDP rupture to the small finger and ulnar nerve symptoms, and is usually treated by EXCISION of the hook fragment in a symptomatic non-union.
    • Trunk and lower limb — rectus abdominis and oblique strains from serving; calf and Achilles injury, ankle sprain, patellar tendinopathy and stress fractures.
  • Equipment and technique modifications that actually reduce load.
    • A LARGER grip size, LOWER string tension, a MORE FLEXIBLE and LIGHTER racquet, and a LARGER head size all reduce transmitted vibration and peak force at the elbow.
    • Technique correction — a TWO-HANDED backhand, avoiding a leading elbow, and generating force from the trunk and legs rather than the wrist — is at least as important as equipment.
    • A counterforce brace gives short-term symptom relief but does not change the natural history.
  • Return-to-sport principles, applicable across every sport.
    • Full PAIN-FREE range of motion.
    • Restored STRENGTH — typically a LIMB SYMMETRY INDEX of at least 90%.
    • Restored NEUROMUSCULAR CONTROL and sport-specific FUNCTIONAL testing — a battery of hop tests (single, triple, crossover and 6-metre timed hop).
    • Completion of a graduated, sport-specific progression (an interval throwing programme, a running progression, contact drills before contact play).
    • PSYCHOLOGICAL READINESS, measured with a validated tool such as the ACL-RSI — fear of re-injury is one of the strongest predictors of failure to return.
    • TIME ALONE IS NOT AN ADEQUATE CRITERION. This is the central modern principle.
  • The ACL return-to-sport numbers, because they get quoted.
    • Delaying return to pivoting sport to at least 9 MONTHS, and achieving strength symmetry, substantially reduces re-injury risk — in the Delaware-Oslo cohort, each month of delay up to 9 months reduced re-injury by about 50%, and meeting the return-to-sport criteria reduced re-injury by roughly 84%.
    • Only about two-thirds of athletes return to their pre-injury level, and around 55% return to competitive sport — a counselling point that matters more than most patients expect.
    • The CONTRALATERAL ACL is at comparable or greater risk than the graft in young athletes returning to pivoting sport.
    • Neuromuscular PREVENTION programmes (FIFA 11+, PEP, and similar) reduce ACL injury rates by roughly a third to a half in female team-sport athletes, and are the highest-yield intervention available.
  • The young athlete: specialisation and load.
    • EARLY SPORT SPECIALISATION and excessive TRAINING VOLUME are the strongest MODIFIABLE risk factors for overuse injury in the skeletally immature.
    • Practical guidance from AAP/AOSSM/POSNA-aligned consensus statements: limit organised sport to fewer hours per week than the athlete's age in YEARS, take at least ONE OR TWO DAYS OFF PER WEEK, and take 2–3 MONTHS OFF PER YEAR from any single sport.
    • Sport-specific load rules where they exist — most notably PITCH COUNTS and mandatory rest days in youth baseball — are evidence-based and reduce elbow and shoulder injury.
    • Also screen for the FEMALE ATHLETE TRIAD / RELATIVE ENERGY DEFICIENCY IN SPORT (RED-S): low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction and low bone mineral density, which presents as recurrent stress fractures and requires a multidisciplinary (not orthopaedic-only) response.
