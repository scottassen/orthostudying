Rotator cuff tears: Natural history, repair indications & healing

What predicts a tendon will heal, who actually benefits from repair, and the options once the tear is irreparable.

7 of 7 sittings2020 (x4), 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025 (x2)

  • Factors predicting poor healing and a worse outcome after repair — the most consistently tested list:
    • Increasing AGE — the strongest single factor (retear rates rise steeply beyond 65).
    • Larger tear size and greater retraction (Patte grade).
    • Higher GOUTALLIER fatty infiltration grade — grade 3–4 is the strongest structural predictor of failure.
    • Muscle atrophy — the Warner classification and the tangent sign (the supraspinatus belly falling below a line drawn across the scapular spine and superior coracoid on a sagittal-oblique image).
    • Chronicity, smoking, diabetes, osteoporosis (poor anchor purchase) and workers' compensation status.
    • Fatty infiltration is IRREVERSIBLE and does NOT improve after repair — it may be arrested, but it does not recover. This is the key biological point.
  • The Goutallier grades, for reference:
    • 0 — no fat. 1 — some fatty streaks. 2 — more muscle than fat. 3 — equal fat and muscle. 4 — more fat than muscle.
    • Originally described on CT, now applied to the sagittal-oblique T1 MRI at the level of the scapular Y.
    • Grade ≥3 is the threshold beyond which repair is unlikely to heal or to restore function.
  • When to repair early:
    • An ACUTE TRAUMATIC tear in a young or active patient, especially with a sudden loss of function.
    • An acute tear with PSEUDOPARALYSIS.
    • A SUBSCAPULARIS tear — it retracts and fattens quickly, and the results of late repair are poor.
    • A tear associated with a shoulder DISLOCATION in a patient over 40 — a first dislocation over 40 is more likely to tear the cuff than to recur.
    • Chronic, degenerative, small tears in older patients can be managed non-operatively first.
  • Natural history and the physiotherapy-versus-repair evidence:
    • For a medium-sized degenerative tear in a middle-aged patient, multiple RCTs show structured physiotherapy achieves good outcomes in a majority.
    • Repair reduces tear PROGRESSION and gives modestly better pain and function scores at 1–2 years; the difference is real but not large.
    • Non-operatively managed tears progress in size in roughly half of patients over 2–5 years, and progression correlates with the development of symptoms.
    • So the honest framing is: physiotherapy is a legitimate first treatment for a degenerative tear, but a tear that is going to be repaired is better repaired before it enlarges and fattens.
    • Asymptomatic tears are common — prevalence rises from ~10% at 50 to over 50% at 80 — so an incidental tear on MRI is not automatically the pain generator.
  • Signs of a massive or irreparable tear:
    • Acromiohumeral interval <7 mm (normal 7–14 mm).
    • Superior escape / static superior migration of the humeral head.
    • Goutallier 3–4 fatty infiltration.
    • Positive tangent sign.
    • Tear retracted to the glenoid (Patte 3).
    • Pseudoparalysis — the inability to actively elevate above 90° with full passive motion and no neurologic deficit.
    • PRESERVED ACTIVE ELEVATION is NOT a sign of irreparability — the classic "all of these are signs except".
  • Treating the massive irreparable tear — match the option to the patient:
    • Massive irreparable tear with PSEUDOPARALYSIS in an elderly patient → REVERSE total shoulder arthroplasty — the most predictable treatment, and the only one that reliably restores overhead elevation.
    • Superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) — best for an irreparable SUPRASPINATUS tear with an intact or repairable SUBSCAPULARIS and a functioning TERES MINOR, no arthritis, and no static superior escape. It restores the superior restraint rather than the force couple.
    • Tendon transfer for posterosuperior tears — latissimus dorsi (classically) or lower trapezius (increasingly favoured, more in line with the infraspinatus vector). Best in a young patient with preserved passive motion and an intact subscapularis.
    • Pectoralis major transfer for an irreparable subscapularis tear (subcoracoid routing is more anatomic).
    • Partial repair to restore the force couples, débridement with biceps tenotomy for pain relief in a low-demand patient, and the subacromial balloon spacer.
    • The intact anterior and posterior force couples (subscapularis versus infraspinatus/teres minor) are what allow a partial repair to work.
  • Why TERES MINOR matters — a repeatedly tested point:
    • It is the main remaining EXTERNAL ROTATOR in a massive posterosuperior tear, and it is often hypertrophied in that setting.
    • It is critical to a good outcome after REVERSE arthroplasty, because the reverse restores elevation but does nothing for external rotation.
    • Fatty infiltration of teres minor predicts a POOR result from rTSA — persistent external rotation lag and a positive HORNBLOWER sign.
    • It may warrant a concurrent latissimus dorsi / teres major transfer at the time of the reverse.
    • It is innervated by the axillary nerve (unlike infraspinatus, which is suprascapular) — so it can be spared in a suprascapular neuropathy and lost in an axillary one.
  • Complications of arthroscopic repair:
    • The commonest complication is RETEAR / failure of healing — structural failure in 20–90% depending on tear size and age.
    • Importantly, a structurally failed repair still often gives good pain relief and patient satisfaction — structural and clinical outcomes are not the same thing.
    • Stiffness is next, particularly after a small tear repaired in a diabetic.
    • Nerve injury and infection (Cutibacterium acnes) are uncommon.
    • Technique factors that improve healing: a double-row/transosseous-equivalent construct for larger tears, tension-free repair, footprint preparation, and protecting the repair for 6 weeks.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: Indications, biomechanics & complications

The Grammont principle and its modern modifications — and how each component position choice trades one complication for another.

6 of 7 sittings2020 (x4), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • The Grammont biomechanical principle:
    • MEDIALISE and DISTALISE the centre of rotation.
    • Medialisation INCREASES the DELTOID MOMENT ARM and recruits more anterior and posterior deltoid fibres into elevation.
    • Distalisation TENSIONS the deltoid, restoring its length–tension relationship in a shoulder where the humerus has migrated superiorly.
    • Medialisation also reduces shear at the glenoid baseplate, which is what made a fixed glenoid component viable at all.
    • The semi-constrained articulation converts the DELTOID into the prime mover, compensating for an absent cuff.
    • The trade-off Grammont accepted: loss of rotation and scapular notching — which the modern lateralised designs try to recover.
  • Scapular notching — erosion of the inferior scapular neck by the humeral component in adduction:
    • REDUCED by: inferior placement of the glenosphere with inferior overhang; inferior TILT of the baseplate; a LARGER glenosphere; a LATERALISED glenosphere or bony increased-offset (BIO-RSA); and a more VALGUS (135°) humeral neck-shaft angle.
    • INCREASED by superior placement with superior tilt — which also raises the risk of baseplate failure by loading it in shear.
    • Notching is graded by Sirveaux (1–4); higher grades correlate with worse outcome scores and with polyethylene wear and osteolysis.
  • Lateralisation — what it buys and what it costs:
    • Improves ROTATION, reduces notching, and improves the wrapping and tension of the remaining cuff (restoring the deltoid wrapping effect and a more normal shoulder contour).
    • But increases stress at the BASEPLATE–BONE interface (a cantilever effect) and on the ACROMION, raising the risk of acromial and scapular spine fracture.
    • And increases deltoid tension, which can cause pain and, at the extreme, neurologic traction injury.
    • The current consensus favours modest glenoid lateralisation with humeral distalisation — distalise on the humeral side, lateralise on the glenoid side.
  • Instability — the commonest early complication:
    • Contributing factors: subscapularis deficiency; insufficient deltoid tension (under-lengthening); component malposition (excessive humeral retroversion, a superiorly placed glenosphere); a lateral offset humeral tray; soft-tissue impingement; and a poor or denervated deltoid.
    • Measures that improve stability: increasing the glenosphere DIAMETER; inferior positioning; adequate deltoid tension/lengthening; a more constrained/deeper humeral liner; and repairing the subscapularis where possible.
    • Glenosphere LATERALISATION is PROTECTIVE against instability, not causative — a common "which is NOT associated with instability" item.
    • Other risk factors: revision surgery, rTSA for fracture or tumour, male sex, higher BMI, and a previous failed instability procedure.
  • Acromial and scapular spine stress fractures:
    • Occur in roughly 1–10%, and are classified by Levy (type I anterior to the AC joint; type II posterior, involving the acromion; type III involving the scapular spine).
    • Risk factors: osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, female sex, greater lengthening/distalisation, excessive lateralisation, and superior baseplate screw placement penetrating the scapular spine (a technical, avoidable cause).
    • Presentation is NEW pain and LOSS OF ELEVATION months to years after a successful arthroplasty — the classic delayed post-rTSA scenario.
    • Diagnosis is by CT (they are frequently invisible on plain films); most are treated non-operatively in a sling, with fair results, though function rarely returns to the pre-fracture level.
  • Acromial stress and tuberosity healing:
    • Stress on the acromion in abduction is DECREASED by reducing lateralisation and by avoiding excessive distalisation.
    • Healing of the TUBEROSITIES in a reverse done for FRACTURE specifically improves EXTERNAL ROTATION (and functional outcome scores).
    • This is why anatomic tuberosity repair is emphasised in fracture arthroplasty, even though the reverse is far less dependent on the tuberosities than a hemiarthroplasty.
    • Use a fracture-specific stem, cement in the correct height and version, and repair the tuberosities to each other and to the stem with heavy non-absorbable suture plus bone graft.
  • The rest of the complication profile:
    • Infection — higher than in anatomic TSA; C. acnes predominates, and men are at higher risk.
    • Neurologic injury — usually a traction neurapraxia of the brachial plexus from lengthening; most recover.
    • Baseplate loosening, humeral loosening (uncommon), and polyethylene wear from notching.
    • Haematoma in the large dead space, and periprosthetic fracture.
    • Overall revision rates are now comparable to anatomic TSA, and indications have expanded well beyond cuff arthropathy — to fracture, fracture sequelae, revision arthroplasty, tumour reconstruction, and even primary osteoarthritis with a B2/B3 glenoid.

Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty: Glenoid, subscapularis & outcomes

Why the glenoid is the weak link, the Walch morphology that drives the plan, and the technical errors that cause early failure.

6 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F (x2)

  • The best glenoid component — a directly asked question:
    • The ALL-POLYETHYLENE, CEMENTED, PEGGED glenoid has the best long-term survivorship and the lowest revision rate in registry and clinical data.
    • Pegged designs OUTPERFORM keeled.
    • METAL-BACKED glenoids have a substantially HIGHER failure rate — polyethylene dissociation, backside wear and osteolysis — and have largely been abandoned.
    • Hybrid and cementless pegged designs are being studied but do not yet displace the all-polyethylene cemented pegged component as the answer.
  • Glenoid loosening is the commonest cause of failure of an anatomic TSA:
    • Radiolucent lines are common and do not necessarily indicate loosening — progression matters more than presence.
    • Key risk factors: eccentric (posterior) wear; retroversion; an unbalanced/eccentric load; and inadequate bone preparation (poor cement interdigitation, thermal necrosis from over-reaming).
    • The "ROCKING HORSE" phenomenon — eccentric loading rocks the component, producing tension on one side and compression on the other, and progressively loosens it.
  • Why anatomic TSA is contraindicated in cuff arthropathy:
    • The unopposed superior pull of the DELTOID produces superior migration of the humeral head.
    • That causes eccentric SUPERIOR loading of the glenoid, generating the rocking-horse mechanism.
    • The result is early glenoid loosening — and this is precisely the reason a REVERSE is used instead.
    • The same logic makes an irreparable subscapularis tear a relative contraindication (anterior escape).
  • Walch classification of glenoid morphology:
    • A1 — concentric, minimal erosion. A2 — concentric with deep central (medial) erosion.
    • B1 — posterior joint-space narrowing with sclerosis and osteophytes, no biconcavity.
    • B2 — BICONCAVE, with a posteriorly worn "neoglenoid".
    • B3 — MONOCONCAVE with posterior wear and >15° retroversion or >70% posterior humeral head subluxation.
    • C — DYSPLASTIC, retroversion >25°.
    • D — ANTEVERTED or with anterior subluxation.
    • B2 and B3 are the technical challenge. Options: eccentric (high-side) reaming — limited, because it loses bone and medialises the joint line; AUGMENTED glenoid components; bone grafting; and REVERSE arthroplasty, which is increasingly the answer for severe B2/B3 and C glenoids.
    • Correcting version to within roughly 10° without medialising more than a few millimetres is the practical target.
  • Improving glenoid exposure — and the manoeuvre that does NOT help:
    • Complete INFERIOR CAPSULAR release (staying on bone to protect the axillary nerve).
    • Release of the subscapularis with the anterior capsule.
    • Resect the humeral head FIRST.
    • Remove inferior humeral and glenoid osteophytes.
    • Adequate retractor placement (a Fukuda or posterior glenoid retractor levering the head posteriorly).
    • Release/mobilise the long head of biceps and the superior capsule; tenodese the biceps.
    • EXCESSIVE EXTERNAL ROTATION of the arm actually WORSENS exposure — INTERNAL rotation and adduction with posterior levering deliver the glenoid. This is the "all of the following help EXCEPT" answer.
  • Subscapularis management — three techniques, no clear winner:
    • Lesser tuberosity OSTEOTOMY, subscapularis PEEL and subscapularis TENOTOMY give comparable clinical outcomes in randomised and comparative studies.
    • The lesser tuberosity osteotomy heals BONE-TO-BONE and may improve belly-press and lift-off strength, but no technique is clearly superior.
    • Whichever is used: protect the repair for 6 weeks, avoid active internal rotation, and avoid passive external rotation beyond the intra-operative safe zone.
    • Subscapularis failure presents with anterior pain, weak lift-off/belly-press, increased passive external rotation, and eventually anterior escape.
  • Overstuffing — the technical error and its consequences:
    • Oversizing the humeral head, or offsetting it anteriorly, OVERSTUFFS the joint.
    • It over-tensions the anterior soft tissues.
    • It DECREASES EXTERNAL ROTATION.
    • It increases the joint reaction force.
    • It risks SUBSCAPULARIS FAILURE.
    • And it accelerates GLENOID WEAR.
    • The head should restore the anatomic centre of rotation and allow roughly 50% posterior translation with the joint reduced; the head–neck resection should follow the anatomic neck.
  • Outcomes and the young patient:
    • Anatomic TSA is a reliable operation for primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis with an intact cuff, with better pain relief and function than hemiarthroplasty and better survivorship.
    • In the young patient (<55–60) with an intact cuff, glenoid survivorship falls and revision rates climb — options include arthroscopic débridement with capsular release (CAM procedure), ream-and-run (humeral resurfacing/hemiarthroplasty with non-prosthetic glenoid arthroplasty), and biologic resurfacing (largely abandoned).
    • Counsel a young manual worker that an anatomic TSA is unlikely to be their last operation.

Anterior shoulder instability & glenoid bone loss

Who will re-dislocate, how to measure the bone loss, and the thresholds that move you from a soft-tissue to a bone procedure.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025 (x2)

  • Risk factors for recurrence after a first-time anterior dislocation:
    • YOUNG AGE — the strongest; under 20 years carries up to 80–90% recurrence.
    • Male sex.
    • Contact/collision or overhead sport.
    • Ligamentous laxity / hyperlaxity.
    • Glenoid or humeral BONE LOSS.
    • A first dislocation over the age of 40 is more likely to be associated with a ROTATOR CUFF TEAR than with recurrence — examine the cuff and image it rather than focusing on stabilisation.
    • The ISIS (Instability Severity Index Score) formalises this — a score >6 predicts failure of arthroscopic Bankart repair and favours a Latarjet.
  • Assessing glenoid bone loss:
    • The best predictor of glenoid bone loss is the NUMBER of dislocation episodes — bone loss is progressive with each event, which is the argument for early stabilisation in a young athlete.
    • 3D CT with the humeral head digitally subtracted is the gold standard, using the best-fit circle method on the en-face view (the inferior two-thirds of the glenoid is a true circle).
    • MRI is reasonable but less precise; it does add labral and cuff assessment.
    • Compare with the contralateral side where the loss is borderline.
  • The GLENOID TRACK concept (Yamamoto / Di Giacomo):
    • The glenoid track ≈ 83% of the glenoid diameter MINUS the glenoid bone loss.
    • It is the zone of contact between the humeral head and the glenoid through the arc of abduction and external rotation.
    • If the Hill-Sachs interval EXCEEDS the track it is "OFF-TRACK" (engaging) and must be addressed.
    • If it lies within the track it is "ON-TRACK" and a soft-tissue repair alone can succeed.
    • The concept matters because glenoid and humeral bone loss are BIPOLAR and interact — a small Hill-Sachs becomes engaging once the glenoid is eroded.
  • Treatment algorithm:
    • <13–15% glenoid bone loss with an ON-TRACK Hill-Sachs → arthroscopic BANKART repair.
    • 13–20% (subcritical) with an OFF-TRACK lesion → Bankart plus REMPLISSAGE, or a bone procedure.
    • >20–25% ("critical") → BONE BLOCK procedure — Latarjet/coracoid transfer, or free bone graft (distal tibial allograft, iliac crest).
    • Remplissage (filling the Hill-Sachs with the infraspinatus tendon and posterior capsule) converts an off-track lesion to on-track; it costs a small amount of external rotation.
    • Revision after a failed Bankart, and a contact athlete with subcritical loss, both push toward a bone procedure.
  • How the Latarjet works — the three mechanisms:
    • The BONE BLOCK increases the glenoid arc (restores the articular surface).
    • The SLING effect of the conjoint tendon across the inferior subscapularis in abduction and external rotation — often the dominant mechanism.
    • Repair of the CAPSULE to the CA ligament stump, restoring a static restraint and keeping the graft extra-articular.
    • Risks: neurovascular injury (musculocutaneous and axillary nerves), graft nonunion or resorption, hardware problems, and later arthritis (particularly with a laterally overhanging graft).
    • Recurrence after Latarjet is low (~5%), but the complication rate is higher than for a Bankart and revision is difficult.
  • Bone grafting options — and the allograft point:
    • Distal tibial OSTEOARTICULAR ALLOGRAFT is an alternative for revision or a large defect — it restores an ARTICULAR CARTILAGE surface with a matching radius of curvature.
    • FRESH-FROZEN (non-irradiated) allograft is preferred over IRRADIATED allograft.
    • Irradiation (particularly >25 kGy) degrades collagen cross-links, reduces mechanical strength and impairs incorporation, with higher graft failure rates.
    • Fresh-frozen grafts retain better biomechanical properties, at a theoretically higher but still very low disease-transmission risk.
    • Iliac crest autograft (Eden-Hybinette) remains an option, without the articular surface.
  • Posterior instability — a different disease:
    • Far less common than anterior (roughly 5% of instability).
    • Usually atraumatic and repetitive — the blocking lineman, the bench press — or from a SEIZURE or ELECTROCUTION.
    • It presents with PAIN rather than frank instability, which is why it is so often missed.
    • Associated with glenoid RETROVERSION and DYSPLASIA, and a posterior labral (reverse Bankart) tear.
    • The mechanism of a traumatic posterior dislocation is a fall on an outstretched, FLEXED, ADDUCTED and INTERNALLY ROTATED arm.
    • The AXILLARY LATERAL view is what makes the diagnosis of a locked posterior dislocation; the AP shows the "light bulb" sign and loss of the normal elliptical overlap.

Glenoid dysplasia & shoulder examination

A developmental posteroinferior deficiency that predisposes to posterior problems — and the physical and radiographic examination that finds shoulder pathology.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2023 (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Glenoid dysplasia (glenoid hypoplasia) — the anatomy:
    • A developmental deficiency of the POSTEROINFERIOR glenoid, from failure of ossification of the inferior glenoid epiphysis.
    • Produces increased RETROVERSION and a rounded "LAZY J" or "DELTA"-shaped posteroinferior glenoid.
    • A HYPERTROPHIED POSTERIOR LABRUM partially compensates, filling the deficiency.
    • It is often BILATERAL and frequently an INCIDENTAL finding — always image the other side.
  • Clinical significance:
    • Most patients are ASYMPTOMATIC or minimally symptomatic.
    • But it predisposes to POSTERIOR INSTABILITY and to early POSTERIOR glenohumeral arthritis — the Walch C glenoid.
    • Management is NON-OPERATIVE in the first instance — posterior capsular and rotator cuff strengthening, scapular control, activity modification.
    • Posterior bone block or glenoid osteotomy is reserved for symptomatic recurrent posterior instability and carries a HIGH complication rate (graft resorption, nonunion, iatrogenic anterior instability, neurovascular injury).
    • When arthritis supervenes in a young patient with a dysplastic retroverted glenoid, an anatomic TSA is technically very difficult and a reverse is often chosen.
  • Subscapularis examination:
    • LIFT-OFF test (hand off the back, actively lift away) — assesses the LOWER subscapularis and is the most SPECIFIC.
    • BELLY-PRESS test — assesses the UPPER subscapularis, and is used when internal rotation is limited and the hand cannot be placed behind the back.
    • BEAR-HUG test — the most SENSITIVE for an upper subscapularis tear.
    • Increased PASSIVE EXTERNAL ROTATION compared with the other side is an indirect sign of subscapularis deficiency.
  • The rest of the cuff, and the biceps:
    • Supraspinatus — Jobe / empty can, and the drop-arm sign.
    • Infraspinatus and teres minor — the external rotation LAG sign, and the HORNBLOWER sign (inability to hold the arm in external rotation at 90° abduction), which indicates TERES MINOR deficiency.
    • Biceps and SLAP — Speed, Yergason, O'Brien active compression, and the biceps load test.
    • The most sensitive COMBINATION for a biceps/labral lesion is a positive O'Brien plus a Speed or biceps load test — no single test is adequate, and combinations improve accuracy. This is the recurring point.
  • Shoulder radiographic views — which view answers which question:
    • True AP (Grashey) — taken 30–45° oblique to the coronal plane; shows the glenohumeral joint space in profile and is the view for arthritis and joint-space narrowing.
    • AXILLARY LATERAL — essential to assess dislocation direction, glenoid version and posterior subluxation. It is the view most often omitted and the one that identifies a LOCKED POSTERIOR DISLOCATION. If the patient cannot abduct, get a Velpeau axillary.
    • Scapular Y — acromial morphology (Bigliani type I flat, II curved, III hooked) and dislocation direction.
    • STRYKER NOTCH view — shows a HILL-SACHS lesion.
    • WEST POINT view — shows anteroinferior GLENOID BONE LOSS.
    • Also useful: the Garth (apical oblique) for both bone lesions, and the Zanca (15° cephalic tilt) for the AC joint.
  • Instability symptoms without an unstable joint:
    • A patient can report a "slipping" or "giving way" sensation WITHOUT a structurally unstable glenohumeral joint.
    • Causes: biceps tendinitis and SLAP lesions; rotator cuff pathology; suprascapular nerve impingement; and scapular dyskinesis or long thoracic nerve palsy.
    • Also consider voluntary/positional instability and generalised hyperlaxity (check the Beighton score), where surgery is contraindicated and rehabilitation is the treatment.
    • Always examine the cervical spine and the scapula from behind before concluding the problem is glenohumeral.

Acromioclavicular joint injury

Which ligament resists which direction, the Rockwood grades, and the Canadian trial that settled type III.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024S

  • The stabilisers — which ligament resists which direction:
    • The AC LIGAMENTS — especially the SUPERIOR and POSTERIOR AC capsule — are the primary restraints to ANTERIOR–POSTERIOR (HORIZONTAL) translation.
    • The CORACOCLAVICULAR ligaments are the primary restraints to SUPERIOR (VERTICAL) translation.
    • The CONOID ligament — medial, from the posteromedial base of the coracoid to the conoid tubercle — is the principal restraint to SUPERIOR/vertical displacement.
    • The TRAPEZOID — lateral, more anterior — resists AXIAL COMPRESSION and contributes to horizontal stability.
    • Normal coracoclavicular distance is 11–13 mm; the deltotrapezial fascia is the additional restraint that fails in higher grades.
  • Rockwood classification:
    • I — AC ligament sprain, no displacement.
    • II — AC ligaments torn, CC ligaments INTACT; up to 25% displacement / widened AC joint.
    • III — both torn, with 25–100% superior displacement of the clavicle.
    • IV — POSTERIOR displacement into or through the trapezius. The AXILLARY LATERAL view makes the diagnosis — it is invisible on the AP.
    • V — >100% displacement (>2× the normal CC distance), with deltotrapezial fascia disruption.
    • VI — INFERIOR / subcoracoid displacement (rare, high-energy).
    • Type III is subdivided into IIIA (stable) and IIIB (with scapular dyskinesis and persistent symptoms).
  • Who gets operated on:
    • Types I and II are treated NON-OPERATIVELY.
    • Types IV, V and VI are generally treated OPERATIVELY.
    • Type III is controversial — and is what gets tested.
  • The Canadian trial that settled type III:
    • The Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society (COTS) multicentre RCT for acute complete AC dislocation (types III–V) compared hook plate fixation with non-operative treatment.
    • Hook plate fixation produced BETTER RADIOGRAPHIC ALIGNMENT.
    • But NO clinically important difference in DASH or Constant scores at ONE YEAR.
    • MORE complications and reoperations (hardware removal) in the surgical group.
    • Non-operative patients returned to work SOONER.
    • (The non-operative group did have significantly better early DASH and Constant scores at 6 weeks and 3 months.)
  • What that means in practice:
    • The modern approach to type III is INITIAL NON-OPERATIVE treatment, with DELAYED reconstruction for the minority who remain symptomatic.
    • Crucially, the outcomes of delayed reconstruction are NOT compromised by an initial trial of non-operative care — so there is nothing lost by waiting.
    • Type IIIB, with scapular dyskinesis or persistent symptoms, is more likely to need surgery.
    • Consider earlier surgery in a heavy manual worker or an overhead athlete whose symptoms persist beyond 3–6 months.
  • Surgical options:
    • Anatomic CC reconstruction with a TENDON GRAFT — the modified Weaver-Dunn (CA ligament transfer, now largely superseded) or free graft reconstruction of the conoid and trapezoid through clavicular and coracoid tunnels.
    • Cortical button / suspensory devices — less soft-tissue dissection, no removal required, but a real rate of loss of reduction and of coracoid or clavicle fracture through the tunnels.
    • Hook plate — temporary; requires removal, with a risk of acromial erosion and subacromial impingement.
    • Address HORIZONTAL instability by reconstructing or imbricating the AC capsule — persistent horizontal instability is a common cause of a poor result despite a good CC reduction on the AP film.
    • Loss of reduction is the commonest problem after any CC reconstruction; graft plus a suspensory device is more robust than either alone.
  • Distal clavicle osteolysis and distal clavicle excision:
    • Distal clavicle osteolysis ("weightlifter's shoulder") is a distinct overuse entity — subchondral cystic change and resorption of the distal clavicle on the Zanca view, with focal AC tenderness.
    • Treated with activity modification and, if refractory, distal clavicle excision.
    • Distal clavicle excision must PRESERVE the CC ligaments and the SUPERIOR/POSTERIOR AC capsule.
    • Resect ≤1 cm — and less (5–7 mm) from a direct SUPERIOR approach, because the superior capsule is violated by that approach.
    • Over-resection produces iatrogenic horizontal instability and persistent pain — the commonest reason for a failed distal clavicle excision.

Scapular winging & long thoracic/spinal accessory palsy

Medial versus lateral winging, the nerve behind each, and why the answer at 4 months is almost always to wait.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2024S (x2), 2024F

  • How winging is defined — the point the question turns on:
    • Winging is defined by the DIRECTION the INFERIOR ANGLE / medial border of the scapula translates.
    • The standard definition is based on the position of the INFERIOR ANGLE of the scapula relative to the MIDLINE.
    • MEDIAL winging — the inferior angle rotates MEDIALLY, toward the spine.
    • LATERAL winging — the inferior angle rotates LATERALLY, away from the spine.
    • Note that it is the direction of rotation of the inferior angle, not simply which border lifts off the chest wall.
  • MEDIAL winging = serratus anterior = LONG THORACIC nerve:
    • Long thoracic nerve (C5-6-7) palsy — the commonest cause of winging.
    • Accentuated by FORWARD FLEXION or a wall push-up.
    • Causes: viral illness / Parsonage-Turner; traction injury; repetitive overhead activity; surgery (axillary node dissection, first rib resection, thoracotomy); and blunt trauma.
    • Serratus anterior protracts the scapula and, critically, upwardly rotates it — so its loss also limits elevation above 90°.
  • LATERAL winging = trapezius = SPINAL ACCESSORY nerve (CN XI):
    • Accentuated by shoulder ABDUCTION and external rotation.
    • Classically follows POSTERIOR TRIANGLE NECK SURGERY — a lymph node biopsy is the archetypal cause, because the nerve is superficial in Erb's point.
    • Presents with a DROOPING shoulder, weakness of abduction above 90°, and pain (from the traction on the levator scapulae and the shoulder girdle).
    • Unlike long thoracic palsy, an iatrogenic sharp transection will not recover — early exploration and repair or grafting should be considered within 3–6 months.
  • Timeline and treatment of long thoracic palsy:
    • Most long thoracic nerve palsies are NEURAPRAXIC and recover spontaneously over 12–24 months.
    • Treat with OBSERVATION, physiotherapy to maintain motion, and a brace / scapular stabilisation for comfort.
    • Serial EMG at 3–6 months guides prognosis (reinnervation potentials are reassuring).
    • Failure to recover by 12–24 months → PECTORALIS MAJOR TRANSFER (split sternal head, with a fascial or tendon graft extension, to the inferior angle) — the operation of choice for serratus palsy.
    • EDEN-LANGE transfer (levator scapulae, rhomboid minor and rhomboid major moved laterally) is the equivalent for trapezius palsy.
    • Scapulothoracic fusion is the salvage, particularly in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy where the whole periscapular musculature is involved.
  • The recurring clinical scenarios:
    • A patient who falls, has neck and shoulder pain with negative radiographs, and returns a month later with winging, weak abduction and lateral arm pain has a traction injury to the brachial plexus / long thoracic nerve.
    • PARSONAGE-TURNER (brachial neuritis / neuralgic amyotrophy) is the classic mimic — severe pain that PRECEDES profound patchy weakness, often after a viral illness, immunisation or surgery, with a generally good but SLOW prognosis (12–36 months).
    • An adolescent with medial scapular winging 4 months after a sports injury has a long thoracic nerve palsy, and the treatment is continued observation with physiotherapy — spontaneous recovery is the rule at that time point.
  • Pseudowinging — always examine for it:
    • A scapular OSTEOCHONDROMA — a mechanical cause, often with a palpable mass and a snapping scapula; imaging is diagnostic and excision is curative.
    • Scapulothoracic bursitis and a snapping scapula.
    • Painful inhibition from any shoulder pathology, and glenohumeral stiffness causing compensatory scapular substitution.
    • Also consider facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (bilateral, with facial weakness) and voluntary winging.

Proximal humerus fracture: Management & outcomes

The trial that made non-operative treatment the default, and — when you do fix it — the technical factors that stop it collapsing.

3 of 7 sittings2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • The evidence for non-operative treatment:
    • The evidence strongly supports NON-OPERATIVE treatment for most displaced proximal humerus fractures in OLDER patients.
    • The PROFHER trial (JAMA 2015) found NO difference in Oxford Shoulder Score or quality of life at 2 YEARS between surgery and sling treatment, for displaced fractures involving the surgical neck, in patients with a mean age of 66.
    • This is one of the highest-yield trials in shoulder trauma.
    • Criticisms to have ready: it excluded head-split fractures and fracture-dislocations, surgeon choice of implant varied, and recruitment was selective — so it is not a licence to treat every fracture in a sling.
    • Start motion early — pendulum exercises at 1–2 weeks — because stiffness, not nonunion, is the usual price of non-operative treatment.
  • Reducing the risk of fixation failure in a 3-part fracture:
    • Restore the MEDIAL CALCAR / medial hinge support, with an inferomedial "calcar" screw.
    • AVOID VARUS malreduction — reduce anatomically or into slight valgus.
    • Repair the TUBEROSITIES with heavy sutures through the rotator cuff to the plate — the sutures, not the screws, hold the tuberosities.
    • Appropriate screw length, avoiding intra-articular penetration — measure short, and use live fluoroscopy in multiple rotations.
    • Augmentation with a FIBULAR STRUT ALLOGRAFT in osteoporotic bone or with medial comminution.
    • VARUS MALREDUCTION and LOSS OF MEDIAL SUPPORT are the strongest predictors of screw cut-out.
  • Construct rigidity:
    • Bending rigidity is most influenced by the WORKING LENGTH — the distance between the most distal proximal (head) screw and the most proximal diaphyseal screw.
    • And by the screw configuration and number in the head (divergent, subchondral screws in the best bone).
    • Plus restoration of the medial column, which shares load rather than leaving the plate to carry it all.
    • Increasing plate LENGTH alone, without changing screw density, does NOT increase rigidity proportionally.
  • When to replace rather than fix:
    • REVERSE total shoulder arthroplasty is the arthroplasty of choice for a 4-part fracture in an ELDERLY patient with poor bone quality or unreconstructible tuberosities.
    • It is far LESS dependent on tuberosity healing than a HEMIARTHROPLASTY — which is why hemiarthroplasty for fracture has largely been abandoned in this group.
    • However, tuberosity healing in a reverse for fracture significantly improves EXTERNAL ROTATION and functional scores, so anatomic tuberosity repair remains worthwhile.
    • Other indications for arthroplasty: head-split fractures, anatomic neck fractures with a high AVN risk, and fracture-dislocations in the elderly.
    • In a young patient, fix almost everything — even a 4-part valgus-impacted fracture, where the AVN risk is lower because the medial periosteal hinge is preserved.
  • Tuberosity malunion:
    • Occurs after non-operative or operative treatment, and causes impingement, loss of motion and pain.
    • Loss of external rotation with a greater tuberosity malunion displaced >5 mm superiorly is the classic pattern — the tuberosity impinges under the acromion.
    • It is a difficult problem. Options: arthroscopic acromioplasty/tuberoplasty; osteotomy (technically demanding, high complication rate); or conversion to reverse arthroplasty.
    • Prevention is better: accept <5 mm of greater tuberosity displacement (and <3 mm in an overhead athlete) as the operative threshold.
  • Axillary nerve injury:
    • Occurs in up to 30–60% of fracture-dislocations, and is often subclinical (detectable only on EMG).
    • Deltoid weakness with lateral shoulder numbness after reduction is an axillary nerve palsy.
    • Management: observe, document carefully, and obtain EMG at ~6 weeks — most are neurapraxic and recover over 3–6 months.
    • Confirm the reduction and rule out ongoing compression first — a nerve draped over an unreduced head will not recover.
    • The nerve is ~5–7 cm distal to the acromion, and the axillary lateral view plus a careful deltoid examination is the minimum documentation.
  • The stiff shoulder a year after a 4-part fracture:
    • Persistent stiffness with a FIRM END-FEEL at 1 year suggests MALUNION with a mechanical block, ± post-traumatic arthritis or AVN.
    • It is not simple capsular contracture, and it will not respond to a manipulation or a capsular release alone.
    • CT is required to define the tuberosity position, the articular surface and the head.
    • AVN after a 4-part fracture is common (up to 30–40%) but is often surprisingly well tolerated if the tuberosities have healed anatomically.

Sternoclavicular joint injury & posterior dislocation

The one shoulder-girdle dislocation that is a surgical emergency, why age changes the diagnosis, and the pin that must never be used.

3 of 7 sittings2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • The anatomy:
    • The SC joint is the ONLY TRUE ARTICULATION between the axial skeleton and the upper limb.
    • It has inherently unstable bony architecture — less than half the medial clavicle articulates with the manubrium — and is stabilised almost entirely by LIGAMENTS.
    • The POSTERIOR sternoclavicular capsular ligament is the MOST IMPORTANT restraint to BOTH anterior and posterior translation.
    • Other stabilisers: the anterior capsular ligament, the interclavicular ligament, the costoclavicular (rhomboid) ligament, and the intra-articular disc ligament.
  • Age changes the diagnosis:
    • The MEDIAL CLAVICULAR PHYSIS is the LAST physis in the body to close — at about 23–25 years (it is also the last to ossify, appearing at ~18).
    • So in anyone under about 25, an apparent "SC dislocation" is usually a PHYSEAL (Salter-Harris I/II) FRACTURE-SEPARATION.
    • That matters because a physeal separation has substantial REMODELLING potential — an important management distinction, and an argument for closed treatment in a young patient with an anterior injury.
    • CT distinguishes the two; the periosteal sleeve remains attached to the sternum.
  • Posterior SC dislocation is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY:
    • Because of the RETROSTERNAL structures: the trachea, oesophagus, great vessels (brachiocephalic and subclavian) and the brachial plexus.
    • Symptoms of compression: dysphagia, dyspnoea, stridor, hoarseness, venous congestion of the arm, or a diminished pulse.
    • CT with contrast (CT angiogram) is the imaging study of choice.
    • Plain films — including the "serendipity" view (40° cephalic tilt) — are unreliable and should not be used to exclude it.
    • Up to a third of posterior dislocations have some degree of mediastinal compression, often without dramatic symptoms.
  • How to reduce a posterior dislocation:
    • In the OPERATING THEATRE, with CARDIOTHORACIC SURGERY available, under general anaesthesia.
    • Place a BUMP between the scapulae.
    • Apply ABDUCTION and EXTENSION traction to the arm.
    • Use a percutaneous TOWEL CLIP on the medial clavicle to lever it forward if traction alone fails.
    • If closed reduction fails or the joint is unstable, perform OPEN reduction with FIGURE-OF-EIGHT SEMITENDINOSUS graft reconstruction.
    • NEVER use K-WIRES or STEINMANN PINS across the SC joint — migration into the mediastinum has caused DEATHS. This is an absolute rule.
    • A posterior dislocation reduced within the first ~10 days is usually stable; later than that, expect to reconstruct.
  • Anterior SC dislocation:
    • Far more common than posterior.
    • Usually treated NON-OPERATIVELY even when it remains subluxed — closed reduction frequently fails to hold, and that is acceptable.
    • Most patients have minimal functional deficit, and surgical stabilisation has a high complication rate.
    • Recurrent instability is common but usually tolerated; the residual bump is a cosmetic rather than a functional problem.
    • Atraumatic anterior subluxation in a hyperlax young patient is treated with rehabilitation, never surgery.
  • The distinction that is easy to get backwards:
    • A patient with a posteriorly displaced distal clavicle relative to the ACROMION has a posterior AC (Rockwood type IV) injury, not an SC injury.
    • The AXILLARY VIEW is the discriminator.
    • So: a clavicle displaced posteriorly AT THE ACROMION is a type IV AC injury; posteriorly AT THE STERNUM is a posterior SC dislocation.
    • Both are missed on the AP film, and both need the lateral.
  • Other SC pathology to recognise:
    • Septic arthritis of the SC joint — rare, but classically in IV drug users, diabetics and the immunosuppressed; it needs CT, aspiration and usually resection arthroplasty because of the retrosternal spread risk.
    • SC osteoarthritis — common, usually incidental, occasionally treated with resection preserving the costoclavicular ligament.
    • Condensing osteitis of the medial clavicle and Friedrich disease (osteonecrosis) — self-limiting, do not operate.
    • SAPHO syndrome — sterile hyperostosis of the anterior chest wall with skin lesions.

Clavicle fracture

Who is at risk of nonunion, the Canadian trial that changed practice, and how the evidence has been tempered since.

3 of 7 sittings2020, 2024S, 2025

  • Predictors of nonunion of a midshaft clavicle fracture:
    • Complete DISPLACEMENT — no cortical contact.
    • COMMINUTION.
    • SHORTENING >2 cm.
    • FEMALE sex.
    • Advanced AGE.
    • SMOKING.
    • These are also the criteria that identify the patients most likely to BENEFIT from fixation — they are prognostic and predictive at the same time.
  • The landmark Canadian trial:
    • The Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society (COTS) RCT showed that PLATE FIXATION of a COMPLETELY DISPLACED midshaft clavicle fracture produced:
    • Improved DASH and Constant scores.
    • Faster time to union.
    • Lower nonunion and symptomatic malunion rates.
    • Higher patient satisfaction.
    • At the cost of hardware irritation and the need for removal in a proportion of patients.
    • This is one of the most cited Canadian orthopaedic trials and is worth knowing by name.
  • How the evidence has been tempered since:
    • Subsequent meta-analyses confirm a LOWER NONUNION rate with surgery.
    • But show that FUNCTIONAL DIFFERENCES NARROW BY 1 YEAR.
    • So shared decision-making based on displacement, shortening, occupation and patient preference is appropriate.
    • The numbers: nonunion ~15–20% for completely displaced fractures treated non-operatively, versus ~1–3% with plating.
    • Roughly 1 in 5 patients treated non-operatively will be dissatisfied enough to consider late surgery — and late reconstruction for nonunion works well, which supports an initial non-operative trial in a patient who prefers it.
  • Distal (lateral third) clavicle fractures — Neer classification:
    • Type I — lateral to the CC ligaments, which remain intact; minimally displaced and stable.
    • Type II — MEDIAL to the CC ligaments. The MEDIAL fragment is displaced SUPERIORLY by the trapezius while the LATERAL fragment is held down by the intact CC ligaments.
    • Type II has a HIGH nonunion rate (up to 30%) and is the type most often FIXED.
    • Type III — intra-articular extension into the AC joint; usually non-operative, may develop late AC arthritis.
    • Types I and III are usually treated non-operatively.
  • Fixation options:
    • SUPERIOR or ANTEROINFERIOR plating — anteroinferior may reduce hardware prominence and protects the neurovascular structures during drilling (the drill exits posteriorly, away from the subclavian vessels and lung); superior plating is biomechanically stronger in bending.
    • Intramedullary devices — less soft-tissue stripping and a smaller scar, but less rotational control and a risk of implant migration.
    • Hook plates for distal fractures — effective but require removal and risk acromial erosion.
    • Distal fractures can also be fixed with a CC suspensory device, a locking distal clavicle plate, or a combination.
    • A FIGURE-OF-EIGHT BRACE confers NO advantage over a simple SLING and is less comfortable — a durable negative finding.
  • Complications:
    • HARDWARE PROMINENCE / irritation is the commonest complication after plating — up to 30% request removal. Warn every patient.
    • SUPRACLAVICULAR NERVE injury causes numbness INFERIOR to the incision — warn patients pre-operatively, and protect the branches by dissecting between them where possible.
    • Rare but serious: pneumothorax and subclavian vessel injury during drilling — control the drill depth and consider anteroinferior plating.
    • Infection is uncommon; refracture after plate removal is a real risk, so delay removal to at least a year and protect the arm afterwards.

Shoulder periprosthetic fracture & infection

Stem stability decides the fracture, and Cutibacterium acnes decides everything about shoulder infection.

  • Periprosthetic humeral fracture — the Wright & Cofield classification:
    • Type A — centred at the STEM TIP with PROXIMAL extension.
    • Type B — AT or just DISTAL to the stem tip.
    • Type C — ENTIRELY DISTAL to the stem tip.
    • The classification is descriptive; management hinges on STEM STABILITY, not on the letter.
  • How stem stability drives the decision:
    • A WELL-FIXED stem with acceptable alignment → NON-OPERATIVE treatment (functional bracing) is reasonable for type C and many type B fractures, or ORIF with a plate and cerclage.
    • A LOOSE stem → REVISION to a LONG-STEM implant ± strut allograft.

  • The practical fixation points:
    • Non-operative treatment has a reasonable union rate but a PROLONGED course — 3–6 months in a brace, with stiffness and a difficult rehabilitation.
    • ORIF is favoured for displaced fractures and for patients unable to tolerate prolonged bracing.
    • Technique: a LONG plate SPANNING the stem, using cerclage cables or unicortical locking screws proximally (around the stem) and bicortical screws distally.
    • Add a STRUT ALLOGRAFT in osteoporotic bone — it acts as a biological plate and improves screw purchase.
    • Identify and protect the radial nerve when plating the distal humeral shaft.
  • Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes — the defining organism of shoulder PJI:
    • A slow-growing, GRAM-POSITIVE, ANAEROBIC ROD.
    • It is a normal resident of the SEBACEOUS GLANDS of the DERMIS.
    • Which is why standard SKIN PREPARATION does not eliminate it — it is below the surface being prepped.
    • And why it is found in a substantial percentage of "sterile" shoulder cases on culture of otherwise normal tissue.
  • The key clinical points about C. acnes:
    • It causes a LOW-GRADE, INDOLENT infection with a NORMAL or only mildly elevated ESR/CRP and NO sinus tract.
    • It often presents simply as UNEXPLAINED PAIN and STIFFNESS, or EARLY LOOSENING — the "aseptic" loosening that grows an organism.
    • It is far more common in MEN — greater sebaceous gland density.
    • CULTURES MUST BE HELD FOR 13–14 DAYS — a 5-day culture will MISS it. Tell the laboratory what you are looking for.
    • Multiple (≥5) deep tissue specimens should be taken, because a single positive culture may be a contaminant; two or more concordant positives are needed for confidence.
    • Alpha-defensin and standard synovial thresholds perform poorly in the shoulder — the ICM shoulder-specific criteria are used instead.
  • Prevention:
    • BENZOYL PEROXIDE skin preparation reduces the C. acnes burden — applied for several days pre-operatively.
    • Chlorhexidine–alcohol prep, changing the scalpel after the skin incision, and adding vancomycin to prophylaxis are all used.
    • None of these ELIMINATES the organism — the reservoir is intradermal.
    • Minimise operative time and avoid unnecessary re-draping of the axilla.
  • Management of shoulder PJI:
    • Coagulase-negative staphylococci and C. acnes are the commonest organisms.
    • ACUTE infections may be managed with débridement and component/liner exchange WITH RETENTION of the well-fixed implants.
    • CHRONIC infections require single- or two-stage revision.
    • The shoulder literature increasingly supports SINGLE-STAGE revision with prolonged antibiotics for LOW-VIRULENCE organisms, with comparable eradication rates and BETTER FUNCTION than two-stage — a genuine difference from the hip and knee.
    • Antibiotic-loaded cement spacers are used in the two-stage approach; a "spacer" shoulder often functions surprisingly well.

Throwing shoulder, GIRD & internal impingement

The late cocking position and what it does to the posterosuperior shoulder — and why the contracture is posterior, not anterior.

3 of 7 sittings2020, 2024F, 2025

  • The late cocking phase is the dangerous position:
    • Maximal ABDUCTION and EXTERNAL ROTATION — the position of peak load.
    • It generates peak ANTERIOR CAPSULAR STRAIN.
    • INTERNAL IMPINGEMENT of the posterosuperior cuff between the greater tuberosity and the posterosuperior glenoid — a physiologic contact that becomes pathologic with repetition.
    • And peak VALGUS STRESS at the ELBOW — the same phase that injures the UCL.
    • The deceleration/follow-through phase generates the peak distraction force and is where the posterior cuff and labrum are eccentrically loaded.
  • What GIRD is:
    • Glenohumeral internal rotation deficit — a loss of INTERNAL rotation in the throwing shoulder.
    • Generally defined as >20° loss compared with the contralateral side, or a loss exceeding the gain in external rotation (i.e. a reduced total arc of motion).
    • It results from POSTEROINFERIOR CAPSULAR CONTRACTURE and, in part, from humeral retroversion adaptation.
    • Measure it supine with the scapula stabilised and the arm at 90° abduction.
  • What GIRD is ASSOCIATED with:
    • Posterosuperior (internal) impingement.
    • SLAP and posterosuperior LABRAL tears.
    • Undersurface partial-thickness ARTICULAR-SIDED cuff tears (PASTA lesions).
    • Scapular dyskinesis.
    • The PEEL-BACK mechanism of the biceps anchor (Burkhart) — in abduction and external rotation the biceps vector shifts posteriorly and peels the superior labrum off the glenoid.
  • What GIRD is NOT associated with — the distractors:
    • ANTERIOR capsular contracture is NOT associated with GIRD — the contracture is POSTEROINFERIOR. This is the single most common wrong answer.
    • A GAIN in internal rotation — the opposite of the definition.
    • An ANTERIOR labral tear as the primary lesion — the lesion is posterosuperior.
    • Anterior instability as the primary problem; the anterior capsule in a thrower is typically lax, not contracted.
  • Treatment is non-operative first, and usually works:
    • SLEEPER STRETCHES and CROSS-BODY ADDUCTION stretching of the POSTERIOR capsule.
    • Scapular stabilisation and periscapular strengthening.
    • Correction of kinetic-chain deficits — hip and trunk rotation, core strength, and throwing mechanics; most "shoulder" problems in a thrower start below the shoulder.
    • A large majority respond to a structured programme over 6–12 weeks.
    • Arthroscopic posteroinferior capsular release is RARELY needed.
    • Operating on a thrower's SLAP tear has notoriously unpredictable return-to-play rates — treat the GIRD first.
  • Total rotational motion and adaptive retroversion:
    • Total rotational motion (IR + ER) should be within ~5° of the contralateral side.
    • Loss of the TOTAL ARC is more predictive of injury than isolated IR loss — a thrower who has lost 25° of IR but gained 25° of ER has a normal total arc and is not at increased risk.
    • HUMERAL RETROVERSION in the dominant arm is an ADAPTIVE BONY change from repeated throwing during growth (the proximal humeral physis remodels), accounts for part of the ER gain, and is NOT pathologic.
    • This is why comparing to the other side without accounting for retroversion over-diagnoses GIRD.
  • Little League shoulder:
    • A PROXIMAL HUMERAL PHYSEAL STRESS INJURY — a widened, irregular physis on radiographs, compared with the other side.
    • Occurs in the skeletally immature thrower, typically 11–15 years.
    • Treat with REST from throwing for ~3 months, then a graded interval throwing programme.
    • Correct mechanics and enforce PITCH COUNTS — the definitive prevention.
    • The elbow equivalent is medial epicondyle apophysitis ("Little League elbow"), and the two frequently coexist.

Adhesive capsulitis

The natural history, stages and evidence-based treatment of frozen shoulder.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2023

  • What it is, and where the contracture sits:
    • A FIBROTIC CONTRACTURE of the glenohumeral capsule.
    • The ROTATOR INTERVAL and the CORACOHUMERAL LIGAMENT are the primary sites of contracture.
    • Which is why EXTERNAL ROTATION is lost FIRST — the coracohumeral ligament is the primary restraint to external rotation with the arm at the side.
    • The axillary pouch and the inferior capsule contract later, limiting abduction.
  • Associations:
    • DIABETES MELLITUS — the strongest, up to 5× risk, and the disease is more severe and more refractory in diabetics.
    • Thyroid disease.
    • Dupuytren disease.
    • Cardiac disease (and stroke).
    • Prolonged immobilisation, including after surgery or a fracture.
    • Also female sex, age 40–60, and the non-dominant arm.
  • The histopathologic sequence (Neviaser stages) — the order is repeatedly tested:
    • Stage 1 — INFLAMMATORY / hypervascular SYNOVITIS with capsular hypertrophy.
    • Stage 2 — FIBROBLASTIC PROLIFERATION with synovitis and early adhesion formation ("FREEZING").
    • Stage 3 — MATURATION / FIBROSIS with dense capsular collagen and LOSS of synovitis ("FROZEN").
    • Stage 4 — CHRONIC burnt-out fibrosis with resolving stiffness ("THAWING").
    • The pattern is: SYNOVITIS first, FIBROBLAST PROLIFERATION and collagen deposition second, then MATURATION and resolution.
  • The clinical hallmark:
    • LOSS OF PASSIVE EXTERNAL ROTATION with the arm at the side, in a patient with NORMAL radiographs.
    • This distinguishes it from CUFF pathology, where passive motion is preserved.
    • And from ARTHRITIS, where the radiographs are abnormal.
    • Always get radiographs — a locked posterior dislocation and glenohumeral arthritis both masquerade as a "frozen shoulder".
  • Natural history:
    • SELF-LIMITING in most patients over 1–3 years.
    • But a meaningful minority — particularly diabetics — retain some restriction long-term.
    • This is the fundamental counselling point: it gets better, but slowly, and not always completely.
  • Treatment:
    • INTRA-ARTICULAR CORTICOSTEROID INJECTION combined with a home STRETCHING programme provides the most rapid improvement in pain and motion in the EARLY (painful/freezing) phase.
    • The injection is most effective EARLY, when synovitis dominates.
    • Physiotherapy alone is effective but slower, and aggressive stretching during the inflammatory phase can WORSEN symptoms.
    • Hydrodilatation is a reasonable adjunct.
    • A short course of oral steroid helps pain in the acute phase but does not change the long-term course.
  • Refractory disease:
    • After ~6 months of good non-operative treatment: MANIPULATION UNDER ANAESTHESIA and/or ARTHROSCOPIC CAPSULAR RELEASE.
    • Release the rotator interval and anterior capsule, ± the posterior capsule (add the posterior release when internal rotation is the dominant loss).
    • Immediate post-operative physiotherapy is essential — the gain is lost within days without it.
    • Beware HUMERAL FRACTURE and DISLOCATION with MUA in osteopenic bone, and avoid MUA in the very stiff diabetic where a controlled release is safer.

    • Angulation >45°.
    • Glenoid articular step >4 mm.

    • The standard approach for scapular body and neck fractures.
    • The interval is developed between INFRASPINATUS and TERES MINOR, elevating the deltoid.

    • A TRACTION injury producing LATERAL DISPLACEMENT of the scapula with disruption of the periscapular musculature.

    • The diagnostic clue is a NON-ROTATED CHEST RADIOGRAPH showing LATERAL DISPLACEMENT of the scapula.
    • Measured as the distance from the midline (spinous processes) to the medial border of the scapula, compared with the opposite side — a ratio >1.29 is suggestive.
    • The classic clinical presentation is massive swelling with a FLAIL, PULSELESS, INSENSATE limb.
    • Early recognition on the TRAUMA CHEST RADIOGRAPH is the key point — it is easy to dismiss as a rotated film.
  • Management and outcome:
    • Immediate vascular assessment (angiography) and repair of any vascular injury.
    • Skeletal stabilisation (usually the clavicle) to restore the strut.
    • Full neurological assessment, with EMG at 3–4 weeks and consideration of early exploration.
    • THE NEUROLOGIC INJURY DETERMINES THE OUTCOME.
    • A complete PREGANGLIONIC brachial plexus avulsion produces a flail, insensate limb, and above-elbow amputation with shoulder arthrodesis may ultimately give better function than a flail limb.
    • Mortality is roughly 10%, and complete plexus injury occurs in about half.

Scapulohumeral rhythm & shoulder biomechanics

Coordinated scapular and glenohumeral motion.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2024F

  • Scapulohumeral rhythm — the ratio:
    • The coordinated motion of the glenohumeral and scapulothoracic joints during elevation.
    • Overall the ratio is approximately 2:1 (glenohumeral : scapulothoracic).
    • So of 180° of elevation, ~120° occurs at the GLENOHUMERAL joint and ~60° at the SCAPULOTHORACIC articulation.
    • The first ~30° (the "SETTING PHASE") is predominantly GLENOHUMERAL; beyond that the ratio approaches 2:1, and in the terminal range the scapular contribution increases further.
  • What the scapula actually does at 150° of abduction:
    • UPWARD ROTATION (~45–60°).
    • POSTERIOR TILT.
    • EXTERNAL ROTATION.
    • With clavicular elevation and retraction, and posterior clavicular axial rotation (~40° at the SC joint).
    • The scapula does NOT internally rotate or anteriorly tilt during normal elevation.
    • Anterior tilt and internal rotation define SCAPULAR DYSKINESIS, which is associated with impingement, cuff pathology and instability.
  • The two force couples:
    • Scapular: the UPPER TRAPEZIUS, LOWER TRAPEZIUS and SERRATUS ANTERIOR produce upward rotation and posterior tilt.
    • Glenohumeral: the DELTOID and the ROTATOR CUFF — the cuff provides a compressive, DEPRESSING force that offsets the superiorly directed deltoid vector.
    • Loss of the cuff (a massive tear) breaks this couple and produces SUPERIOR MIGRATION — which is the whole basis of cuff arthropathy and of the reverse arthroplasty.
    • In the axial plane, the subscapularis anteriorly balances the infraspinatus and teres minor posteriorly — the transverse force couple that a partial cuff repair aims to restore.
  • Supraspinatus insertion — the anatomy has been revised:
    • It inserts on the SUPERIOR FACET of the greater tuberosity.
    • And typically covers the entire superior facet plus a small portion of the highest part of the MIDDLE facet.
    • Contemporary anatomic studies show the supraspinatus footprint is SMALLER and MORE ANTERIOR than classically taught, with the INFRASPINATUS covering MORE of the greater tuberosity — including part of the superior facet — than previously believed.
    • The supraspinatus footprint does NOT normally extend onto the LESSER tuberosity — that is subscapularis, and a footprint extending there would be abnormal.
    • Practical relevance: a "supraspinatus" tear that extends posteriorly is often an infraspinatus tear, and the repair must restore the whole footprint.
  • The coracoacromial arch:
    • Provides STATIC SUPERIOR RESTRAINT to the humeral head.
    • Releasing the CA ligament in a CUFF-DEFICIENT shoulder can precipitate ANTEROSUPERIOR ESCAPE — an irreversible and disabling problem.
    • So do not perform an acromioplasty or CA ligament release in a shoulder with an irreparable cuff tear; preserve the arch.

Suprascapular nerve entrapment

Compression at the suprascapular versus spinoglenoid notch.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2025

  • The anatomy, and the mnemonic:
    • The suprascapular nerve (C5-6, from the UPPER TRUNK) passes through the SUPRASCAPULAR NOTCH beneath the TRANSVERSE (SUPERIOR) SCAPULAR LIGAMENT.
    • Mnemonic: "the ARMY goes OVER the bridge, the NAVY goes UNDER" — the suprascapular ARTERY passes OVER the ligament, the NERVE passes UNDER it.
    • It then runs around the base of the scapular spine through the spinoglenoid notch to reach the infraspinatus.
    • It is purely motor to the two muscles, with sensory branches to the AC and glenohumeral joints given off proximally.
  • Localising the compression — the repeatedly tested distinction:
    • Compression at the SUPRASCAPULAR NOTCH affects BOTH the SUPRASPINATUS AND the INFRASPINATUS — weak initiation of abduction and weak external rotation, with deep posterolateral shoulder PAIN.
    • Compression at the SPINOGLENOID NOTCH (distal) affects ONLY the INFRASPINATUS — isolated infraspinatus atrophy and external rotation weakness.
    • And it is typically PAINLESS or minimally painful, because the sensory branches to the AC and glenohumeral joints have already departed proximally.
    • So: pain plus both muscles = notch; painless plus infraspinatus only = spinoglenoid.
  • Causes:
    • A SPINOGLENOID (PARALABRAL) CYST from a posterior labral / SLAP tear — the classic cause at the spinoglenoid notch.
    • Ganglia elsewhere along the course.
    • Traction — overhead athletes, particularly volleyball players.
    • A hypertrophied or OSSIFIED transverse scapular ligament, or a narrow notch.
    • Massive retracted rotator cuff tears, through traction and tethering of the nerve as the muscle retracts medially.
    • Iatrogenic injury — the Judet approach, medial retraction during cuff mobilisation, and glenoid anchor placement.
  • Diagnosis:
    • MRI shows the cyst and the pattern of denervation oedema or fatty atrophy.
    • WHICH MUSCLES ARE INVOLVED LOCALISES THE LESION — this is the whole diagnostic logic.
    • EMG/nerve conduction studies confirm and quantify the lesion, and give a baseline.
    • Injection of local anaesthetic at the notch can be diagnostic.
    • Exclude a C5-6 radiculopathy and Parsonage-Turner, both of which produce a similar pattern.
  • Treatment:
    • For a PARALABRAL CYST, ADDRESS THE LABRAL TEAR — arthroscopic labral repair with or without cyst decompression resolves the cyst in most cases.
    • Isolated cyst aspiration RECURS, because the one-way valve of the labral tear keeps filling it.
    • For idiopathic compression: arthroscopic or open release of the transverse scapular ligament.
    • Massive cuff tear-associated neuropathy often improves with cuff repair — repairing the tendon detensions the nerve.
    • Non-operative treatment (activity modification, physiotherapy) is reasonable first for traction neuropathy in an athlete without a structural lesion.

Biceps & SLAP pathology

Diagnosis of SLAP lesions and the tenotomy/tenodesis decision.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • Examination for a SLAP lesion:
    • NO single physical examination test is adequate for a SLAP lesion.
    • The most useful COMBINATION is the O'BRIEN active compression test PLUS a biceps-specific test (SPEED's or the BICEPS LOAD II test).
    • Combining tests improves specificity substantially.
    • Isolated tests have poor specificity, because of overlap with cuff, AC joint and instability pathology.
  • Snyder classification of SLAP lesions:
    • Type I — fraying with an INTACT anchor; degenerative, usually débrided.
    • Type II — DETACHMENT of the BICEPS ANCHOR; the commonest surgically treated type. Subdivided into anterior, posterior and combined.
    • Type III — BUCKET-HANDLE tear with an INTACT anchor.
    • Type IV — bucket-handle EXTENDING INTO the biceps tendon.
  • The peel-back mechanism:
    • Described by Burkhart for POSTERIOR type II SLAP lesions in THROWERS.
    • With the arm ABDUCTED and EXTERNALLY ROTATED, the biceps vector shifts POSTERIORLY and PEELS the posterosuperior labrum off the glenoid.
    • It is closely linked to GIRD and posterior capsular contracture — treat the GIRD, and the peel-back load falls.
  • Treatment — age drives the decision:
    • In patients over ~35–40 years, SLAP REPAIR has LESS PREDICTABLE results — persistent pain, stiffness and hardware complications.
    • So BICEPS TENODESIS is preferred for a symptomatic type II lesion in that group.
    • SLAP repair is reserved for YOUNG ATHLETES, especially overhead throwers — although return to elite throwing is only ~60–70%, which must be discussed before surgery.
    • Débridement alone is appropriate for type I and for degenerative fraying, which is a normal finding with age.
    • An incidental degenerative SLAP lesion found while treating a cuff tear in an older patient should be left alone, not repaired.
  • Biceps tenotomy versus tenodesis:
    • Both relieve pain reliably — this is the starting point.
    • TENOTOMY is faster, simpler, requires no rehabilitation protection, and is preferred in older, low-demand patients.
    • But it causes a "POPEYE" deformity in ~20–40%, plus cramping and a small loss of supination and elbow flexion strength.
    • TENODESIS avoids the deformity and cramping, and is preferred in younger, thinner or more cosmetically concerned patients and in manual labourers.
    • Subpectoral tenodesis removes the tendon from the groove entirely and avoids residual groove pain, at the cost of a small risk of humeral fracture through the tunnel.
  • Normal variants that must NOT be "repaired":
    • SUBLABRAL FORAMEN — an anterosuperior labral detachment that is a normal variant.
    • BUFORD COMPLEX — a cord-like middle glenohumeral ligament with an ABSENT anterosuperior labrum.
    • MENISCOID superior labrum — a mobile, free superior labral edge.
    • Repairing a BUFORD COMPLEX causes SEVERE LOSS OF EXTERNAL ROTATION — a well-described iatrogenic disaster.
    • All three are confined to the anterosuperior quadrant; a detached labrum below the equator is never a normal variant.

Cervical vs shoulder pain differentiation

Sorting out referred neck pain from primary shoulder pathology.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • The single most useful discriminator:
    • The effect of NECK movement versus SHOULDER movement on the pain, combined with the LOCATION of the pain.
    • CERVICAL source: pain DISTAL to the elbow, in a DERMATOMAL distribution, reproduced by neck extension/rotation (Spurling) and relieved by shoulder abduction (the shoulder abduction relief sign).
    • SHOULDER source: pain localised to the DELTOID INSERTION, reproduced by shoulder-specific provocative tests and a painful arc, with a NORMAL neurologic examination.
  • Spurling's test:
    • Extension, ipsilateral rotation and axial compression, reproducing radicular arm pain (not neck pain alone).
    • HIGH specificity but LOW sensitivity for cervical radiculopathy.
    • A positive test is meaningful; a negative test does not exclude it.
    • It is the highest-yield single manoeuvre in separating the two.
  • Neurologic findings that favour a cervical source:
    • Dermatomal sensory loss.
    • Diminished reflexes — biceps C5-6, brachioradialis C6, triceps C7.
    • Myotomal weakness that does not fit a single tendon.
    • Symptoms BELOW the elbow.
    • By contrast, weakness confined to a specific rotator cuff tendon with a positive LAG SIGN favours the shoulder.
  • History features:
    • Favouring the SHOULDER: nocturnal pain lying on the shoulder, and a painful arc with positive impingement signs.
    • Favouring the NECK: pain worse with prolonged neck posture, worse with coughing or Valsalva, and relieved by the hand-on-head position.
    • Ask about hand clumsiness and gait — those point past radiculopathy to myelopathy.
  • They frequently coexist:
    • The "double crush" or dual-pathology patient is common in older adults — an incidental cuff tear and incidental cervical spondylosis are both near-universal past 60.
    • A DIAGNOSTIC SUBACROMIAL or GLENOHUMERAL INJECTION is often the most practical way to separate the two.
    • Substantial temporary relief localises the pain to the shoulder; no relief points elsewhere.
    • The same logic applies to a selective cervical nerve root block.
  • The red-flag mimics to keep in mind:
    • Suprascapular neuropathy.
    • Brachial neuritis (Parsonage-Turner) — severe pain preceding patchy weakness.
    • Pancoast tumour — lower trunk symptoms, Horner syndrome, weight loss, a smoking history.
    • Cardiac referral — left shoulder pain with exertion.
    • Cervical MYELOPATHY — gait and balance disturbance, hand clumsiness, hyperreflexia.
    • Also thoracic outlet syndrome and a diaphragmatic/subphrenic source referring to the shoulder tip (C4).

Inflammatory (rheumatoid) shoulder arthropathy

Recognising the inflammatory glenohumeral joint and its arthroplasty implications.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • The radiographic signature of an inflammatory shoulder:
    • CENTRAL/MEDIAL glenoid EROSION.
    • Periarticular OSTEOPENIA.
    • SYMMETRIC joint space narrowing.
    • Marginal EROSIONS.
    • ABSENCE of osteophytes.
    • Contrast with primary OSTEOARTHRITIS: inferior humeral osteophytes ("goat's beard"), POSTERIOR glenoid wear, subchondral sclerosis and cysts, and PRESERVED bone density.
  • Recognising it clinically:
    • Diffuse osteopenia with CENTRAL glenoid wear in an older patient with lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or another connective tissue disease points to an inflammatory arthropathy.
    • Associated rotator cuff tearing is common — up to 25–50% in long-standing rheumatoid disease.
    • That cuff status strongly influences implant choice, and must be assessed with MRI or ultrasound before planning.
  • Arthroplasty considerations:
    • Central glenoid erosion with medialisation limits glenoid bone stock and may preclude a glenoid component in an anatomic TSA — CT is essential for planning, and bone grafting or a reverse baseplate with a long central post may be needed.
    • Rotator cuff deficiency mandates a REVERSE.
    • Bone quality is poor, so periprosthetic fracture and glenoid baseplate failure are more likely.
    • Infection risk is higher in rheumatoid patients, particularly those on biologics.
  • Medication management around surgery:
    • CONTINUE conventional synthetic DMARDs (methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine).
    • HOLD biologics and schedule surgery at the END of the dosing interval.
    • Restart at ~14 days post-operatively, once the wound is satisfactory (ACR/AAHKS guideline).
    • Minimise glucocorticoids — taper to the lowest feasible dose pre-operatively; routine stress-dose steroid is not required.
    • Hold JAK inhibitors for 3 days.
  • The cervical spine — the safety point:
    • The cervical spine must be evaluated before general anaesthesia in any patient with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis.
    • ATLANTOAXIAL INSTABILITY is common and can cause catastrophic injury during intubation and positioning — particularly relevant in the beach-chair position.
    • Get flexion-extension cervical radiographs; a posterior atlanto-dens interval <14 mm is the warning threshold.
  • Other inflammatory shoulders to recognise:
    • MILWAUKEE SHOULDER — a rapidly destructive crystalline (basic calcium phosphate) arthropathy in an elderly woman, with a massive cuff tear and a large bloody effusion, and dramatic glenohumeral destruction.
    • SEPTIC ARTHRITIS — must be excluded before attributing an effusion to inflammation; aspirate if there is any doubt.
    • Crystal arthropathy (gout, pseudogout) and haemophilic arthropathy in the appropriate patient.

Shoulder arthrodesis

The rare salvage operation, its indications and contraindications.

1 of 7 sittings2023

  • Modern indications — all salvage:
    • Complete BRACHIAL PLEXUS PALSY, particularly with a functioning trapezius, serratus and levator scapulae.
    • Paralytic DELTOID AND rotator cuff loss together.
    • Failed arthroplasty with an irreparable deltoid or unreconstructible bone loss.
    • Chronic recalcitrant infection.
    • Post-tumour resection.
    • It also has a role in refractory instability in patients with neurologic or seizure disorders, where soft-tissue procedures fail.
  • The critical prerequisite — and the principal contraindication:
    • FUNCTIONING SCAPULOTHORACIC MUSCULATURE (trapezius, serratus anterior, levator scapulae) is essential.
    • Because ALL post-fusion motion comes from the SCAPULOTHORACIC articulation.
    • So PARALYSIS OF THE PERISCAPULAR MUSCLES is the PRINCIPAL CONTRAINDICATION.
    • Also contraindicated: active infection (relative — stage it), and, in most series, the presence of a functioning deltoid and cuff, in which case arthroplasty is better.
  • Optimal position of fusion:
    • Approximately 20–30° of ABDUCTION.
    • 20–30° of FORWARD FLEXION.
    • 30–45° of INTERNAL ROTATION.
    • All measured relative to the SCAPULA, not to the thorax.
    • The functional test: the position must allow the hand to reach the MOUTH, the MIDLINE anteriorly, and the PERINEUM.
    • Excessive abduction causes periscapular pain and winging — the commonest positioning error.
  • Technique:
    • Plate fixation — a contoured 4.5 mm pelvic reconstruction plate from the scapular spine, across the acromion, onto the humerus.
    • With compression screws across the glenohumeral joint and cancellous bone graft.
    • Decorticate both the glenohumeral and the acromiohumeral surfaces — fusing both increases the union rate.
    • External fixation or a spica cast are alternatives in specific circumstances (infection, poor bone).
  • Expected outcomes:
    • Reliable PAIN RELIEF and STABILITY, at the cost of MOTION.
    • Residual elevation is roughly 90–110°, and rotation is markedly limited.
    • Patients cannot reach behind their back or overhead easily, and cannot sleep prone.
    • Counsel explicitly about these specific limitations before consenting — patients tolerate a well-explained fusion far better than a surprising one.
  • Complications:
    • NONUNION — up to 10–15%.
    • MALPOSITION, which is functionally disabling and difficult to revise.
    • Peri-implant HUMERAL FRACTURE — a well-recognised late complication, because the fused unit acts as a LONG LEVER ARM.
    • Hardware prominence, frequently requiring removal.
    • Persistent periscapular pain from the increased demand on the scapulothoracic muscles.
