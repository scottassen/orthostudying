Cerebral palsy: Classification, hip surveillance & management

GMFCS, hip surveillance, spasticity management and the specific surgical decisions tested.

7 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021 (x3), 2022 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025 (x3)

  • GMFCS — the single most important prognostic and communication tool (valid from age 2, generally stable over time):
    • I — walks without limitation (climbs stairs without a railing; runs/jumps with reduced speed).
    • II — walks with limitations (no assistive device; difficulty on uneven ground/stairs).
    • III — walks using a hand-held mobility device; sits independently but needs trunk support when using the hands.
    • IV — self-mobility with limitations; may use powered mobility; does not walk functionally.
    • V — transported in a manual wheelchair; no independent mobility, no head/trunk control.
  • Hip displacement risk is directly proportional to GMFCS level — the key relationship:
    • GMFCS I ~0%, II ~15%, III ~40%, IV ~70%, V ~90%.
    • GMFCS level is the strongest predictor of subluxation/dislocation — not age, spasticity severity or tone.
    • Formal hip surveillance with serial AP pelvis radiographs and migration percentage is standard of care.
  • Migration percentage (Reimers) — the proportion of the femoral head lateral to Perkin's line:
    • >30% = subluxation requiring intervention; >50% = significant displacement; >100% = dislocation.
    • >50% with pain or difficulty with perineal care is an indication for reconstruction.
  • Reconstructing the displaced hip in a non-ambulator (GMFCS IV–V, migration >50%):
    • Varus derotation osteotomy (VDRO) COMBINED with a pelvic (acetabular) osteotomy and soft-tissue releases (adductor ± psoas).
    • Adding the pelvic osteotomy is what most improves outcome and reduces recurrence — acetabular dysplasia is always present and does not remodel in a non-ambulator.
    • Soft-tissue release alone is inadequate once migration exceeds ~40–50%; salvage (resection/interposition) is reserved for the painful, arthritic, unreconstructible hip.
  • Botulinum toxin A:
    • Blocks acetylcholine release presynaptically by cleaving SNAP-25 (a SNARE protein) — reversible chemodenervation lasting 3–6 months.
    • Botox plus serial casting beats casting alone short-term; botox alone is comparable to casting alone.
    • It treats dynamic tone, not a fixed contracture, and does not prevent or delay eventual surgery.
  • Equinus/toe-walking in spastic diplegia — use the Silfverskiöld test:
    • Dorsiflexion −10° knee-extended improving to 0° knee-flexed = isolated gastrocnemius → gastrocnemius recession (Strayer).
    • Over-lengthening the Achilles produces a calcaneal/crouch gait — far worse and largely irreversible. The cardinal error.
  • Jump gait in an ambulatory adolescent (hip flexion + knee flexion contracture + equinus):
    • Single-event multilevel surgery is the standard.
    • A fixed knee flexion contracture (~35°) needs distal femoral extension osteotomy with patellar tendon advancement — isolated hamstring lengthening is inadequate and risks crouch.
  • Upper extremity surgery:
    • The strongest predictor of a good outcome is preserved sensation — proprioception and stereognosis (plus selective motor control), not the degree of spasticity, contracture or age.
    • A child with poor stereognosis will not use the hand however well it is positioned.
    • Beware releasing a contracture that is functionally useful (e.g. an elbow flexion contracture aiding hand-to-mouth function in a child with good hand function).
  • Scoliosis in CP:
    • Correlates with GMFCS level and non-ambulatory status (70–90% in GMFCS V).
    • Long C-shaped curves with pelvic obliquity that progress after skeletal maturity.
    • Bracing does not prevent progression (it may aid sitting); surgery is posterior instrumented fusion to the pelvis for pelvic obliquity.

DDH: Screening, Pavlik & surgical management

Screening, ultrasound parameters, Pavlik use and failure, and the surgical algorithm by age.

7 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022 (x3), 2023, 2024S (x3), 2024F (x3), 2025 (x3)

  • Risk factors:
    • Breech presentation is the strongest (especially frank breech); female sex; family history; firstborn; oligohydramnios.
    • Left hip predominance — it lies against the maternal sacrum.
    • Associated "packaging" disorders: congenital muscular torticollis, metatarsus adductus, congenital knee dislocation.
  • Examination changes with age — a favourite discriminator:
    • Ortolani (reduces a dislocated hip) and Barlow (dislocates a reducible hip) are reliable only in the first ~3 months; after that the hip becomes fixed and both go negative.
    • After 3 months the most reliable finding is LIMITED HIP ABDUCTION, with asymmetric thigh folds, apparent femoral shortening (Galeazzi), and a limp/Trendelenburg once walking.
  • Imaging:
    • Ultrasound until the head ossifies (~4–6 months), then AP pelvis radiograph.
    • Graf alpha angle (bony roof): normal >60°; 50–59° immature/mild dysplasia; <50° dysplastic. Beta (cartilaginous roof) normal <55°. Femoral head coverage >50%.
    • Ultrasound before 6 weeks over-diagnoses physiologic immaturity.
  • Pavlik harness — the mainstay under ~6 months:
    • Position: hip flexion 90–100° and gentle (not forced) abduction.
    • Follow with ultrasound at 3 weeks; if not reduced by 3–4 weeks, abandon the harness and proceed to closed reduction.
  • Pavlik complications — the exam favourites:
    • Femoral nerve palsy from excessive FLEXION (>120°) — absent quadriceps function/inability to actively extend the knee. Discontinue the harness until it resolves, then reapply with less flexion; it marks a higher failure risk.
    • AVN from excessive ABDUCTION, caused by over-tightening the POSTERIOR strap (the posterior strap controls abduction; the anterior strap controls flexion).
  • Predictors of Pavlik failure:
    • An irreducible (Ortolani-negative, dislocated) hip is the strongest.
    • Bilateral dislocation; initially dislocated rather than subluxed; absent femoral head coverage; age at initiation >7 weeks; development of femoral nerve palsy; low initial alpha angle.
    • A large baby is NOT a recognised predictor — a common distractor.
  • Treatment algorithm by age:
    • 0–6 months → Pavlik harness.
    • 6–18 months → closed reduction with arthrogram ± adductor tenotomy and spica; open reduction if closed fails or the reduction is not concentric/stable in the safe zone (Ramsey).
    • 18 months–2 years → open reduction ± femoral shortening.
    • >2–3 years → open reduction with femoral shortening derotational osteotomy ± pelvic osteotomy. Femoral shortening is preferred to traction to reduce AVN.
  • Blocks to reduction:
    • Inverted limbus (infolded labrum), hypertrophied ligamentum teres, pulvinar, transverse acetabular ligament, capsular constriction (hourglass), and the iliopsoas tendon.
    • An inverted limbus in a young infant does not automatically mandate open reduction — many evert with reduction.
  • Medial (Ludloff) approach for infant open reduction — know its limitations:
    • Gives direct access to the inferomedial blocks (transverse acetabular ligament, iliopsoas, ligamentum teres) with less blood loss.
    • But: no capsulorrhaphy, no access to the superior/lateral acetabulum, and no concurrent femoral or pelvic osteotomy.
    • Higher reported AVN rate — the medial femoral circumflex artery is at risk.
  • The deformity: the femoral side shows excessive anteversion with coxa valga; the acetabulum is deficient anterolaterally. Trisomy 21 produces posterior/posterolateral deficiency with generalised laxity — so standard anterior-coverage osteotomies may be inadequate.

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: Natural history, bracing & surgery

Progression risk, maturity staging, BrAIST, MRI indications and surgical planning.

6 of 7 sittings2020, 2021 (x3), 2022, 2023 (x3), 2024F (x3), 2025 (x2)

  • Risk factors for progression:
    • Remaining skeletal growth is dominant — quantified by Risser stage, Sanders stage, triradiate cartilage status and menarchal status.
    • Larger curve at presentation; female sex; thoracic location; double curves.
    • A curve <30° at maturity rarely progresses; curves >50° at maturity progress ~1°/year into adulthood.
  • Sanders staging (hand/wrist digital physes) is more precise than Risser for predicting peak growth velocity:
    • Sanders 1–2 = pre-peak growth velocity — highest risk.
    • Sanders 3–4 = peak; Sanders 5–8 = declining/closed growth — low risk.
    • So the highest-risk patient on non-operative treatment is Sanders 1–2 with a curve already 30–39°.
  • BrAIST (Weinstein, NEJM 2013) — the landmark trial:
    • Bracing significantly reduced progression to the 50° surgical threshold vs observation (72% vs 48% success).
    • Clear dose-response — >12–13 hours/day gave ~90% success. The trial was stopped early for efficacy.
    • Indicated for curves 25–40° in a skeletally immature patient (Risser 0–2).
  • Bracing prevents progression — it does not correct the curve. Ineffective at maturity, in curves >45–50°, and traditionally in hypokyphotic/thoracic lordotic curves. Compliance dominates success.
  • Indications for MRI of the neuraxis — a high-yield list:
    • Atypical curve pattern (LEFT thoracic curve); rapid progression; age <10 years.
    • Abnormal neurologic examination — absent abdominal reflexes is the classic sign.
    • Significant or nocturnal pain; associated cavovarus foot; excessive thoracic kyphosis or a short sharp curve.
    • These raise concern for syringomyelia, Chiari malformation, tethered cord or an intraspinal tumour.
    • A young child with a left thoracic curve and wedge vertebrae also suggests congenital scoliosis → assess VACTERL (renal ultrasound, cardiac echo).
  • Predicting correction: curve flexibility on side-bending (or fulcrum-bending/traction) films — not the standing Cobb angle — predicts achievable correction; fulcrum-bending correlates best for thoracic curves.
  • Surgical indications and technique:
    • Curves >45–50° in the skeletally immature, >50–55° at maturity, progression despite bracing, significant deformity/imbalance, pain.
    • Posterior spinal instrumented fusion with pedicle screws; selective thoracic fusion for Lenke 1 curves with a compensatory lumbar curve that corrects spontaneously.
  • Intraoperative neuromonitoring:
    • A >50% drop in motor evoked potentials is the alert criterion.
    • The commonest cause after instrumentation/correction is hypotension and the correction manoeuvre itself — not screw malposition.
    • Response is stepwise: raise the MAP (>80–85 mmHg), check normothermia/haemoglobin/anaesthetic, release the correction, then inspect implants.
  • Post-operative complications:
    • Low urine output with normal haemodynamics after AIS correction = SIADH — treat with fluid restriction, not a bolus.
    • VTE risk in an adolescent is very low — mechanical prophylaxis (early mobilisation, compression devices), not chemical anticoagulation.
    • Superior mesenteric artery (cast) syndrome after significant lengthening in a thin patient — post-prandial vomiting and abdominal pain; treat with NG decompression, positioning and nutrition.

Paediatric Monteggia & radial neck fractures

The Bado classification, causes of failed radial head reduction, and radial neck management.

5 of 7 sittings2021, 2023, 2024S (x3), 2024F (x3), 2025 (x3)

  • Bado classification — defined by the direction of radial head dislocation:
    • Type I — anterior (commonest, ~70%; apex-anterior ulnar fracture).
    • Type II — posterior/posterolateral (commonest in adults, rare in children).
    • Type III — lateral (common in children; olecranon/proximal metaphyseal ulna greenstick).
    • Type IV — anterior dislocation with fractures of both radius and ulna.
  • The cardinal principle: anatomic reduction of the ULNA reduces the radial head.
    • Correct ulnar length and angulation — closed reduction/casting for plastic or greenstick deformity; IM nailing or plating for a complete/unstable ulnar fracture.
    • If the radial head will not stay reduced, the ulnar reduction is inadequate until proven otherwise.
  • Causes of failed radial head reduction:
    • Residual ulnar deformity (plastic deformation, malreduction, shortening) — the commonest and most important.
    • Interposed annular ligament (buttonholed over the head), capsule or biceps tendon.
    • Delayed/chronic presentation (>3–4 weeks) with contracture and a deformed head; an osteochondral fragment.
    • A PIN palsy is NOT a cause of failed reduction — it is a consequence of the injury.
  • PIN palsy with paediatric Monteggia:
    • Occurs in up to ~10–20% of type I and III injuries.
    • Almost always a neurapraxia that resolves once the radial head is reduced.
    • Reduce the ulna and observe; EMG at ~6 weeks, explore only if no recovery by ~3 months. Do not explore acutely.
  • Bado type II in an adult is a different injury — usually a transolecranon/proximal ulna fracture-dislocation with comminution, a coronoid fragment and a radial head fracture. Restore the proximal ulna (trochlear notch) with a dorsal plate and fix or replace the radial head; a small coronoid tip fragment is least likely to need fixation.
  • Chronic/missed Monteggia: requires ulnar osteotomy (usually opening-wedge with over-correction) ± annular ligament reconstruction and open reduction. Results deteriorate with time; a chronically dislocated, dome-shaped head in an older child may be best left alone.
  • Radial neck fractures — acceptable alignment:
    • <30° angulation and <3–4 mm translation (up to 45° accepted in very young children given remodelling).
    • Reduction techniques: Patterson manoeuvre (traction, varus, direct pressure), the Israeli technique (thumb pressure with rotation), and Métaizeau intramedullary nail joystick reduction — the workhorse when closed reduction fails.
  • Predictors of difficult/failed closed reduction: greater initial angulation (>60°), greater translation/complete displacement, delayed presentation, associated elbow injuries, older age. A small angulation (<30°) is not "associated with decreased odds of reduction" — it simply does not need reducing.
  • Open reduction of a radial neck fracture is a last resort — substantially higher rates of AVN of the radial head, stiffness, synostosis and premature physeal closure. Never excise the radial head in a child.
  • The radiocapitellar line — a line down the radial neck must pass through the capitellum on every view. Any paediatric ulna fracture demands scrutiny of this line.

Supracondylar humerus fracture

Gartland classification, the pulseless hand, nerve injury patterns and loss of reduction.

6 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2023 (x3), 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Gartland classification (extension type):
    • I non-displaced; II displaced with an intact posterior hinge (IIA angulation only, IIB with rotation/translation).
    • III completely displaced, no cortical contact (posteromedial displacement in ~75%).
    • IV multidirectional instability in both flexion and extension — diagnosed intra-operatively.
  • Nerve injury patterns — memorise the pairing:
    • Extension type, posteromedial displacement → anterior interosseous nerve (cannot make an "OK" sign; no sensory loss).
    • Posterolateral displacement → radial nerve.
    • Flexion type → ulnar nerve.
    • Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury is the commonest nerve complication of treatment, related to medial pin placement.
  • Blocks to reduction:
    • Flexion type — the ULNAR NERVE entrapped at the fracture site; these are more often irreducible and need open reduction.
    • Extension type — the brachialis ("brachialis/pucker sign") and the anterior neurovascular bundle.
    • The brachialis sign (anterior skin puckering) means the proximal fragment has penetrated the brachialis — associated with irreducibility, open reduction, neurovascular injury and compartment syndrome. It is not benign.
  • Predictors of loss of reduction after CRPP:
    • Inadequate pin configuration — insufficient pin spread at the fracture, too few pins, or pins not engaging both cortices.
    • Residual malreduction; sagittal obliquity of the fracture line is the most cited single radiographic predictor, then coronal obliquity and comminution.
  • Pin configuration:
    • Two or three lateral divergent pins are adequate for most fractures and avoid ulnar nerve risk.
    • A medial pin adds torsional stability (for medial comminution or type IV) but must be placed with the elbow extended (letting the ulnar nerve fall posteriorly) or via a mini-open incision.
    • Crossed pinning carries a several-fold higher rate of iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury (~4–6%).
  • Post-operative ulnar palsy after crossed pinning → remove the medial pin. Usually a neurapraxia recovering over weeks to months; explore if it appeared after pinning and fails to improve.
  • The pulseless supracondylar fracture — the highest-yield algorithm:
    • Pulseless but WELL-PERFUSED (pink, warm, cap refill <3 s) → urgent reduction and pinning, then reassess.
    • If it stays pink and perfused without a palpable pulse → observe in hospital 24–48 hours; collateral flow is usually adequate and the pulse commonly returns. Routine exploration or angiography is not required.
    • Pulseless AND poorly perfused (white, cold) → immediate reduction and pinning (reduction alone frequently restores perfusion); if still poorly perfused, urgent vascular exploration.
  • Compartment syndrome risk is increased by a floating elbow, vascular injury with reperfusion, high energy, delayed presentation, and cast hyperflexion >90°.
    • In children the 3 A's — increasing Analgesia requirement, Anxiety and Agitation — precede the classic signs.
  • Cubitus varus (gunstock deformity) is the classic malunion — from malreduction (coronal tilt/medial column collapse and internal rotation), not growth arrest. Associated with later posterolateral rotatory instability and lateral condyle fracture; treat symptomatic cases with a lateral closing wedge (or dome) osteotomy.

Paediatric septic arthritis & osteomyelitis

Kocher criteria, distinguishing sepsis from transient synovitis, and the neonatal hip.

5 of 7 sittings2022 (x3), 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • Kocher criteria (septic arthritis of the hip vs transient synovitis):
    • (1) Non-weightbearing; (2) fever >38.5°C; (3) ESR >40 mm/h; (4) WBC >12,000/mm³.
    • Probability: 1 predictor ~3%, 2 ~40%, 3 ~93%, 4 ~99%.
    • CRP >2.0 mg/dL is an independent predictor; in Caird's modification fever is the strongest single predictor.
  • Definitive diagnosis requires aspiration — the criteria are a probability tool, not a substitute:
    • Synovial WBC >50,000/mm³ with >90% neutrophils supports septic arthritis (lower counts occur early or if partially treated).
    • Aspirate before starting antibiotics wherever possible.
    • Ultrasound detects an effusion but cannot distinguish septic arthritis from transient synovitis — a repeatedly tested limitation.
  • Organisms by age:
    • Staphylococcus aureus is commonest at all ages (including MRSA in endemic areas).
    • Neonates — add group B Streptococcus and gram-negative bacilli.
    • 6 months–5 years — Kingella kingae: subacute/indolent, often with normal or minimally raised markers; grows poorly on standard media — inoculate blood culture bottles and use PCR.
    • Sickle cell disease — consider Salmonella (though S. aureus remains commonest).
  • The neonatal hip is a surgical emergency and behaves differently:
    • Often afebrile with normal or minimally elevated markers (immature immune response).
    • Presents as pseudoparalysis and irritability with hip/knee motion.
    • Transphyseal vessels persist until ~18 months, allowing rapid metaphysis↔joint spread — concomitant osteomyelitis is the rule.
    • Delay causes catastrophic destruction of the femoral head.
  • Suspect concomitant osteomyelitis when:
    • Symptoms >3–4 days; CRP >13–15 mg/L and persistently elevated after drainage; age <1 year; platelets <310,000; positive blood cultures; failure to improve after adequate drainage.
    • MRI is the study of choice for adjacent osteomyelitis, subperiosteal abscess and pyomyositis. ~a third of children with septic arthritis have adjacent osteomyelitis.
  • Osteomyelitis basics:
    • Metaphyseal involvement of a long bone is the commonest (distal femur, proximal tibia) — sluggish flow in metaphyseal sinusoidal loops adjacent to the physis.
    • Radiographs are normal for the first 7–14 days (until 30–50% of bone mineral is lost); MRI is the most sensitive early; bone scan for unclear site or multifocal disease.
  • Treatment:
    • Urgent arthrotomy/drainage for a septic hip (intracapsular blood supply to the head) plus IV antibiotics.
    • Osteomyelitis without abscess often responds to antibiotics alone; drain for subperiosteal/intraosseous abscess, sequestrum, or failure to respond in 48–72 hours.
    • Modern practice: a short IV course transitioning to oral once clinically improving with a falling CRP.
  • Special situations:
    • MRSA (especially PVL-positive) → more severe, multifocal, with DVT, septic emboli and pyomyositis — image the whole limb and consider Doppler.
    • CRMO — sterile, culture-negative inflammatory mimic with multiple symmetric metaphyseal lesions; treat with NSAIDs.

Paediatric ACL & tibial spine injury

Physeal-sparing techniques, graft failure risk, and tibial eminence fractures.

5 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024S (x2)

  • Paediatric ACL tears do NOT do well non-operatively — the central management point:
    • Delay is associated with higher rates of meniscal tears (particularly medial), chondral damage and instability episodes.
    • Evidence and consensus support early reconstruction in the skeletally immature athlete rather than bracing until maturity.
  • Technique is chosen by SKELETAL age, not chronological age:
    • Prepubescent / Tanner 1–2 (much growth remaining) → physeal-sparing: iliotibial band (Micheli/Kocher) combined intra-/extra-articular, or all-epiphyseal reconstruction.
    • Adolescent / Tanner 3 (modest growth remaining) → transphyseal with a soft-tissue (hamstring) graft, using vertical, central, small-diameter tunnels and avoiding bone blocks and hardware across the physis.
    • Skeletally mature → standard adult reconstruction.
  • Growth disturbance is uncommon but well described:
    • Distal femoral valgus, leg-length discrepancy, tibial recurvatum — from a bone block or a large/oblique tunnel across the physis, or hardware crossing it.
    • A BTB graft with bone blocks across an open physis is contraindicated.
  • Risk factors for graft failure in adolescents:
    • Young age at reconstruction; return to high-level pivoting sport; allograft use (markedly higher failure in this population).
    • Non-anatomic tunnels; increased posterior tibial slope; generalised laxity; early return before strength symmetry.
    • Female sex is a risk for primary ACL injury but not a consistent risk for graft failure — the standard "all except".
  • Paediatric vs adult knee ligament injury: children more often sustain tibial eminence avulsions and physeal fractures in place of some ligament injuries — a physeal fracture is more likely than a collateral ligament rupture in a skeletally immature knee, so stress-test and consider stress views.
  • Tibial spine (eminence) fracture — Meyers & McKeever:
    • I non-displaced; II hinged anteriorly (posterior cortex intact); III completely displaced; IV comminuted/rotated.
    • I and reducible II → closed reduction in extension and immobilisation.
    • Irreducible II, III and IV → arthroscopic reduction and fixation (suture or screw).
  • The block to reduction is the anterior horn of the medial meniscus (and the intermeniscal ligament) entrapped under the fragment — recognise and extract it arthroscopically.
  • The commonest complication after fixation is ARTHROFIBROSIS / loss of motion (especially extension), in up to 10–20% — hence early range of motion.
    • Residual anterior laxity (a positive Lachman without symptomatic instability) is very common and usually well tolerated.
    • Nonunion and growth arrest are rare.

Paediatric femoral shaft fracture & spica casting

Treatment by age, contraindications to early spica, and fixation options.

5 of 7 sittings2021, 2022 (x2), 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025 (x2)

  • Treatment algorithm by age (AAOS CPG):
    • 0–6 months → Pavlik harness or simple splint.
    • 6 months–5 years → early spica casting (main limit is >2–3 cm shortening).
    • 5–11 years → flexible (titanium elastic) IM nails; submuscular bridge plating for length-unstable or comminuted patterns.
    • >11 years/adolescent → rigid locked IM nail via a lateral trochanteric entry.
  • Contraindications to early spica — high-yield:
    • Shortening >2–3 cm or unacceptable angulation; age over ~5–6 years or weight >~50 lb.
    • Associated abdominal/thoracic injury or polytrauma requiring access and monitoring; head injury.
    • Open fracture; vascular injury; suspected non-accidental injury requiring investigation.
  • Acceptable alignment in a spica (using remodelling potential):
    • Shortening <2 cm; varus/valgus <10–15°; AP angulation <20–30°; rotation <10°.
    • Remodelling is greatest closest to the physis, in the plane of joint motion, and in the youngest children.
  • Overgrowth after paediatric femoral shaft fracture:
    • Averages ~1 cm (0.7–2 cm) in children aged 2–10, driven by physeal hyperaemia, maximal in the first 2 years.
    • The best predictor of final leg length is serial measurement over time (multiplier method, Green-Anderson/Moseley charts) — a single early measurement is unreliable.
  • Flexible (elastic) IM nails — the numbers:
    • Use two nails of equal diameter, each filling ~40% of the canal at the isthmus (~80% combined fill).
    • Work by three-point elastic fixation; ideal for length-stable transverse or short-oblique mid-diaphyseal fractures.
    • Relatively contraindicated in length-unstable (long spiral/comminuted) patterns and children over ~50 kg — high malunion and shortening rates.
  • Entry points:
    • Retrograde femoral flexible nails — ~2 cm proximal to the distal femoral physis, medially and laterally; avoid the physis.
    • Radius — dorsal entry just proximal to the distal radial physis, between the 2nd and 3rd dorsal compartments, protecting the EPL.
  • Submuscular bridge plating with non-locking screws in a spiral/oblique fracture gives relative stability → healing by secondary bone healing with abundant callus. The plate is an internal splint over a long working length; no anatomic reduction of intercalary fragments is attempted.
  • Complications:
    • Flexible nails — symptomatic hardware at the entry site (commonest), malunion, shortening in length-unstable patterns.
    • Rigid nail via the piriformis fossa in the young → AVN of the femoral head — the disaster complication, hence lateral trochanteric entry under ~14–16 years.
    • Spica — skin breakdown, compartment syndrome of the leg (particularly 90/90 casting with excessive traction), loss of reduction.
  • A femoral shaft fracture in a child under walking age (<1 year) is a red flag for non-accidental injury and mandates full evaluation.

Non-accidental injury (child abuse)

Recognising the fracture patterns of abuse and the physician's legal duty.

6 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2025 (x2)

  • Fracture patterns HIGHLY specific for abuse:
    • Classic metaphyseal lesion (CML) — the "corner" or "bucket-handle" fracture — the single most specific, from shearing/torsional force (violent pulling, twisting, shaking).
    • Posterior rib fractures (anteroposterior chest compression — squeezing).
    • Scapular, spinous process and sternal fractures; bilateral/multiple fractures of differing ages.
  • Moderate and low specificity:
    • Moderate — multiple fractures, fractures of different ages, epiphyseal separations, vertebral body fractures, digital fractures, complex skull fractures.
    • Low (common in accidents) — clavicle fractures, long bone diaphyseal fractures, linear skull fractures.
    • A spiral femur fracture in an ambulatory toddler is NOT in itself diagnostic; the same fracture in a non-ambulatory infant is highly concerning.
  • The single strongest red flag is a history inconsistent with the injury or the child's developmental stage:
    • No history of trauma at all with multiple healing fractures; a mechanism that could not generate the observed force.
    • Other red flags: delay in seeking care, changing history, inappropriate affect, prior injuries.
  • Any fracture in a non-ambulatory child (<1 year, or not yet cruising/walking) is abuse until proven otherwise and mandates a full work-up.
  • Investigation:
    • Skeletal survey is mandatory in every child under 2 with suspected abuse (considered up to age 5), often repeated at 2 weeks to catch healing injuries.
    • Head CT/MRI (subdural haematoma) and ophthalmologic examination for retinal haemorrhages in infants.
    • Bone profile (calcium, phosphate, ALP, PTH, vitamin D) and consideration of osteogenesis imperfecta.
  • The duty to report is legal and mandatory in every Canadian province and territory:
    • The threshold is reasonable grounds to SUSPECT, not proof.
    • Report to child protection services (and admit the child if home is unsafe). You do not need certainty, you do not confirm the diagnosis first, and you do not confront or investigate the family yourself.
    • Reporting in good faith carries statutory protection from liability. Uncertainty is exactly the situation the threshold is designed for.
  • Differential to consider and document: osteogenesis imperfecta (family history, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, wormian bones, low bone density), rickets/nutritional deficiency, Menkes disease, osteopenia of prematurity, congenital insensitivity to pain, birth trauma.
  • Why it matters: roughly a third of children returned to an abusive environment are re-injured, and a proportion die — recognition is a genuine life-saving intervention.

Paediatric forearm & distal radius fractures

Acceptable alignment, the cast index, refracture and physeal arrest.

5 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2023, 2024F, 2025 (x3)

  • Acceptable angulation depends on age and location:
    • Under 9 years — up to 15° angulation, 45° malrotation, and complete (bayonet) apposition with <1 cm shortening may remodel.
    • Over 10 years — accept <10° angulation and minimal malrotation.
    • Rotational malalignment does NOT remodel and is the least forgiving deformity. Fractures nearer the physis remodel best.
  • Cast index = internal cast width on lateral ÷ internal width on AP, measured at the fracture:
    • >0.8 predicts loss of reduction.
    • Good three-point moulding with a proper cast index matters more than cast material or layers.
    • Other predictors of redisplacement: complete initial displacement, an associated ulna fracture, poor initial reduction, older child.
  • Refracture is a well-recognised problem:
    • Overall risk ~5%; after open reduction and plating ~5–10% at 1 year, often after plate removal (through screw holes) or at plate ends.
    • Highest risk in the first 6–12 months; mid-diaphyseal plated fractures carry the highest rate. Protect the arm after removal.
  • Flexible nailing of the radius: dorsal entry just proximal to the distal radial physis, between the 2nd and 3rd dorsal compartments (radial to Lister's tubercle) — protect the EPL and superficial radial nerve, and do not cross the physis.
  • Distal radius physeal (Salter-Harris) fractures:
    • SH II is the commonest. Growth arrest occurs in ~4–7%.
    • Arrest is associated with repeated or late (>7–10 days) reduction attempts, high energy, and SH III/IV patterns.
    • Avoid repeated forceful manipulation after 7 days — accept the deformity and allow remodelling rather than risk iatrogenic physeal injury.
  • Which pattern is LEAST associated with arrest: the classic SH II in a young child treated with a single early gentle reduction — whereas SH III/IV, crush (SH V), open injuries and late/repeated manipulation are strongly associated.
  • Distal radius physeal arrest produces progressive ulnar positive variance with ulnocarpal impaction and radial deviation:
    • Options: distal ulnar epiphysiodesis (in a growing child), ulnar shortening osteotomy, or bar resection with fat interposition if the bar is <50% of the physis and >2 years of growth remain.
  • Do not miss the associated injury: Galeazzi and Monteggia equivalents are easily overlooked — image the elbow and wrist in every forearm fracture and check the radiocapitellar line and DRUJ.

Paediatric lateral condyle & medial epicondyle fractures

Two elbow fractures with very different management thresholds.

5 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • Lateral condyle fractures are intra-articular Salter-Harris IV injuries that displace readily and go on to nonunion. Displacement drives management:
    • <2 mm → cast immobilisation with weekly radiographs (they displace late — follow closely).
    • 2–4 mm with an intact cartilage hinge → closed reduction and percutaneous pinning, or arthrogram-assisted assessment.
    • >4 mm or an incongruent articular surface → open reduction and internal fixation.
  • Imaging: the internal oblique view is the most accurate for assessing displacement — standard AP and lateral underestimate it. An intra-operative arthrogram defines articular congruity.
  • Complications of lateral condyle fractures:
    • Nonunion — the most feared (intra-articular location, extensor origin pull, tenuous blood supply).
    • Cubitus valgus with a late tardy ulnar nerve palsy.
    • Lateral spur formation — very common, usually cosmetic and benign.
    • AVN of the lateral condyle from aggressive POSTERIOR dissection — never strip posteriorly, where the blood supply enters.
  • Fishtail deformity: a central defect of the distal humeral trochlea from osteonecrosis of the lateral aspect of the medial crista, after a lateral condyle, supracondylar or T-condylar fracture.
    • Usually asymptomatic or an incidental finding; it does not routinely progress to major dysfunction and is not a cause of progressive cubitus varus.
  • Medial epicondyle fractures are extra-articular apophyseal avulsions pulled by the flexor-pronator origin and UCL:
    • Occur with elbow dislocation in ~50% and in the throwing athlete.
    • The controversy is the displacement threshold (historically 5 mm) — plain-film measurement is unreliable; CT is more accurate.
  • Absolute indications for ORIF of a medial epicondyle fracture:
    • An INCARCERATED (intra-articular) fragment; an open fracture; ulnar nerve dysfunction with an entrapped fragment.
    • Relative: significant displacement (5–15 mm) in a high-demand throwing athlete or gymnast, and valgus instability.
  • The non-incarcerated displaced medial epicondyle with a congruent joint is treated non-operatively in a long-arm cast:
    • Reliably produces a fibrous union that is functionally asymptomatic in the great majority.
    • Studies show equivalent functional outcomes to surgery, with the main difference being a higher rate of radiographic nonunion.
  • Recognising an incarcerated fragment is the critical skill — use CRITOE:
    • Capitellum ~1 yr, Radial head ~3, Internal (medial) epicondyle ~5, Trochlea ~7, Olecranon ~9, External (lateral) epicondyle ~11.
    • If the trochlea appears ossified but the medial epicondyle is absent from its normal position, the "trochlear" ossification centre IS the incarcerated epicondyle.
  • Ulnar nerve symptoms occur in 10–15% acutely; routine transposition is not required, but identify and protect the nerve during ORIF.

Clubfoot (talipes equinovarus) & Ponseti method

Deformity components, the Ponseti sequence, and recurrence.

6 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • The deformity — CAVE (and the correction sequence follows the same order):
    • Cavus (forefoot, from first-ray plantarflexion/forefoot pronation relative to the hindfoot) — corrected FIRST.
    • Adductus (forefoot); Varus (hindfoot); Equinus (hindfoot) — corrected LAST.
  • The most commonly tested technical point — correcting the cavus:
    • SUPINATE the forefoot and elevate (dorsiflex) the first ray to align it with the hindfoot.
    • Pronating the forefoot is WRONG and worsens the cavus.
    • The whole foot is then abducted around the head of the talus as a fulcrum, with counter-pressure on the lateral talar head — never press on the calcaneus or lateral foot.
  • Equinus is corrected last, after ~60–70° of abduction is achieved:
    • 70–90% require a percutaneous Achilles tenotomy, followed by 3 weeks in a cast.
  • Bracing is mandatory — foot abduction orthosis (Denis Browne bar, affected foot at 60–70° external rotation):
    • 23 hours/day for 3 months, then night-time and nap-time until age 4–5 years.
  • Non-compliance with the foot abduction orthosis is BY FAR the greatest risk factor for recurrence — increasing it roughly five- to seventeen-fold. This is the single most important thing to counsel the family about.
  • Ponseti has replaced extensive surgical release, which produced a stiff, painful, weak adult foot. Ponseti corrects >90% of idiopathic clubfeet and works even in older infants and delayed presentations.
  • Recurrence (~20–40%) presents first as recurrent equinus and hindfoot varus:
    • Manage with repeat Ponseti casting, then percutaneous Achilles tenotomy if needed.
    • For a dynamic supination/adduction deformity in a walking child (>2.5–3 years) → tibialis anterior tendon transfer to the lateral cuneiform (which must be ossified).
    • Bony procedures are reserved for older children with rigid deformity.
  • Other features: the commonest residual deformity is equinus with hindfoot varus; expect a smaller foot and calf, a shorter limb, and internal tibial torsion.
  • Non-idiopathic clubfoot (arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele, amniotic band, Möbius) is stiffer, responds less well and recurs more — but Ponseti is still first-line.

Paediatric ankle fractures: Tillaux & triplane

The transitional fractures of the closing distal tibial physis.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2023 (x3), 2024S

  • Transitional fractures occur in adolescents (12–15 years) during the ~18-month window of asymmetric physeal closure:
    • The distal tibial physis closes CENTRAL first, then MEDIAL (anteromedial), then LATERAL last.
    • The still-open anterolateral portion is the last to close — and is the part avulsed.
  • Juvenile Tillaux fracture:
    • A Salter-Harris III of the anterolateral distal tibial epiphysis, avulsed by the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament.
    • Mechanism = external rotation; a two-fragment, purely epiphyseal injury.
  • Triplane fracture — components in all three planes:
    • Sagittal through the epiphysis; transverse through the physis; coronal through the metaphysis.
    • So it looks like a Salter-Harris III on the AP and a Salter-Harris II on the lateral.
    • May be 2-, 3- or 4-part; lateral is more common than medial.
  • CT is essential for both — it defines fragment number, fracture planes and, critically, articular displacement, which plain films consistently underestimate.
  • Treatment threshold:
    • <2 mm articular displacement → closed reduction and cast (internal rotation for a Tillaux; internal rotation in a long-leg cast for a lateral triplane).
    • ≥2 mm → closed or open reduction with epiphyseal (± metaphyseal) screws placed parallel to, not across, the physis.
  • Growth arrest is generally NOT a concern in true transitional fractures — the key conceptual point and the standard "all true except":
    • Because the physis is already closing, minimal growth remains.
    • The priority is anatomic restoration of the articular surface to prevent post-traumatic arthritis, not the physis.
  • In contrast, a SH III/IV medial malleolar fracture in a YOUNGER child (e.g. 10 years) carries a high risk of a medial physeal bar and progressive VARUS:
    • Mechanism is supination-inversion; requires anatomic reduction and close serial follow-up.
    • If a bar forms: <50% of the physis with >2 years of growth remaining → bar resection with interposition (fat, cranioplast); larger bars or minimal growth → completion epiphysiodesis ± contralateral epiphysiodesis and corrective osteotomy.

Paediatric polytrauma & resuscitation

How the injured child differs from the injured adult.

4 of 7 sittings2022, 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2024F (x2)

  • Physiologic differences — the central point:
    • Children maintain blood pressure until ~30–45% of blood volume is lost — hypotension is a LATE and pre-terminal sign.
    • Tachycardia is the earliest and most reliable sign of hypovolaemia, then decreased peripheral perfusion, narrowed pulse pressure and altered mental state.
    • Never wait for hypotension.
  • Resuscitation numbers:
    • 20 mL/kg warmed isotonic crystalloid bolus, repeated once.
    • If still unstable after 2 boluses (40 mL/kg) → blood, 10–20 mL/kg packed cells (many centres move to blood earlier in haemorrhagic shock).
    • Maintenance uses the 4-2-1 rule.
  • Anatomic differences and their consequences:
    • Relatively larger head → more head injury, more heat loss.
    • More compliant, cartilaginous skeleton → significant internal injury without fracture (pulmonary contusion without rib fractures; solid organ injury without lower rib fractures).
    • Higher surface-area-to-mass ratio → rapid hypothermia. Mobile mediastinum; smaller airway.
  • Injury patterns:
    • Head injury is the leading cause of death in paediatric trauma.
    • Solid organ (liver/spleen) injury is commoner and more often managed non-operatively than in adults.
    • Paediatric pelvic fractures are less often associated with catastrophic haemorrhage because the elastic pelvis absorbs energy without disruption.
  • Paediatric pelvic ring fractures:
    • Majority are stable, treated non-operatively, healing rapidly with high remodelling potential.
    • NOT usually associated with massive haemorrhage requiring angioembolisation — the classic "what is false" item.
    • But associated intra-abdominal, genitourinary and head injuries are common, and triradiate cartilage injury risks acetabular growth arrest.
  • Outcome: children have lower mortality than adults for an equivalent ISS, with faster healing, greater remodelling, and much less nonunion, fat embolism and ARDS — but a higher rate of long-term neurologic disability after head injury.
  • Practical points:
    • Length-based (Broselow) tape for weights and drug/tube sizes.
    • Intraosseous access after two failed IV attempts.
    • Children desaturate rapidly (higher O₂ consumption, lower FRC); aggressively prevent hypothermia.
    • Consider non-accidental injury in every injured child where the history does not match the injury or developmental stage.

Congenital muscular torticollis & atlantoaxial rotatory displacement

Two causes of the tilted, rotated head — with very different management.

4 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022, 2024S, 2025

  • Congenital muscular torticollis (CMT) — fibrosis/contracture of the sternocleidomastoid:
    • The SCM produces ipsilateral side-bending and CONTRALATERAL rotation.
    • So a contracted right SCM gives the head tilted TOWARD the right with the chin rotated AWAY (left).
    • Working backwards: head tilted right + chin rotated left = RIGHT SCM contracture — the side of the contracture is the side the head tilts toward, not the side the chin points to.
  • Presentation and associations:
    • Noted in the first 2–3 months, often with a palpable non-tender SCM "pseudotumour" that resolves.
    • Always seek the associated "packaging" conditions: DDH (10–20% — examine the hips and image), metatarsus adductus, plagiocephaly.
  • Treatment of CMT:
    • Passive stretching and physiotherapy is >90% effective if started early (before 1 year).
    • Surgical release (unipolar or bipolar) for failure by ~12–18 months or a fixed deformity — best between 1 and 4 years, before permanent facial and skull asymmetry develops.
  • Torticollis WITHOUT a tight SCM, or of acute onset, is not CMT — differential includes AARD, ocular torticollis (superior oblique palsy), Klippel-Feil, posterior fossa/spinal cord tumour, syringomyelia, Sandifer syndrome. Non-CMT torticollis warrants neuraxis imaging.
  • Atlantoaxial rotatory displacement (AARD) — the acute painful "cock-robin" posture:
    • Head tilted one way with chin rotated to the opposite side, and the SCM on the side of chin rotation is in spasm — the opposite muscle pattern to CMT (here the SCM is trying to correct, not cause, the deformity).
    • Follows minor trauma, upper respiratory infection or retropharyngeal inflammation (Grisel syndrome).
  • Fielding & Hawkins classification:
    • I rotatory fixation without anterior displacement (normal ADI, transverse ligament intact) — commonest.
    • II with 3–5 mm anterior displacement (unilateral facet subluxation); III >5 mm (both facets); IV posterior displacement.
  • Diagnosis: plain films (open-mouth odontoid showing lateral mass asymmetry) are suggestive but confounded by the rotated position. Dynamic CT (head in maximal rotation each way) is definitive — the C1-C2 relationship does not change with rotation.
  • Management by duration — the key clinical point:
    • <1 week → soft collar, analgesia/anti-inflammatories, observation — most resolve spontaneously.
    • 1 week–1 month → halter traction, muscle relaxants, analgesia.
    • >1 month (chronic) → halo traction; consider C1-C2 fusion for irreducible or recurrent cases.

Congenital vertical talus & paediatric flatfoot

Distinguishing the rigid rocker-bottom foot from the common flexible flatfoot.

4 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022, 2023, 2024S

  • Congenital vertical talus (CVT) — a rigid rocker-bottom flatfoot with fixed dorsal dislocation of the navicular on the talar head:
    • The talus is vertical and "points at the floor"; hindfoot in equinus and valgus, forefoot dorsiflexed and abducted.
    • The opposite of clubfoot in the forefoot, but sharing hindfoot equinus.
  • The diagnostic radiograph is the FORCED PLANTARFLEXION LATERAL:
    • In CVT the talo-first metatarsal alignment does NOT correct (the navicular stays dorsally dislocated).
    • In the commoner oblique talus or flexible flatfoot, it reduces.
    • A forced dorsiflexion lateral confirms the fixed hindfoot equinus.
  • CVT is associated with an underlying condition in ~50% — neuromuscular disease (myelomeningocele, arthrogryposis), chromosomal abnormalities (trisomy 13/15/18), syndromes. Every child needs neurologic and genetic evaluation including spinal imaging.
  • Treatment of CVT — the reverse Ponseti (Dobbs) method:
    • Serial manipulation and casting with the foot in plantarflexion and inversion (the reverse of clubfoot casting).
    • Then percutaneous reduction and pinning of the talonavicular joint with a percutaneous Achilles tenotomy.
    • This has largely replaced extensive soft-tissue release, which produced stiff, painful feet.
  • Flexible paediatric flatfoot is normal in young children (the arch develops by ~5–10 years):
    • Usually asymptomatic and needs no treatment. Orthotics do NOT change the natural history or create an arch — they may relieve symptoms.
    • The key test is flexibility: the arch reconstitutes on toe standing (heel rise) and with the Jack toe-raise test, and the hindfoot inverts.
  • A RIGID flatfoot is pathological until proven otherwise — differential is tarsal coalition (commonest), CVT, accessory navicular with PTT dysfunction, and neurologic causes.
  • Tarsal coalition presents in adolescence as the coalition ossifies:
    • Calcaneonavicular — ossifies 8–12 years; 45° internal oblique view, "anteater nose" sign.
    • Talocalcaneal (middle facet) — ossifies 12–16 years; lateral "C sign" and talar beak; CT is the best study.
    • Treat symptomatic coalitions with immobilisation first, then resection with interposition (fat/EDB); fuse if extensive or arthritic.
  • Symptomatic accessory navicular with a normal arch → non-operative first; if refractory, modified Kidner (excision with PTT advancement). A flatfoot reconstruction is NOT indicated when the arch is normal.
  • Symptomatic flexible flatfoot failing orthotics → lateral column (Evans) lengthening for forefoot abduction, MDCO for hindfoot valgus, ± Cotton osteotomy for forefoot supination, plus gastrocnemius recession — always assess for equinus.

Paediatric cervical spine: Pseudosubluxation & normal variants

Normal paediatric radiographic variants that mimic injury.

4 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022, 2024S

  • Anatomic differences of the immature cervical spine:
    • Relatively larger head; horizontal, shallow facet joints; incompletely ossified wedge-shaped vertebral bodies.
    • Increased ligamentous laxity and elastic soft tissues; underdeveloped uncinate processes; weaker neck musculature.
    • Consequence: the fulcrum of motion is HIGHER (C2-C3) in the young child, migrating caudally to the adult C5-C6 by ~8–10 years.
  • Pseudosubluxation — the commonest normal variant:
    • Physiologic anterior displacement of C2 on C3 (and less so C3 on C4) of up to 3–4 mm, in up to 40% of children under 8, accentuated by flexion.
    • Swischuk's (posterior cervical/spinolaminar) line distinguishes it from a true injury — a line from the anterior cortex of the C1 spinous process to that of C3 should pass within 1–2 mm of the C2 spinolaminar line.
  • Other normal variants:
    • Atlanto-dens interval up to 4–5 mm in children (vs 3 mm in adults).
    • Absent cervical lordosis in up to 15%; anterior wedging of vertebral bodies.
    • The dens synchondrosis fuses at ~6 years and can be mistaken for a fracture; widened prevertebral soft tissues with crying/expiration.
  • SCIWORA (spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality):
    • Occurs almost exclusively under age 8 — the elastic column stretches and rebounds while the cord cannot (the cord tolerates ~5 mm of stretch, the column ~5 cm).
    • Normal radiographs and CT do not exclude a cord injury — MRI is required in any child with neurologic symptoms.
    • Delayed onset of deficit hours after injury is characteristic.
  • Practical immobilisation: use a spine board with an occipital recess or a thoracic elevation pad — a standard board forces the large head into flexion. Use paediatric-specific clearance rules (PECARN); the Canadian C-Spine Rule was not validated under age 16.
  • Occipitocervical (atlanto-occipital) dissociation is proportionally more common in children (horizontal occipital condyles, ligamentous laxity) and frequently fatal:
    • It is NOT stable and must never be managed with a collar alone — treatment is occipitocervical fusion.
    • Traction is contraindicated. Assess the BAI/BDI and condylar-C1 interval on CT.
  • The commonest paediatric cervical injuries are in the UPPER cervical spine (occiput to C2) — atlanto-occipital dissociation, odontoid synchondrosis fractures, and atlantoaxial rotatory displacement.

Physeal injury, growth arrest & angular deformity

Physeal anatomy, Salter-Harris, and post-traumatic deformity.

3 of 7 sittings2022, 2024S (x2), 2024F (x2)

  • Physeal zones (epiphysis → metaphysis):
    • Reserve/resting → proliferative (longitudinal growth; highest cell division; where the physeal blood supply enters) → hypertrophic (maturation, degeneration, provisional calcification — the WEAKEST zone, where Salter-Harris fractures propagate) → metaphysis (primary spongiosa).
  • Zone-specific disease — a classic matching question:
    • Proliferative — achondroplasia (FGFR3 inhibits chondrocyte proliferation).
    • Hypertrophic — SCFE, rickets, most fractures.
    • Reserve — mucopolysaccharidoses. Primary spongiosa/metaphysis — scurvy, metaphyseal dysplasia.
  • Salter-Harris classification:
    • I through the physis; II physis + metaphyseal (Thurston-Holland) fragment — the commonest.
    • III physis + epiphysis (intra-articular); IV metaphysis + physis + epiphysis (intra-articular); V crush.
    • Arrest risk rises with grade (III–V), high energy, repeated/late reduction attempts, and location (distal femur is the highest-risk physis).
  • Cozen phenomenon:
    • A proximal tibial metaphyseal fracture in a young child (2–6 years) treated non-operatively, developing progressive VALGUS at 12–18 months.
    • Mechanism: relative overgrowth/asymmetric stimulation of the medial proximal tibial physis (± fibular tethering, soft-tissue interposition).
    • The critical management point is that it largely resolves spontaneously over 1–2 years — observe and reassure; do not rush to osteotomy.
  • Physeal bar management:
    • Bar <50% of the physeal area with >2 years (or >2 cm) of growth remaining → bar resection with interposition (fat graft, cranioplast/PMMA, silicone) ± deformity correction.
    • Bar >50%, or minimal growth remaining → completion epiphysiodesis ± contralateral epiphysiodesis, with corrective osteotomy as needed.
    • Map the bar with MRI or CT.
  • Remodelling potential is greatest in the youngest child, closest to the physis, and in the plane of joint motion. Rotational deformity does not remodel.
  • Growth contributions worth memorising:
    • Distal femur ~9 mm/year (38% of limb growth); proximal tibia ~6 mm/year (27%) — together ~70% of lower limb growth.
    • Proximal humerus ~80% of humeral growth; distal radius ~75–80% of forearm growth — hence their great remodelling potential.

Skeletal dysplasias: Achondroplasia & others

Achondroplasia and the dysplasias that recur on the exam.

4 of 7 sittings2022, 2023, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Achondroplasia — the commonest skeletal dysplasia:
    • FGFR3 gain-of-function mutation, autosomal dominant, >80% sporadic (advanced paternal age).
    • Features: rhizomelic shortening, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, trident hands, genu varum, thoracolumbar kyphosis, lumbar hyperlordosis.
    • Spinal stenosis from short pedicles and decreasing interpedicular distance caudally.
  • Know the shortening terminology: rhizomelic = proximal segment (humerus/femur) — achondroplasia; mesomelic = middle segment (Léri-Weill); acromelic = distal segment/hands and feet.
  • Pre-operative anaesthetic concerns in achondroplasia:
    • Foramen magnum stenosis with cervicomedullary compression — the most important; causes central sleep apnoea, hypotonia and sudden death in infancy.
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea; restrictive lung disease from a small thoracic cage.
    • Difficult airway (midface hypoplasia, large tongue) and difficult neuraxial anaesthesia (spinal stenosis).
  • Thoracolumbar kyphosis is present in infancy and resolves in the majority once the child ambulates. Persistence is associated with apical vertebral wedging and translation, kyphosis >30° past ambulation, and delayed motor development/prolonged unsupported sitting.

    • Pseudoachondroplasia (COMP) — normal facies, normal at birth, presents at 2–3 years, ligamentous laxity, C1-2 instability.

    • Cleidocranial dysplasia (RUNX2/CBFA1) — absent/hypoplastic clavicles, delayed suture closure, coxa vara, dental anomalies.

Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

Classification, associated conditions, AVN risk and contralateral slip prediction.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • What it is: a Salter-Harris I fracture through the hypertrophic zone of the proximal femoral physis.
    • The epiphysis stays in the acetabulum while the metaphysis displaces anteriorly and externally rotates — described as the epiphysis slipping posteriorly and inferiorly.
    • Typical patient: an obese adolescent (10–16 years) with hip, thigh or referred KNEE pain and an externally rotated gait — always examine the hip in a child with knee pain.
  • The Loder stability classification is the most important prognostic factor:
    • Stable = able to weightbear (with or without crutches) → AVN risk <10%.
    • Unstable = unable to weightbear even with crutches → AVN risk 20–60%.
    • Chronicity and severity (Southwick angle: mild <30°, moderate 30–50°, severe >50°) are secondary.
  • Risk factors for AVN in an unstable slip:
    • Instability itself is dominant; slip severity; delay to decompression/fixation.
    • Attempted forceful/complete closed reduction of an unstable slip (gentle incidental reduction from positioning is accepted).
    • Screw placement in the posterosuperior quadrant of the epiphysis, where the retinacular vessels enter.
  • Treatment is in-situ fixation with a single central screw perpendicular to the physis, aiming for the centre of the epiphysis on both views and avoiding the posterosuperior quadrant.
    • For unstable slips, many favour urgent (within 24 hours) fixation with capsular decompression.
    • Severe slips may be treated with a modified Dunn procedure (surgical hip dislocation with subcapital realignment) — powerful correction but a significant AVN rate.
  • Screen for endocrine/metabolic disease when SCFE occurs outside the typical age range (<10 or >16), in a child of normal or low weight, or bilaterally at presentation:
    • Hypothyroidism, growth hormone therapy/deficiency, hypogonadism, panhypopituitarism, renal osteodystrophy, and prior radiation therapy.
    • Hyperthyroidism and hyperparathyroidism are NOT classic associations — a standard distractor.
  • Predicting a contralateral slip:
    • Risk is ~20–40%, and most occur within 18 months.
    • The posterior sloping angle (PSA) on the frog-leg lateral is the most predictive measure — PSA >14° carries markedly increased risk (>12–14° is the quoted threshold).
  • Prophylactic contralateral pinning is indicated for an endocrinopathy or renal disease, an open triradiate cartilage with young age at presentation, a PSA >14°, and where reliable follow-up is doubtful — otherwise close surveillance is acceptable.

Arthrogryposis & congenital knee dislocation

The stiff newborn and the hyperextended knee.

3 of 7 sittings2022, 2024S, 2024F (x2)

  • Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC) — non-progressive multiple congenital contractures from fetal akinesia:
    • The classic form is amyoplasia: symmetric featureless cylindrical limbs, absent skin creases, internally rotated shoulders, extended elbows, flexed wrists, clubfeet.
    • Intelligence is normal, sensation is preserved, and it is NOT progressive — distinguishing it from muscular dystrophy and neurologic disease.
    • Amyoplasia is sporadic and not inherited (some syndromic forms are genetic) — key counselling for families.
  • Management — the goal is FUNCTION, not normal anatomy:
    • Aggressive early physiotherapy and serial casting in the first months, when joints are most responsive; surgery for residual deformity.
    • Priorities: a plantigrade foot for walking, and at least one elbow that flexes for hand-to-mouth function (and one that extends for perineal care).
  • Hips in arthrogryposis:
    • Unilateral dislocation → generally reduce (open reduction).
    • Bilateral dislocations are controversial and many are left alone — surgery risks stiffness, and a stiff reduced hip is worse than a mobile dislocated one in a patient whose problem is stiffness. Motion matters more than reduction here.
  • Clubfoot in arthrogryposis is rigid and recurrent — Ponseti is still first-line but needs more casts, more frequent tenotomy, and has a much higher recurrence rate; talectomy may be required.
  • Congenital knee dislocation/hyperextension — tibia anterior to the femur:
    • Associated with arthrogryposis, Larsen syndrome, myelomeningocele, DDH in ~50% (always examine the hips), clubfoot, and breech position.
    • It is NOT associated with cerebral palsy.
  • Larsen syndrome (FLNB) — multiple congenital joint dislocations (knees, hips, elbows), flattened facies with depressed nasal bridge and hypertelorism, and critically CERVICAL KYPHOSIS AND INSTABILITY — screen before any anaesthetic.
  • Treatment of congenital knee dislocation:
    • Early serial casting/splinting into progressive flexion in the first weeks succeeds in the majority; surgery (quadriceps V-Y lengthening, anterior capsular release) for failures.
    • Treat the KNEE BEFORE the hip — a Pavlik cannot position the hip while the knee is hyperextended.

Congenital scoliosis & vertebral anomalies

Failures of formation and segmentation, progression risk, and the mandatory work-up.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • Congenital scoliosis arises from a vertebral defect in the 4th–6th week of gestation:
    • Failures of FORMATION — hemivertebra (fully segmented, semi-segmented, incarcerated), wedge vertebra.
    • Failures of SEGMENTATION — unilateral unsegmented bar, block vertebra. Or mixed.
  • Progression risk, worst to best — the key list:
    • Unilateral unsegmented bar WITH a contralateral hemivertebra — the worst (>10°/year, virtually 100% progression).
    • Then unilateral unsegmented bar > fully segmented hemivertebra > semi-segmented hemivertebra > wedge vertebra > block vertebra (essentially non-progressive).
  • Bracing does NOT work for the congenital curve itself (it is structural) — it may control a flexible compensatory curve. A central management point.
  • Mandatory work-up — the highest-yield component:
    • The deformity forms when the cord, heart and kidneys are forming, so ~30–40% have intraspinal anomalies and 20–40% have extraspinal (VACTERL) anomalies.
    • Every child needs MRI of the whole neuraxis (tethered cord, diastematomyelia, syrinx), renal ultrasound and cardiac echocardiography.
  • Surgical options:
    • Hemivertebra excision with short-segment fusion — best for a lumbosacral/thoracolumbar fully segmented hemivertebra causing trunk imbalance, ideally young (2–5 years) with a short fusion.
    • In-situ fusion for a slowly progressive curve without significant deformity; convex hemiepiphysiodesis in selected young patients; growth-friendly techniques for long curves.
    • Early, short fusion beats a long fusion later — intervene while the curve is short and flexible.
  • Neuromonitoring is essential — neurologic risk is higher than in idiopathic scoliosis because of anomalous anatomy and associated cord pathology. Always image the cord first.

Osteochondritis dissecans (capitellum & knee) in the young

Paediatric discitis & spine infection

The toddler who refuses to walk or sit.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2023, 2024S, 2025

  • Paediatric discitis — a low-grade infection of the disc and adjacent endplates:
    • Predominantly under 5 years (peak 6 months to 4 years).
    • Possible because the child's disc retains a vascular supply from the endplates until ~age 8–10, which then regresses.
  • Presentation is non-specific and subtle:
    • Refusal to walk or sit, irritability, back or abdominal pain, a limp, loss of lumbar lordosis with refusal to flex.
    • Most children are afebrile or have only a low-grade fever, with a normal or mildly elevated WBC.
    • The ESR is the most consistently elevated marker.
  • Imaging:
    • Radiographs are normal for the first 2–4 weeks, then show disc-space narrowing with endplate irregularity.
    • MRI is the study of choice and is diagnostic much earlier; bone scan is an alternative when the site is unclear.

    • Tumour — Langerhans cell histiocytosis (vertebra plana), Ewing sarcoma, leukaemia, neuroblastoma metastasis.
    • Tuberculous spondylitis — classically spares the disc, involves multiple contiguous levels with large paravertebral abscesses and anterior vertebral body destruction.

Spondylolysis & spondylolisthesis in the young

Pars defects, slip grading and the surgical decision.

3 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2024S, 2024F

  • Definitions: spondylolysis = a defect (usually a stress fracture) of the pars interarticularis, most commonly L5, in ~4–6% of the population and far more in athletes doing repetitive hyperextension and rotation (gymnasts, linemen, divers, cricket bowlers, dancers). Isthmic spondylolisthesis is the resulting forward slip.
  • Wiltse classification:
    • I dysplastic (congenital, dysplastic L5-S1 facet, high risk of progression); II isthmic (commonest in the young — IIA lytic, IIB elongated pars, IIC acute fracture).
    • III degenerative (older adults, L4-5, female, intact pars); IV traumatic; V pathologic.
  • Meyerding grading by percentage slip: I <25%, II 25–50%, III 50–75%, IV 75–100%, V spondyloptosis. The slip angle (lumbosacral kyphosis) predicts progression and outcome better than the percentage slip.
  • A lytic L5-S1 slip with radicular pain gives an L5 radiculopathy — the L5 root is compressed in the foramen by the fibrocartilaginous pars mass and descending pedicle (dorsum of the foot, great toe, EHL weakness).
  • Treatment is non-operative in the majority:
    • Activity restriction from the provoking sport, bracing for acute lesions, and core/hamstring rehabilitation.
    • Acute pars stress reactions (marrow oedema on MRI, positive SPECT) have the best chance of bony healing with rest and bracing.
  • Surgical indications: persistent pain despite ≥6 months of non-operative treatment, progressive slip, high-grade slip (>50%), neurologic deficit, or significant deformity/gait disturbance.
    • Direct pars repair — young patient, L1–L4 defect, minimal disc degeneration, no slip.
    • Posterolateral fusion ± instrumentation otherwise; adding instrumentation improves fusion rates but sacrifices the motion segment — favoured in higher-grade slips, older adolescents and a degenerate disc.
  • Reduction of a high-grade slip is controversial — it improves sagittal alignment but risks L5 nerve root injury in up to 20–30%; in-situ fusion has lower neurologic risk but higher pseudarthrosis and no deformity correction.
  • Coexisting scoliosis: often a sciatic/antalgic (olisthetic) curve that resolves when the spondylolisthesis is treated; a true idiopathic curve is treated on its own merits.

    • Mild with a stable hip → lengthening with contralateral epiphysiodesis.
    • Severe → prosthetic fitting with a Syme/Boyd amputation to create a functional prosthetic level, and/or a Van Nes rotationplasty (converting the ankle into a knee).

    • Observation and stretching/orthotics for the calcaneovalgus foot in infancy (it resolves), with serial monitoring of limb length.
    • Contralateral epiphysiodesis for ~2–5 cm; lengthening for larger discrepancies. Osteotomy for residual angular deformity is rarely needed.
  • The multiplier method (Paley) predicts the discrepancy at maturity from a single measurement, using an age- and sex-specific multiplier:
    • It works for CONGENITAL discrepancies that grow proportionately (fibular hemimelia, posteromedial bowing, hemihypertrophy).
    • It does NOT apply to acquired/developmental discrepancies — growth arrest after trauma, infection, physeal injury — and does not predict overgrowth after a femoral shaft fracture. Use serial measurements (Green-Anderson/Moseley) for those.
  • Assessment of limb length: standing block test; scanogram/EOS for the true discrepancy; and a bone age (hand/wrist radiograph) for skeletal maturity — plot it, do not assume it from chronological age.

Blount disease & paediatric tibia vara

Distinguishing pathologic from physiologic bowing.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2022

  • Blount disease (tibia vara) — a growth disturbance of the posteromedial proximal tibial physis producing progressive varus, internal tibial torsion and procurvatum:
    • Infantile (<4 years) — often bilateral, associated with early walking and obesity.
    • Adolescent (late-onset) — often unilateral/asymmetric, strongly associated with obesity.
  • The metaphyseal-diaphyseal (Drennan) angle distinguishes it from physiologic bowing:
    • >16° likely Blount; <11° likely physiologic; 11–16° indeterminate — follow up.
    • Also look for medial metaphyseal beaking, depression of the medial plateau, and physeal widening.
  • Langenskiöld staging (I–VI) describes progressive medial physeal changes — stages V–VI have an established physeal bar and need bar resection or completion epiphysiodesis in addition to correction.
  • Treatment by age and stage:
    • <3 years, Langenskiöld I–II → bracing (KAFO) may be tried, though efficacy is debated.
    • >3–4 years, or progressive/advanced → proximal tibial (and fibular) valgus-producing osteotomy with correction of internal torsion — ideally before age 4 to reduce recurrence.
  • Focal fibrocartilaginous dysplasia (FFCD) is the key differential for unilateral tibia vara in a young child:
    • A benign, self-limiting fibrocartilaginous lesion at the pes anserinus insertion on the medial proximal tibial metaphysis.
    • Abrupt varus with a well-defined radiolucent lesion with sclerotic margins — observe; most resolve spontaneously.
  • Other causes of pathologic tibia vara: rickets/metabolic bone disease, skeletal dysplasia (metaphyseal chondrodysplasia), physeal injury from trauma or infection, osteogenesis imperfecta.
  • In the adolescent form the deformity is often multiplanar with a distal femoral component — full-length standing radiographs are essential to localise the deformity before planning correction.

Congenital radioulnar synostosis & upper limb anomalies

The stiff forearm and other congenital upper limb differences.

2 of 7 sittings2024S, 2024F

  • Congenital radioulnar synostosis — failure of longitudinal segmentation at ~5–7 weeks producing a bony or fibrous proximal bridge with a FIXED forearm position, most often in PRONATION:
    • Bilateral in ~60%; often autosomal dominant with variable penetrance.
  • Diagnosis is frequently DELAYED (often to age 3–6 years or later):
    • Children compensate remarkably well with shoulder and wrist motion, and the deformity is not visually obvious.
    • The presenting complaint is difficulty with tasks needing supination — receiving change, holding a bowl, eating with a spoon.
    • Elbow flexion/extension is preserved; it is forearm rotation that is absent. Radiographs show proximal fusion with a hypoplastic, often posteriorly dislocated radial head.
  • Management is based on the FIXED POSITION and functional impairment, not the presence of the synostosis:
    • Mild-to-moderate pronation (up to ~30–60°) is well tolerated and requires NO treatment — the key management point.
    • Surgery = derotational osteotomy through the synostosis mass to reposition into a more functional degree of pronation.
    • Attempts to RESTORE rotation (excision with interposition) have poor, unpredictable results with recurrence — the goal is repositioning, not motion restoration.
    • Neurovascular compromise from acute derotation is the main surgical risk — gradual correction is safer for large corrections.
  • Other congenital upper limb anomalies to recognise:
    • Radial longitudinal deficiency (radial club hand) — associated with VACTERL, Fanconi anaemia (obtain a chromosomal breakage test — fatal if missed), Holt-Oram (cardiac), TAR syndrome.

Discoid meniscus

The congenital meniscal variant of childhood.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2022

  • What it is: a congenital variant in which the meniscus is abnormally thick and disc-shaped rather than semilunar:
    • Almost always LATERAL (~3–5%); medial discoid is very rare (<0.3%). Bilateral in ~20%.
  • Watanabe classification:
    • I complete — covers the entire lateral plateau, normal posterior attachments.
    • II incomplete — partial coverage, normal attachments.
    • III Wrisberg variant — lacks the normal posterior tibial attachment, anchored only by the Wrisberg ligament, hence hypermobile.
  • Presentation:
    • The Wrisberg type presents in a young child with a painless "snapping knee" on extension.
    • Complete/incomplete types are often asymptomatic until they tear, then present in older children with pain, locking, effusion, loss of terminal extension and a lateral joint-line bulge.
  • Imaging:
    • Radiographs — widened lateral joint space, squaring of the lateral femoral condyle, cupping of the lateral plateau, high fibular head.
    • MRI — three or more contiguous 5 mm sagittal slices showing meniscal continuity ("bow-tie sign"), or a transverse meniscal width >15 mm.
  • Treatment:
    • An incidentally discovered ASYMPTOMATIC discoid meniscus requires NO treatment — the key management point.
    • Symptomatic → arthroscopic saucerisation (partial central meniscectomy) leaving a stable 6–8 mm peripheral rim, with repair of any peripheral instability.
    • Total meniscectomy must be avoided — it reliably produces early lateral compartment arthritis (Fairbank changes) in a young patient.
  • Why they tear easily: abnormal collagen orientation, increased thickness and relative central hypovascularity — so they tear with lower-energy mechanisms than a normal meniscus.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy & Friedreich ataxia

Two progressive neuromuscular diseases with characteristic orthopaedic patterns.

1 of 7 sittings2020 (x2)

  • Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD):
    • X-linked recessive, dystrophin gene mutation (absent dystrophin) — affects boys, presenting at 3–6 years.
    • Waddling gait, toe walking, calf pseudohypertrophy, a positive Gowers sign, proximal-to-distal weakness, and a markedly elevated CK.
  • Natural history: loss of independent ambulation at ~10–12 years (later with corticosteroids), then rapidly progressive scoliosis once wheelchair-bound, and death from cardiorespiratory failure in the second-to-third decade. Corticosteroids prolong ambulation and delay scoliosis.
  • Orthopaedic priorities:
    • Maintain ambulation as long as possible (stretching, night splints, KAFOs, selective contracture releases).
    • Operate on the SPINE EARLY — scoliosis develops in ~90% after ambulation is lost and progresses rapidly.
    • Fuse once the curve reaches ~20–30°, while forced vital capacity is still adequate (>30–40% predicted) — waiting makes surgery far more dangerous.
  • Anaesthetic risk in DMD is a critical safety point:
    • Risk of malignant-hyperthermia-like reactions, rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest with succinylcholine and volatile agents.
    • Avoid succinylcholine and inhalational agents — use total intravenous anaesthesia. Assess the cardiomyopathy pre-operatively.
  • Friedreich ataxia:
    • Autosomal recessive, GAA trinucleotide repeat expansion in the frataxin (FXN) gene; presents at 8–15 years.
    • Progressive gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria, loss of proprioception and vibration (posterior column), ABSENT deep tendon reflexes with UPPER motor neuron signs (extensor plantars), cavovarus feet, scoliosis, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
    • The combination of cerebellar signs + cavovarus feet + areflexia is pathognomonic.
  • Orthopaedic care in Friedreich ataxia: the scoliosis behaves more like idiopathic than a typical neuromuscular curve (bracing may be tried in flexible curves), with posterior fusion for progressive curves; cavovarus feet are managed with orthoses, tendon transfers and osteotomies.

Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease

Idiopathic osteonecrosis of the immature femoral head.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2023

  • What it is: idiopathic osteonecrosis of the capital femoral epiphysis, typically 4–8 years (range 2–12), boys 4–5:1, often small for age with delayed bone age — an insidious painless or minimally painful limp with restricted abduction and internal rotation.
  • Features arguing AGAINST Perthes:
    • Symmetric bilateral involvement at the same stage → suggests multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia or hypothyroidism.
    • Age outside the typical range; family history of dysplasia; short stature with other skeletal changes.
  • Herring lateral pillar classification (assessed at the fragmentation stage — the most reliable prognostic tool):
    • A — no loss of lateral pillar height (good outcome).
    • B — >50% of height maintained; B/C border — ~50% with a narrow pillar.
    • C — <50% of height maintained (worst outcome).
  • The two most important prognostic factors are AGE AT ONSET and the lateral pillar class:
    • Under 6 (some say 8) at onset do well regardless of treatment.
    • Over 8 with lateral pillar B/C or C do poorly and benefit most from containment surgery.
    • Loss of containment/subluxation and hinge abduction are further poor signs.
  • The treatment principle is CONTAINMENT — keeping the head within the acetabulum during the plastic phase so it remodels spherically:
    • Non-operative — activity restriction, physiotherapy to maintain abduction, brief traction/casting for a painful stiff hip.
    • Operative — femoral varus osteotomy and/or pelvic (Salter/shelf) osteotomy.
    • Selecting between them (Herring multicentre study) — under 8 at onset, or lateral pillar A at any age, does well non-operatively; over 8 with lateral pillar B or B/C is the group that benefits from containment surgery; lateral pillar C does poorly regardless.
  • Late sequelae: coxa magna, coxa breva, a short neck with trochanteric overgrowth, femoroacetabular impingement, hinge abduction and early osteoarthritis. Salvage: trochanteric advancement, valgus osteotomy for hinge abduction, and head-neck osteochondroplasty for impingement.

Rickets & metabolic bone disease of childhood

Recognising the bowed, short child with metabolic bone disease.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2025

  • Radiographic hallmarks of rickets:
    • Physeal WIDENING with metaphyseal FLARING, CUPPING and FRAYING — especially knees, wrists and costochondral junctions ("rachitic rosary").
    • Generalised osteopenia, bowing of the long bones (genu varum), and in older children Looser zones (pseudofractures).

    • Risk factors: exclusive breastfeeding without supplementation, darker skin pigmentation, limited sun exposure, northern latitude (highly relevant in Canada), maternal deficiency, malabsorption.
    • Biochemistry: low/normal calcium, low phosphate, HIGH ALP, HIGH PTH, LOW 25-OH vitamin D.
  • X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets — the commonest inherited form:
    • X-linked dominant, PHEX mutation, renal phosphate wasting via elevated FGF23.
    • Biochemistry: LOW phosphate, NORMAL calcium, HIGH ALP, NORMAL/low-normal PTH, normal 25-OH vitamin D — the pattern that distinguishes it.

  • Renal osteodystrophy (CKD): HIGH phosphate, low calcium, HIGH PTH; a mixture of osteomalacia and hyperparathyroid bone disease — associated with SCFE and severe deformity.

    • Physiologic — symmetric, corrects by age 2, normal physes and biochemistry.

    • Skeletal dysplasia — short stature with generalised changes.

    • Correcting the biochemistry allows remodelling and often corrects the deformity in the young child.
    • Operating on untreated metabolic bone disease leads to recurrence, delayed union and hardware failure. Guided growth or osteotomy only for residual deformity once corrected.
  • In any child with unexplained bowing, check calcium, phosphate, ALP, 25-OH vitamin D, PTH and renal function.

    • Substantially increased embryonal tumour risk — the same surveillance applies.
  • Other overgrowth conditions to distinguish:
    • Klippel-Trénaunay-Weber — capillary/port-wine stain, venous and lymphatic malformations, limb overgrowth; the vascular malformation is the defining feature.
    • Proteus syndrome (AKT1) — progressive, disproportionate, asymmetric overgrowth with cerebriform connective tissue naevi.
    • Neurofibromatosis type 1 — can produce localised overgrowth.
  • NOT associated with hemihypertrophy — the usual distractors: Turner syndrome (short stature), achondroplasia, and Marfan syndrome (tall but symmetric).
  • Orthopaedic management centres on the limb-length discrepancy:
    • Measure serially with scanograms and bone age; the multiplier method works here because the overgrowth is proportionate/congenital.
    • Treat with contralateral epiphysiodesis timed to skeletal maturity; lengthening or shortening for larger discrepancies.
  • Refer to genetics — establishing the underlying diagnosis determines the tumour surveillance protocol, which is the intervention that saves lives.

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

Classification and orthopaedic implications of JIA.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • Definition: arthritis of unknown cause beginning before the 16th birthday and persisting at least 6 weeks, after excluding other causes.
    • ILAR subtypes: oligoarticular (≤4 joints — commonest, ~50%), polyarticular (≥5 joints, RF-positive or negative), systemic, enthesitis-related, psoriatic, undifferentiated.
  • Oligoarticular JIA — the commonest subtype and the classic exam vignette:
    • A young girl (2–4 years) with a swollen knee, ANA-positive.
    • The critical association is CHRONIC ANTERIOR UVEITIS — ASYMPTOMATIC and can cause blindness.
    • All children with JIA require regular slit-lamp ophthalmologic screening; ANA-positive young girls are highest risk.
  • Systemic JIA (Still disease): quotidian spiking fevers, an evanescent salmon-pink rash, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, serositis, and markedly elevated inflammatory markers/ferritin, with arthritis appearing later. It mimics infection and malignancy — exclude leukaemia and sepsis.
  • Orthopaedic manifestations — local growth disturbance:
    • Chronic hyperaemia around an inflamed joint causes OVERGROWTH early (limb-length discrepancy, an enlarged epiphysis, a valgus knee).
    • Premature physeal closure with shortening late.
    • Also micrognathia from TMJ involvement, leg-length discrepancy, joint contractures, and cervical spine fusion/instability.
  • Modern medical therapy has transformed the disease (methotrexate, TNF inhibitors, IL-1 and IL-6 blockade), dramatically reducing the need for joint surgery. The surgeon's role is now largely intra-articular corticosteroid injection (highly effective), synovectomy in refractory cases, contracture management and late arthroplasty.
  • Always exclude septic arthritis and malignancy in a child with a single swollen joint — a positive ANA and a chronic course do not exclude sepsis, and leukaemia commonly presents with joint pain.
  • Perioperative management: continue conventional DMARDs, hold biologics and time surgery at the end of the dosing cycle, and screen the cervical spine before general anaesthesia.

Paediatric compartment syndrome

How compartment syndrome differs in children.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • The adult sign of "pain out of proportion" is unreliable in a child — too young, too frightened or too distracted to report it, and often given analgesia or a regional block.
    • The paediatric presentation is the "3 A's": increasing Analgesia requirement, Anxiety and Agitation.
    • An escalating analgesia requirement is the earliest and most useful sign.
  • Highest-risk paediatric injuries:
    • Tibial shaft fractures (particularly with a concomitant fibular fracture and high energy).
    • Supracondylar humerus fractures — especially with a floating elbow, vascular injury, or excessive elbow flexion in a cast.
    • Forearm fractures; femoral fractures treated in a 90/90 spica.
  • A floating elbow (ipsilateral supracondylar and forearm fracture) markedly increases the risk and is an indication to fix both fractures rather than cast.
  • Measurement: use compartment pressures when the examination is unreliable (obtunded, very young, regional block).
    • The ΔP (diastolic − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg threshold applies as in adults.
    • Children often have a higher baseline compartment pressure (13–16 mmHg) — interpret absolute values with care.
  • Prevention:
    • Avoid circumferential casts on high-risk injuries — use a splint with room to swell.
    • Release the padding and split the cast to skin — this relieves far more pressure than splitting the cast alone.
    • Avoid elevating the limb above the heart — it reduces perfusion pressure.
  • Treatment is emergent fasciotomy of all involved compartments. Outcomes in children after timely fasciotomy are generally good, with better recovery capacity than adults; delay leads to Volkmann ischaemic contracture.
  • Neonatal compartment syndrome of the forearm is a rare distinct entity — present at birth with a swollen, discoloured forearm and a sentinel skin lesion; usually diagnosed late, with Volkmann contracture the consequence.
