Metastatic bone disease: Workup, prophylactic fixation & prognosis

The work-up that finds the primary without a biopsy, when to fix before the fracture, and the constructs and adjuncts that make fixation durable.

7 of 10 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022 (x4), 2023 (x2), 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • The cardinal rule:
    • NEVER biopsy a destructive bone lesion in an adult over 40 without a full work-up.
    • The commonest cause is METASTASIS, and the primary can usually be found non-invasively.
    • An unplanned biopsy of what turns out to be a primary sarcoma contaminates tissue planes, changes the resection and increases the amputation rate.
    • Equally, a "routine" nail through an undiagnosed sarcoma seeds the entire medullary canal and usually costs the limb.
  • The standard work-up — it finds the primary in roughly 85% of cases:
    • History and physical, including breast, prostate, thyroid and skin examination, plus smoking and weight-loss history.
    • Bloods: CBC, ESR/CRP, electrolytes, calcium, ALP, LDH, SPEP/UPEP with serum free light chains (myeloma), PSA, TSH.
    • Plain films of the WHOLE involved bone (proximal and distal joints included).
    • CT chest, abdomen and pelvis.
    • Whole-body bone scan (or PET) for staging and to find other lesions.
    • MRI of the lesion is for LOCAL staging, not for finding the primary — a point worth being clear about.
    • If a primary is still not identified, THEN biopsy — through a tract that can be excised with the definitive resection, planned by the surgeon who will do the definitive operation.
  • Which primaries, and what they mean:
    • Commonest to metastasise to bone: Breast, Lung, Thyroid, Kidney, Prostate ("BLT with Kosher Pickle").
    • RENAL and THYROID are classically HYPERVASCULAR — embolise within 24–48 hours before surgery or expect catastrophic bleeding. This is a repeatedly tested practical point.
    • LUNG has the worst prognosis (median survival often a few months); breast, prostate, thyroid and myeloma the best.
    • In children, the "metastasis" differential is different — neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma and leukaemia.
    • Distribution: axial skeleton and proximal long bones (red marrow), via Batson's valveless vertebral venous plexus for pelvic/prostatic primaries; distal to the elbow and knee (acrometastasis) is rare and is classically LUNG.
  • The pathophysiology worth knowing:
    • Tumour cells do not resorb bone directly — they stimulate OSTEOCLASTS, chiefly through PTHrP and RANKL upregulation (the "vicious cycle" of Mundy: bone resorption releases growth factors that feed the tumour).
    • Even "blastic" prostate metastases have increased osteoclastic activity — which is why antiresorptives help there too.
    • This is the rationale for bisphosphonates and denosumab as adjuncts.
  • Predicting impending fracture:
    • Mirels' score — four variables each scored 1–3: SITE (upper limb / lower limb / peritrochanteric), PAIN (mild / moderate / functional), LESION (blastic / mixed / lytic), SIZE (<1/3 / 1/3–2/3 / >2/3 of the cortex).
    • ≥9 of 12 predicts impending fracture and warrants prophylactic fixation; ≤7 can be irradiated and observed; 8 is a judgement call.
    • Other classic indications: >50% cortical destruction, a lesion >2.5 cm, a lesion in the peritrochanteric or subtrochanteric femur, and functional/mechanical pain with weight-bearing.
    • Functional (mechanical) pain is the most reliable single clinical indicator — pain that occurs on loading and is relieved by rest.
    • Mirels over-predicts (low specificity), so use it as a prompt rather than a mandate; CT-based structural rigidity analysis performs better but is not widely available.
  • Why fixing BEFORE the fracture is better:
    • Less pain, shorter hospital stay, faster rehabilitation and better function than fixation after fracture.
    • Lower blood loss, fewer complications, and a higher chance of returning to independent mobility.
    • It also allows the operation to be scheduled with embolisation and the oncology team, rather than done as an emergency.
  • Construct principles — fixation for metastatic disease is not fracture fixation:
    • Assume the lesion will NOT heal — the construct must outlive the patient.
    • Protect the ENTIRE bone — a long cephalomedullary nail for the femur, spanning from the head to the condyles, because other deposits are likely.
    • Cement (PMMA) augmentation of a curetted lesion restores immediate load-bearing.
    • Consider ENDOPROSTHETIC replacement for a solitary, extensively destructive, or radioresistant (renal) lesion in a patient with a reasonable life expectancy — a nail through a destroyed femoral head or a large periarticular defect will fail.
    • Load-sharing beats load-bearing where bone stock permits; plates alone in a lytic diaphysis fail.
    • Address the whole patient: hypercalcaemia, anaemia, VTE risk (very high in malignancy), and nutrition.
  • The solitary metastasis — the exception:
    • A SOLITARY bone metastasis with a CONTROLLED primary and a long expected survival — classically renal cell carcinoma, and also thyroid and some breast — is the situation where WIDE EN-BLOC RESECTION with reconstruction can improve SURVIVAL, not just function.
    • Otherwise the goal of surgery is palliative, durable fixation and pain relief, and an aggressive resection is not justified.
    • Survival estimation tools (Tokuhashi, Tomita, PATHFx) help decide how much surgery is warranted — the honest question is whether the patient will outlive the recovery.
  • Adjuvant treatment:
    • Post-operative radiotherapy to the WHOLE surgical field reduces local progression and reoperation; start after wound healing, at roughly 2–3 weeks.
    • Bisphosphonates (zoledronate) and denosumab reduce skeletal-related events (fracture, cord compression, need for radiotherapy or surgery) — denosumab is modestly superior in most trials. Watch for osteonecrosis of the jaw and hypocalcaemia at oncologic doses.
    • Radiosensitivity guides expectations: lymphoma, myeloma, Ewing and small-cell are highly radiosensitive; breast and prostate intermediate; RENAL CELL, MELANOMA, THYROID, SARCOMA and GI are relatively RADIORESISTANT.
    • Modern systemic therapy has substantially extended survival in several primaries — ask the medical oncologist for a realistic prognosis before choosing a construct.
  • Communicating the diagnosis — a tested professional skill, not an afterthought:
    • Tell the patient the diagnosis honestly, even when it is bad news — withholding a serious diagnosis from a competent adult is not justified.
    • Use a structured approach (SPIKES): private setting, find out what they already understand, ask how much they want to know, give a warning shot then plain-language facts in small pieces, respond to emotion, and finish with a plan.
    • Never say "it is definitely benign" or "definitely cancer" before tissue diagnosis — say what is known, what is not, and what the next step is.
    • Refer to a sarcoma/tumour centre BEFORE biopsy when a primary bone or soft-tissue sarcoma is possible — this single decision has the largest effect on the patient's outcome.
    • A family asking for a suspected paediatric osteosarcoma to be managed locally should be told, kindly and clearly, why referral matters.

Osteosarcoma & variants

The conventional disease and its treatment sequence, then the surface and lytic variants and how their prognosis differs.

6 of 10 sittings2017, 2020, 2021 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2025

  • Conventional high-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma:
    • The commonest primary bone SARCOMA (myeloma is the commonest primary bone malignancy overall).
    • Bimodal age — a peak in the second decade (around the growth spurt) and a second peak in older adults with secondary disease.
    • METAPHYSIS of the distal femur > proximal tibia > proximal humerus — around the knee in roughly half.
    • Radiographs: a mixed lytic/blastic lesion with aggressive periosteal reaction — Codman triangle, "sunburst"/spiculated pattern — a wide zone of transition, and a soft-tissue mass.
    • Elevated ALP and LDH are prognostic (higher = worse) and are followed during treatment.
  • The defining histology and the treatment sequence:
    • Malignant spindle cells producing OSTEOID — the presence of malignant osteoid is the diagnostic criterion, whatever else the tumour contains (chondroblastic, fibroblastic and osteoblastic subtypes all behave the same).
    • Treatment: NEOADJUVANT chemotherapy → WIDE resection → ADJUVANT chemotherapy.
    • Chemotherapy transformed survival from ~20% with surgery alone to ~65–70% for localised disease (MAP: methotrexate, doxorubicin/Adriamycin, cisplatin).
    • Neoadjuvant chemotherapy also allows time to plan and build the reconstruction, and treats micrometastases.
  • Prognostic factors:
    • The strongest prognostic factor is the PERCENTAGE TUMOUR NECROSIS after neoadjuvant chemotherapy — >90% necrosis defines a good responder and predicts markedly better survival (Huvos grading).
    • The presence of METASTASES at diagnosis is the worst prognostic feature — roughly 20% at presentation, and survival falls to ~20–30%.
    • Other adverse factors: axial/pelvic location, large size, elevated ALP and LDH, older age, pathologic fracture, and a positive resection margin.
    • Metastases go to LUNG first, then BONE.
    • SKIP METASTASES (discontinuous foci within the same bone or across the joint) occur in ~5% and mandate whole-bone MRI before resection — missing one leaves tumour behind.
    • Limb salvage and amputation give equivalent survival; limb salvage gives better function and is standard where a wide margin is achievable.
  • PAROSTEAL osteosarcoma — the best-prognosis variant:
    • Arises on the SURFACE of the POSTERIOR DISTAL FEMUR (the classic site), in the 3rd–4th decade — older than conventional.
    • LOW grade, with the best prognosis of all osteosarcomas (>90% survival).
    • Grows as a heavily ossified, lobulated, stalked mass with a CLEAVAGE PLANE between it and the cortex.
    • Medullary canal involvement occurs in a minority and predicts a worse outcome / higher grade — CT or MRI is needed to assess it.
    • Treated by WIDE RESECTION ALONE — NO chemotherapy if low grade. This is the point most often tested.
    • It can dedifferentiate to high grade, usually at recurrence.
  • PERIOSTEAL osteosarcoma — the intermediate one:
    • Surface of the DIAPHYSIS of the tibia or femur — a different location from parosteal.
    • INTERMEDIATE grade, and CHONDROBLASTIC in its histology.
    • Perpendicular ("hair-on-end") periosteal reaction and a broad-based surface mass.
    • Prognosis is intermediate — better than conventional, worse than parosteal.
    • Usually treated with wide resection ± chemotherapy.
  • TELANGIECTATIC osteosarcoma — the lytic mimic:
    • Purely LYTIC with FLUID-FLUID LEVELS — the classic mimic of an aneurysmal bone cyst, which is why every ABC needs tissue.
    • The discriminator on imaging is thick, nodular, enhancing septa and a soft-tissue mass; on histology, malignant cells in the septa.
    • High rate of pathologic fracture (~25%).
    • With modern chemotherapy the prognosis is AT LEAST AS GOOD as conventional osteosarcoma — it is highly chemosensitive. The old teaching that it is uniformly lethal is wrong, and this reversal is frequently tested.
  • SECONDARY and low-grade central osteosarcoma:
    • Secondary osteosarcoma arises in PAGET disease or previously IRRADIATED bone (and rarely in a bone infarct or chronic osteomyelitis).
    • It occurs in older patients and has a POOR prognosis — often under 20% survival, both from the biology and from the difficulty of resection.
    • Suspect it when a patient with known Paget disease develops new pain and a lytic destructive change.
    • LOW-GRADE CENTRAL osteosarcoma is easily confused histologically with FIBROUS DYSPLASIA — both are bland spindle cells with bone formation.
    • The discriminators are PERMEATIVE growth (entrapping host trabeculae and infiltrating marrow) and MDM2/CDK4 positivity on immunohistochemistry or FISH.
    • Getting this wrong means treating a sarcoma with curettage — hence the rule that a "fibrous dysplasia" that hurts or looks aggressive needs an expert opinion.
  • Predisposing syndromes worth recognising:
    • Hereditary retinoblastoma (RB1) — a very large relative risk, amplified by radiotherapy.
    • Li-Fraumeni syndrome (p53).
    • Rothmund-Thomson syndrome (RECQL4).
    • Paget disease and prior radiation (as above).

Soft-tissue sarcoma: Staging, biopsy & prognosis

What predicts survival versus what predicts local recurrence, the biopsy rules, and the Canadian trial that defined radiotherapy timing.

5 of 10 sittings2017, 2021 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x3), 2025

  • Prognostic factors — and a distinction that is frequently tested:
    • GRADE is the single most important prognostic factor for survival in soft-tissue sarcoma.
    • Then SIZE (>5 cm), DEPTH (deep to fascia), and the presence of METASTASES.
    • MARGIN status predicts LOCAL RECURRENCE, NOT SURVIVAL — the distinction to have ready.
    • Histologic subtype, patient age and anatomic site (retroperitoneal and proximal do worse) also matter.
    • The AJCC staging system for soft-tissue sarcoma is driven by grade, unlike most carcinoma staging, which is driven by size and nodes.
  • When to assume a soft-tissue mass is malignant:
    • >5 cm (larger than a golf ball).
    • DEEP to the fascia.
    • Painful — and remember that most sarcomas are PAINLESS, so painlessness is not reassuring.
    • Enlarging, or recurrent after a previous excision.
    • Any of these means assume malignant until proven otherwise. A "lipoma" that is deep, large or growing is a sarcoma until imaging says otherwise.
  • Imaging and staging:
    • MRI WITH CONTRAST BEFORE biopsy — biopsy changes the MRI appearance with haematoma and oedema.
    • Stage the chest with CT — LUNG is the dominant metastatic site for soft-tissue sarcoma.
    • MYXOID LIPOSARCOMA is the exception — it characteristically metastasises to EXTRAPULMONARY sites: other soft tissue, retroperitoneum and SPINE. It therefore needs whole-body or spinal imaging, not just a chest CT.
    • Lymph node metastasis is rare overall (~3%) but occurs in rhabdomyosarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, clear cell sarcoma, angiosarcoma and synovial sarcoma ("RECAS").
  • Biopsy principles — Mankin's rules:
    • LONGITUDINAL incision, in line with the definitive resection, so the tract can be excised en bloc.
    • THROUGH, not between, compartments — going between compartments contaminates two of them.
    • Meticulous haemostasis — a haematoma spreads tumour cells along tissue planes.
    • Drain (if used) brought out IN LINE with, and close to, the incision, so the tract is excised with the specimen.
    • Do not violate a neurovascular bundle or an uninvolved joint.
    • Ideally performed at the centre that will do the definitive surgery.
    • Mankin's classic studies showed that poorly planned biopsies alter management, worsen outcomes and increase amputation rates — errors were 3–5× more common when the biopsy was done at a referring centre.
  • Which biopsy technique:
    • Core-needle biopsy is accurate in experienced hands (diagnostic in >90%) and is the usual first choice; it can be image-guided.
    • FNA is inadequate — it cannot grade the tumour or assess architecture.
    • Open incisional biopsy remains the gold standard when core biopsy is non-diagnostic.
    • Excisional biopsy is only acceptable for a small (<3 cm), superficial, clearly benign-appearing lesion.
    • Always send tissue for culture as well as histology — infection mimics tumour and vice versa.
  • The unplanned excision ("whoops" procedure):
    • A sarcoma shelled out as a presumed lipoma or ganglion leaves residual tumour in up to 35–60% of re-excision specimens.
    • Management is re-staging MRI, then RE-EXCISION of the entire previous surgical bed including the scar and drain site, usually with radiotherapy.
    • It increases the extent of resection required and the risk of local recurrence — the strongest argument for the "image before you excise" rule.
  • Treatment — limb salvage and radiotherapy:
    • Limb-salvage WIDE RESECTION plus RADIOTHERAPY gives local control equivalent to amputation, with better function (Rosenberg's NCI trial) — the foundation of modern practice.
    • Radiotherapy timing — the Canadian NCIC SR2 trial (O'Sullivan, Lancet 2002):
    • PRE-operative radiation uses a LOWER dose and a SMALLER field, giving better long-term function and less fibrosis, oedema and joint stiffness — but roughly DOUBLE the acute wound complication rate (35% vs 17%), concentrated in the lower limb.
    • POST-operative radiation has fewer wound problems but more LATE fibrosis.
    • Local control and survival are the same either way — the choice is a trade between an early wound problem and a late functional one.
  • Chemotherapy:
    • Chemotherapy has a LIMITED role in adult soft-tissue sarcoma generally — unlike osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma, where it is central.
    • It is used in chemosensitive histologies: rhabdomyosarcoma, and synovial sarcoma in young patients; also considered for large, high-grade, deep tumours.
    • Doxorubicin and ifosfamide remain the backbone; targeted agents are histology-specific.
    • Isolated limb perfusion is an option for otherwise unresectable extremity disease.
  • Surveillance:
    • Local recurrence and metastasis are most likely in the first 2–3 years.
    • Typical follow-up: clinical examination and chest imaging every 3–6 months for 2–3 years, then annually to 10 years.
    • Pulmonary metastasectomy can be curative in selected patients with limited, resectable lung disease and a long disease-free interval.

Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (MFH)

A diagnosis of exclusion that behaves like two different diseases depending on whether it arises in bone or soft tissue.

6 of 10 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • What UPS actually is:
    • UPS (formerly malignant fibrous histiocytoma, MFH) is a DIAGNOSIS OF EXCLUSION: a high-grade PLEOMORPHIC sarcoma with NO identifiable line of differentiation.
    • Modern immunohistochemistry and molecular testing re-classify many historic "MFH" cases as dedifferentiated liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, or pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma.
    • The category has therefore shrunk substantially; "MFH" as a specific entity no longer exists in the WHO classification.
    • The diagnosis requires a panel excluding muscle, adipocytic, neural and epithelial markers — and an MDM2/CDK4 FISH to exclude dedifferentiated liposarcoma.
  • Soft-tissue UPS:
    • One of the commonest adult soft-tissue sarcomas.
    • Typically a DEEP, PAINLESS, ENLARGING mass in the THIGH of a patient in the 5th–7th decade.
    • Behaves like any other high-grade soft-tissue sarcoma — grade, size and depth drive prognosis.
  • UPS of BONE — a different and rarer disease:
    • Rare — under 5% of primary bone malignancies.
    • LYTIC and PERMEATIVE with a WIDE zone of transition, in the metaphysis of a long bone (distal femur is commonest).
    • Often presents with PATHOLOGIC FRACTURE (roughly a third).
    • There is NO MATRIX PRODUCTION radiographically — an aggressive, purely lytic destructive lesion in an adult.
    • Which is exactly why metastasis, myeloma and lymphoma must be excluded first in that age group.
  • The treatment distinction — the most repeatedly tested point:
    • UPS OF BONE is treated LIKE OSTEOSARCOMA: NEOADJUVANT chemotherapy → WIDE resection → ADJUVANT chemotherapy.
    • SOFT-TISSUE UPS is treated with WIDE RESECTION plus RADIOTHERAPY, with a much more limited role for chemotherapy.
    • Getting these the wrong way round is the classic error — the site, not the histology, determines the systemic plan.
    • Response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in UPS of bone is prognostic in the same way as in osteosarcoma.
  • Secondary UPS:
    • It may arise secondarily in a BONE INFARCT, in PAGET disease, or in IRRADIATED bone.
    • New pain or a lytic destructive change in any of those settings should prompt imaging and biopsy.
    • Secondary lesions carry a worse prognosis, as with secondary osteosarcoma.
  • Prognosis and spread:
    • Grade- and stage-dependent, with 5-year survival roughly 50–60% for localised disease.
    • Metastases go to LUNG.
    • Older age, pathologic fracture at presentation, axial location and a poor chemotherapy response all worsen the outlook.
  • Myxofibrosarcoma — the relative worth knowing:
    • Now recognised as a distinct entity, and one of the commonest sarcomas in the elderly.
    • Typically SUPERFICIAL/subcutaneous in the limb — unusual for a sarcoma.
    • It has an infiltrative "tail" along fascial planes on MRI, so it has a notoriously high local recurrence rate and needs generous margins.
    • Metastatic potential is lower than its local aggressiveness suggests.

Atypical lipomatous tumour, liposarcoma & lipoma

Why the same lesion has two names, what it can and cannot do, and the molecular test that settles the diagnosis.

5 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2020 (x2), 2021, 2023 (x2)

  • ALT and WDL are the same lesion with different names by SITE:
    • Atypical lipomatous tumour (ALT) = well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDL) — histologically and molecularly identical.
    • "ALT" is used in the EXTREMITY and trunk — surgically resectable, with an excellent prognosis.
    • "WDL" is used in the RETROPERITONEUM and mediastinum — often unresectable, with high mortality from local effects and repeated recurrence, not from metastasis.
    • The naming exists precisely to stop an extremity lesion being over-treated and a retroperitoneal one being under-treated.
  • What it does and does not do — the classic true/false stem:
    • ALT/WDL does NOT metastasise in its well-differentiated state.
    • It CAN DEDIFFERENTIATE into a high-grade sarcoma with metastatic potential — roughly 2% in the extremity, up to 20% retroperitoneally.
    • So both "they do not dedifferentiate" and "they metastasise" are wrong, and both appear as distractors.
    • Dedifferentiation typically presents as a discrete non-fatty nodule within an otherwise fatty mass — that nodule is what must be biopsied.
  • The molecular signature:
    • MDM2 and CDK4 AMPLIFICATION — from supernumerary ring and giant marker chromosomes derived from 12q13-15 — is the defining molecular feature.
    • MDM2 FISH is used to distinguish ALT/WDL from a benign lipoma when the histology is equivocal.
    • MDM2 is CHARACTERISTICALLY POSITIVE, not "rarely positive" — a wording trap.
    • MDM2 amplification inactivates p53; the same amplification is seen in low-grade central and parosteal osteosarcoma, which is why that test also separates those from fibrous dysplasia.
  • MRI features that should worry you about a "lipoma":
    • Thick (>2 mm) septations.
    • Nodular non-adipose areas or areas of enhancement.
    • Less than 75% fat content.
    • Size >10 cm, and a deep location.
    • Deep, large and non-homogeneous lesions warrant referral, not a shell-out in a day-surgery unit.
    • A simple lipoma follows subcutaneous fat on every sequence, suppresses completely on fat-saturation, and has no enhancing component.
  • Treatment of ALT of the extremity:
    • Marginal or wide excision — a wide resection with a cuff of normal tissue is not required for a well-differentiated lesion.
    • Local recurrence in the extremity is roughly 10–20% (much higher retroperitoneally) — recurrence, not metastasis, is the main risk.
    • Radiotherapy is NOT routine for ALT of the extremity.
    • Long-term surveillance is warranted because recurrence can be late and can be dedifferentiated.
    • Amputation is essentially never indicated for a well-differentiated lesion.
  • The other liposarcoma subtypes:
    • MYXOID / ROUND CELL liposarcoma — t(12;16) FUS-DDIT3. Occurs in younger patients (3rd–5th decade), typically the thigh.
    • It is RADIOSENSITIVE — unusual among sarcomas — and responds well to pre-operative radiotherapy.
    • It characteristically metastasises to EXTRAPULMONARY sites: other soft tissue, retroperitoneum and SPINE — hence whole-body or spinal imaging in addition to a chest CT.
    • The round-cell component (>5%) marks high grade and a much worse prognosis.
    • PLEOMORPHIC liposarcoma — the highest grade and most aggressive, with a high metastatic rate; it behaves like other high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas.
    • DEDIFFERENTIATED liposarcoma — retains MDM2 amplification, behaves as a high-grade sarcoma, and is the entity many historic "MFH" cases turn out to be.
  • Benign lipomatous lesions to distinguish:
    • Ordinary lipoma — the commonest soft-tissue tumour; superficial, mobile, homogeneous fat.
    • Intramuscular (deep) lipoma — infiltrates muscle, recurs if incompletely excised, and is the lesion most often mistaken for ALT.
    • Lipoma arborescens — a frond-like fatty synovial proliferation with a chronic effusion, classically in the suprapatellar pouch.
    • Hibernoma — brown fat, intensely FDG-avid on PET (a benign lesion that looks alarming on PET).

Chondrosarcoma

The sarcoma that surgery alone must cure, why pelvic location is the problem, and how to separate a benign cartilage lesion from a low-grade malignant one.

5 of 10 sittings2020, 2021, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Who gets it and where:
    • The commonest primary bone SARCOMA of adults over 50 — osteosarcoma dominates in children, chondrosarcoma in older adults.
    • Common sites: PELVIS, proximal femur, proximal humerus, scapula and ribs — axial and proximal lesions.
    • Radiographically: a lytic lesion with "rings-and-arcs" or popcorn CHONDROID matrix, endosteal scalloping, cortical thickening or destruction, and often a soft-tissue mass.
    • It is typically slow-growing, and pain often long precedes any radiographic change.
  • The single most important management point:
    • Chondrosarcoma is RADIORESISTANT and CHEMORESISTANT.
    • The treatment is WIDE SURGICAL RESECTION — there is no effective adjuvant to rescue an inadequate margin.
    • The biological reasons: a low mitotic rate, poor vascularity, a dense hyaline matrix limiting drug delivery, and expression of multidrug-resistance proteins.
    • Exceptions worth knowing: mesenchymal and dedifferentiated subtypes are treated with chemotherapy, and proton/carbon-ion radiotherapy is used for unresectable skull-base and sacral disease.
  • Grade and location determine behaviour:
    • Grade I (now "atypical cartilaginous tumour", ACT, in the appendicular skeleton) RARELY metastasises.
    • Grade II and III metastasise to LUNG.
    • Low-grade lesions of the EXTREMITY may be treated with EXTENDED INTRALESIONAL CURETTAGE (burr plus adjuvant) with excellent local control and much better function.
    • PELVIC and AXIAL lesions must be resected EN BLOC, because local recurrence there is high and often fatal — there is no salvage.
    • Pelvic location is the strongest predictor of LOCAL RECURRENCE; larger size and higher grade predict METASTASIS.
    • Chondrosarcoma is one of the few sarcomas where local recurrence itself kills.
  • Dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma:
    • A low-grade cartilage tumour with an abrupt, juxtaposed HIGH-GRADE NON-CARTILAGINOUS component (usually osteosarcoma-like or UPS-like), with a sharp interface between the two.
    • Radiographically it is a cartilage lesion with a separate lytic destructive area and soft-tissue mass — biopsy that area, not the cartilage.
    • Dismal prognosis — under 10% 5-year survival.
    • It is treated with wide resection plus chemotherapy (as for high-grade sarcoma), though the response is poor.
  • Secondary chondrosarcoma:
    • Arises in a pre-existing OSTEOCHONDROMA (~1% solitary, up to 5% in multiple hereditary exostoses, higher in pelvic and proximal lesions) or in an ENCHONDROMA (Ollier, Maffucci).
    • Secondary lesions are usually LOW GRADE with a BETTER prognosis than primary chondrosarcoma — a repeated exam distinction.
    • Red flags for transformation in an osteochondroma: GROWTH after skeletal maturity, new pain, and a cartilage cap >2 cm in an adult (measured on MRI).
    • In Maffucci syndrome the risk of malignancy (including non-skeletal cancers) approaches 100% over a lifetime; in Ollier disease it is roughly 25–30%.
  • Enchondroma versus low-grade chondrosarcoma — one of the hardest problems in orthopaedic oncology:
    • PAIN not attributable to another cause — the single most useful clinical feature. A cartilage lesion found incidentally in a shoulder being investigated for rotator cuff impingement is almost always an enchondroma; a cartilage lesion causing rest pain is not.
    • ENDOSTEAL SCALLOPING >2/3 of the cortical thickness, or over a long segment.
    • Cortical destruction, expansion or periosteal reaction.
    • A soft-tissue mass.
    • Size >5–6 cm.
    • AXIAL location (pelvis, scapula, ribs, proximal femur) — the same lesion is far more likely to be malignant in the pelvis than in the hand.
    • HISTOLOGY ALONE IS UNRELIABLE — the diagnosis is clinical, radiographic and pathologic together. This is the key concept.
    • Serial radiographs at 3–6 months are a legitimate strategy for an indeterminate, asymptomatic lesion.
  • The hand is a special case:
    • Enchondroma is the commonest bone tumour of the hand, and features that would be alarming elsewhere — expansion, cortical thinning, pathologic fracture — are expected and benign there.
    • Chondrosarcoma of the hand is rare and is usually low grade.
    • Conversely, a cartilage lesion in the pelvis or a flat bone should be treated as malignant until proven otherwise.
  • The subtypes worth recognising:
    • CLEAR CELL chondrosarcoma — the LOW-grade EPIPHYSEAL variant; it mimics chondroblastoma but occurs in an ADULT (3rd–5th decade), classically in the femoral head. Treated by wide resection, not curettage.
    • MESENCHYMAL chondrosarcoma — HIGH grade, occurs in the YOUNG, biphasic (small round cells plus cartilage islands), and is CHEMOSENSITIVE; it can arise in soft tissue.
    • Periosteal (juxtacortical) chondrosarcoma — a surface lesion, to be distinguished from periosteal chondroma (<3 cm, benign) and periosteal osteosarcoma.
    • IDH1/IDH2 mutations are found in conventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma and in Ollier/Maffucci — a potential therapeutic target.

Giant cell tumour of bone

A benign tumour that behaves badly — the RANKL biology that explains both the histology and the drug, and how to get local control.

7 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2019 (x3), 2021, 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Who and where:
    • Benign but LOCALLY AGGRESSIVE, in skeletally MATURE patients — 20–40 years, with a slight female predominance.
    • Arises in the EPIPHYSIS/metaphysis and extends to SUBCHONDRAL bone.
    • Classic sites: distal femur, proximal tibia, distal radius and sacrum.
    • Radiographically: an ECCENTRIC, LYTIC, well-defined lesion WITHOUT a sclerotic rim, often extending to within 1 cm of the joint surface.
    • An open physis essentially excludes it — in a child, the same appearance is chondroblastoma (epiphyseal) or ABC.
    • Campanacci grading: I well-marginated with an intact cortex; II expanded thinned cortex; III cortical breach with a soft-tissue mass.
  • Histology and biology — the concept that ties the card together:
    • Multinucleated osteoclast-like GIANT CELLS on a background of MONONUCLEAR STROMAL cells.
    • The NEOPLASTIC cell is the MONONUCLEAR STROMAL cell — the giant cells are reactive.
    • The stromal cell expresses RANKL, which recruits and activates the osteoclast-like giant cells that do the bone destruction.
    • H3F3A (histone H3.3 G34W) mutation is characteristic and is now used diagnostically by immunohistochemistry.
    • The differential for a "giant cell rich" lesion is wide: ABC, chondroblastoma, brown tumour of hyperparathyroidism, non-ossifying fibroma, giant cell reparative granuloma — always check the calcium and PTH.
  • Standard treatment — extended intralesional curettage:
    • A wide cortical window, thorough curettage, then a HIGH-SPEED BURR to extend the cavity beyond the visible tumour.
    • Plus an ADJUVANT: phenol, liquid nitrogen, argon beam or hydrogen peroxide.
    • Then cementation or bone grafting.
    • PMMA cementation is preferred, for three reasons: it allows early detection of recurrence at the cement–bone interface, it provides immediate structural support allowing early weight-bearing, and the exothermic reaction may kill residual cells.
    • Local recurrence is ~10–25%, usually within 2 years — so surveillance imaging for at least that long, including a chest radiograph.
  • When to resect instead:
    • Extensive articular destruction that cannot be reconstructed by curettage.
    • Expendable bones — proximal fibula, distal ulna, rib, and a small metacarpal.
    • Repeated recurrence.
    • Wide resection lowers recurrence but at a functional cost, so it is not the default.
    • A pathologic fracture is not in itself an indication for resection — many can still be curetted after the fracture is controlled.
  • Denosumab — and its caveats:
    • A RANKL monoclonal antibody, so it directly targets the mechanism above.
    • It dramatically reduces tumour activity and induces a peripheral rim of new bone, with impressive radiographic responses.
    • Used for unresectable, axial and sacral disease, and to downstage before surgery (for example to make a Campanacci III lesion curettable).
    • Caution: it makes the tumour hard and sclerotic and may obscure the margins, making curettage of the true tumour boundary harder; several series report higher recurrence after neoadjuvant denosumab followed by curettage.
    • Recurrence is common after stopping, so it is not a definitive treatment for resectable disease.
    • Watch for osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femoral fracture and rebound hypercalcaemia in the young.
  • "Benign pulmonary metastases":
    • Occur in ~2–5% of histologically BENIGN GCT.
    • They are typically indolent, may regress spontaneously, and do NOT indicate malignancy.
    • Chest imaging is nevertheless part of staging and of follow-up.
    • Management is usually observation, or resection if they grow; systemic therapy (denosumab) for progressive disease.
    • They are more common after local recurrence and with distal radius lesions.
  • Malignant transformation — and the radiotherapy warning:
    • Frank malignant transformation is RARE and is strongly associated with previous RADIOTHERAPY.
    • Radiotherapy should therefore be AVOIDED in giant cell tumour wherever possible.
    • A recurrence that looks different — rapid growth, a large soft-tissue mass, new pain — needs re-biopsy for secondary malignancy.
  • The sacrum and spine:
    • GCT of the sacrum and mobile spine is difficult, with a high recurrence rate, and en-bloc resection carries major neurologic and functional cost.
    • This is where DENOSUMAB and SERIAL ARTERIAL EMBOLISATION have their greatest role, often as definitive treatment.
    • Intralesional surgery with adjuvant, after embolisation and denosumab, is a common compromise.

Osteochondroma & multiple hereditary exostoses

The one diagnostic feature, the causes of pain (and the one that is not), and the deformities that define the hereditary form.

3 of 10 sittings2021 (x2), 2024S, 2024F

  • The diagnostic feature:
    • An osteochondroma is a cartilage-capped bony projection with CORTICAL AND MEDULLARY CONTINUITY with the underlying bone.
    • This continuity is THE diagnostic feature and is what distinguishes it from a parosteal osteosarcoma, a periosteal chondroma, or myositis ossificans, all of which sit on the cortex without medullary continuity.
    • It is sessile or pedunculated, METAPHYSEAL, and points AWAY from the joint (drawn along by muscle pull).
    • It is the commonest benign bone tumour, and most are found incidentally.
  • Where it comes from and when it stops:
    • It arises from displaced physeal cartilage that grows outward from the perichondrial ring of Ranvier.
    • It therefore GROWS ONLY UNTIL SKELETAL MATURITY, when its own cartilage cap ossifies.
    • Growth after skeletal maturity, NEW PAIN, or a cartilage cap >2 cm in an adult is the RED FLAG for secondary CHONDROSARCOMA — roughly 1% in solitary lesions, up to 5% in MHE.
    • MRI measures the cap; a cap over 2 cm in an adult should be resected.
  • Causes of pain — and the one that is not:
    • Mechanical irritation and bursa formation over the lesion (a reactive bursa can become large and inflamed).
    • Fracture of the stalk of a pedunculated lesion.
    • Impingement on adjacent tendons or muscles, and snapping.
    • Neurovascular compression — classically the common peroneal nerve at the proximal fibula, and a PSEUDOANEURYSM of the popliteal artery at the distal femur.
    • Malignant transformation.
    • Pain arising from the marrow or medullary canal is NOT a mechanism — the classic "which is not a cause of pain" distractor. The lesion's marrow is continuous and normal.
  • Multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE / hereditary multiple osteochondromas):
    • Autosomal dominant, from EXT1 or EXT2 mutations (heparan sulphate biosynthesis).
    • Short stature and multiple lesions, typically around the knee, proximal humerus, scapula and pelvis.
    • Deformity progresses with growth and is more severe with EXT1, which also carries the higher malignancy risk.
  • The characteristic MHE deformities:
    • FOREARM deformity — ulnar shortening, radial bowing, and radial head subluxation or dislocation (the Masada classification); the ulna is affected because it grows predominantly from its distal physis.
    • ANKLE VALGUS from fibular shortening.
    • Coxa valga and genu valgum.
    • Also: scoliosis, and spinal canal lesions (an indication for MRI screening in some protocols).
  • Surgical management:
    • Excise SYMPTOMATIC lesions at the BASE, including the PERICHONDRIUM and the FULL cartilage cap.
    • Incomplete excision recurs, particularly in skeletally immature children where residual perichondrium regrows.
    • Address forearm and ankle deformity with guided growth (hemiepiphysiodesis), ulnar lengthening, or corrective osteotomy as indicated — and excise the lesions tethering the growth.
    • Routine PROPHYLACTIC excision of asymptomatic lesions is NOT indicated.
    • Warn about the risk of injuring the peroneal nerve when excising a proximal fibular lesion — it is draped over the mass.
  • Related entities:
    • Subungual exostosis (Dupuytren exostosis) of the distal phalanx — a related but distinct entity, presenting as a painful nail-bed lesion in a young adult.
    • Trevor disease (dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica) — an osteochondroma arising from an EPIPHYSIS, usually the medial distal femur or talus, in a child; unilateral and asymmetric.
    • Turret exostosis — a post-traumatic subperiosteal ossification on the dorsum of a phalanx; distinguish from BPOP (Nora lesion), which has no medullary continuity.

Spine tumours & SINS score

Scoring instability, the trial that established surgery before radiation, and locating a primary spine tumour by which part of the vertebra it occupies.

6 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018 (x2), 2020, 2021, 2024F, 2025

  • SINS (Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score) — the six components:
    • LOCATION — junctional (occiput-C2, C7-T2, T11-L1, L5-S1) 3, mobile spine 2, semi-rigid (T3-T10) 1, rigid (S2-S5) 0.
    • MECHANICAL PAIN — pain with movement or loading, relieved by lying down: yes 3, occasional non-mechanical pain 1, none 0.
    • BONE LESION QUALITY — lytic 2, mixed 1, blastic 0.
    • SPINAL ALIGNMENT — subluxation/translation 4, de novo kyphosis/scoliosis 2, normal 0.
    • VERTEBRAL BODY COLLAPSE — >50% 3, <50% 2, no collapse but >50% body involved 1, none 0.
    • POSTEROLATERAL INVOLVEMENT of the spinal elements (facet, pedicle, costovertebral) — bilateral 3, unilateral 1, none 0.
    • MECHANICAL PAIN carries the highest-weighted single CLINICAL variable and is the strongest predictor — the point most often asked.
  • Interpreting the score:
    • 0–6 STABLE.
    • 7–12 INDETERMINATE (potentially unstable) — obtain a surgical consultation.
    • 13–18 UNSTABLE.
    • SINS assesses STABILITY ONLY — it says nothing about neurological compromise or oncologic prognosis.
    • Use it alongside the ESCC (Bilsky) grade for epidural cord compression and a survival estimate (Tokuhashi, Tomita, PATHFx) to decide on surgery — the "NOMS" framework (Neurologic, Oncologic, Mechanical, Systemic).
  • Metastatic epidural cord compression — the defining trial:
    • Patchell's RCT showed that DECOMPRESSIVE SURGERY FOLLOWED BY RADIOTHERAPY is superior to radiotherapy alone for ambulation and continence in patients with a single-level lesion and a reasonable life expectancy.
    • More patients regained the ability to walk, and they retained it for far longer (median 122 vs 13 days).
    • The trial excluded highly radiosensitive tumours and patients with a very short expected survival — those are still treated with radiotherapy alone.
    • The modern paradigm is SEPARATION SURGERY plus STEREOTACTIC BODY RADIOTHERAPY: a limited operation to create a few millimetres of space between the tumour and the cord, allowing a high, ablative SBRT dose to be delivered safely — less morbid than a full resection and effective even for radioresistant histologies.
    • Steroids (dexamethasone) are given immediately in any patient with a neurologic deficit from cord compression.
  • Radiosensitivity guides the whole plan:
    • Highly radiosensitive: LYMPHOMA, MYELOMA, EWING and SMALL-CELL (and germ cell) — radiotherapy alone may suffice even with cord compression.
    • Intermediate: BREAST and PROSTATE.
    • Relatively RADIORESISTANT: RENAL CELL, MELANOMA, THYROID, SARCOMA and GI.
    • The classic "least responsive to radiation" answer is renal cell, melanoma or sarcoma.
    • RENAL metastases must be EMBOLISED before surgery — within 24–48 hours — or bleeding can be uncontrollable.
    • SBRT has substantially changed this, giving good local control even in "radioresistant" histologies.
  • Primary spine tumours by LOCATION — a reliable way to narrow the differential:
    • POSTERIOR ELEMENTS: osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, aneurysmal bone cyst, osteochondroma.
    • VERTEBRAL BODY: haemangioma, giant cell tumour, chordoma, eosinophilic granuloma (vertebra plana), metastasis, myeloma.
    • In the PAEDIATRIC spine the commonest primary bone tumours are osteoid osteoma/osteoblastoma and ABC.
    • The commonest benign vertebral body lesion overall is a HAEMANGIOMA — typically incidental, with a "corduroy/jail-bar" appearance on the lateral radiograph and "polka dots" on axial CT.
    • Chordoma — arises from notochordal remnants, so it is midline: sacrococcygeal (~50%) > skull base (spheno-occipital) > mobile spine. It is slow-growing, locally destructive, radioresistant to conventional doses, and requires en-bloc resection with a wide margin for cure; brachyury is the diagnostic marker, and proton/carbon-ion therapy is used when resection is impossible.
    • Vertebra plana in a child = eosinophilic granuloma (Langerhans cell histiocytosis) until proven otherwise, and it usually reconstitutes height with observation.
  • The destructive vertebral lesion in an adult:
    • A destructive vertebral body lesion in an adult over 40, especially with weight loss, is METASTASIS or MYELOMA until proven otherwise.
    • Preservation of the DISC SPACE with vertebral body destruction favours TUMOUR.
    • DISC destruction favours INFECTION — with tuberculosis the classic exception, which spares the disc and produces large paraspinal abscesses.
    • Pedicle involvement ("winking owl" sign) favours metastasis.
    • Get the full metastatic work-up (see the metastatic disease card) before any biopsy, and remember myeloma needs SPEP/UPEP and free light chains.

Unicameral & aneurysmal bone cysts

Two lytic paediatric lesions that look alike on a plain film and behave completely differently — and the malignancy each can hide.

7 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x5), 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024F, 2025

  • Unicameral (simple) bone cyst — the picture:
    • CENTRAL, METAPHYSEAL, well-defined lytic lesion that does not expand beyond the width of the physis.
    • PROXIMAL HUMERUS (most common) and proximal femur, in the first two decades.
    • It is a fluid-filled cavity lined by a thin fibrous membrane — not a true neoplasm.
    • Classically ASYMPTOMATIC until pathologic fracture — that is usually how it presents.
    • The "FALLEN LEAF / FALLEN FRAGMENT" sign — a fragment of cortex lying DEPENDENTLY within the fluid-filled cyst — is PATHOGNOMONIC, and confirms the lesion is fluid-filled.
    • "Active" cysts abut the physis (in younger children, higher recurrence); "latent" cysts have migrated away from it with growth.
  • Managing a UBC:
    • Most non-displaced pathologic fractures of the proximal humerus are treated NON-OPERATIVELY first — sling and immobilisation.
    • The fracture heals, and a proportion of cysts heal or shrink as a result — so a fracture is an opportunity, not an emergency.
    • Persistent or recurrent cysts: aspiration and injection (methylprednisolone, autologous bone marrow, DBM) — often needing several injections — or curettage with grafting ± decompression with cannulated screws or a flexible nail (which relieve the pressure and keep the cyst decompressed).
    • PROXIMAL FEMORAL lesions are more likely to need INTERNAL FIXATION because of the mechanical demand and the consequences of a femoral neck fracture — the important site-specific distinction.
    • Recurrence is common in young children with an active cyst; expect to treat more than once.
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst — the picture:
    • ECCENTRIC, EXPANSILE, "blown-out" lytic lesion with a thin rim of periosteal new bone.
    • Contains BLOOD-FILLED spaces with FLUID-FLUID LEVELS on MRI or CT.
    • Occurs in the METAPHYSIS of long bones and in the POSTERIOR ELEMENTS of the spine, in the first two decades.
    • Often PAINFUL, including night pain — unlike the typically silent UBC.
    • It can grow rapidly and can look alarming.
  • ABC biology — a genuine change in understanding:
    • ABC is now known to be a TRUE NEOPLASM, with a USP6 gene rearrangement in primary lesions (also seen in nodular fasciitis and myositis ossificans).
    • It can be SECONDARY to another lesion — a reactive ABC-like change within something else.
    • Always exclude TELANGIECTATIC OSTEOSARCOMA, giant cell tumour, chondroblastoma and osteoblastoma.
    • Tissue diagnosis is therefore ESSENTIAL — never treat a "fluid-fluid level lesion" without histology. This is the central safety point of the card.
    • USP6 testing distinguishes a primary ABC from a secondary one, and from telangiectatic osteosarcoma.
  • Treating an ABC:
    • Intralesional CURETTAGE with a HIGH-SPEED BURR and an ADJUVANT, plus PRE-OPERATIVE SELECTIVE ARTERIAL EMBOLISATION — the standard answer, and particularly for a spinal/pedicle ABC in a child, where blood loss can be dramatic.
    • Alternatives for surgically inaccessible lesions: SERIAL EMBOLISATION alone, sclerotherapy (doxycycline, polidocanol), and DENOSUMAB.
    • Recurrence is ~10–20% and is highest in YOUNG children with OPEN physes and in incompletely curetted lesions.
    • En-bloc resection of a spinal ABC gives the lowest recurrence but at a high morbidity cost in a child — intralesional treatment with embolisation is preferred where feasible.
    • Radiotherapy is avoided because of the risk of secondary sarcoma in a child.
  • Telling them apart at a glance:
    • UBC: central, non-expansile (within the physeal width), painless, fluid, fallen fragment.
    • ABC: eccentric, expansile beyond the physeal width, painful, blood, fluid-fluid levels.
    • Both are metaphyseal, both are lytic, and both occur in the same age group — which is why the MRI and the biopsy matter.
    • Fluid-fluid levels are NOT specific to ABC — they occur in telangiectatic osteosarcoma, GCT, chondroblastoma, simple bone cyst and fibrous dysplasia.

Desmoid tumour (fibromatosis)

Locally aggressive but never metastatic — and the tumour where the correct first treatment is now to do nothing.

3 of 10 sittings2023, 2024F, 2025

  • The defining behaviour:
    • Desmoid tumours (aggressive fibromatosis) are LOCALLY AGGRESSIVE but DO NOT METASTASISE.
    • They are infiltrative, cross fascial planes and encase neurovascular structures — which is what causes morbidity.
    • They are typically PAINFUL and FIRM — the notion that they are painless like most benign soft-tissue tumours is a common distractor.
    • The natural history is genuinely unpredictable: some grow, many stabilise, and a substantial proportion regress spontaneously.
  • Molecular basis:
    • SPORADIC lesions have CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutations — roughly 85%, with nuclear β-catenin staining on immunohistochemistry.
    • MULTIFOCAL and MESENTERIC desmoids are associated with FAMILIAL ADENOMATOUS POLYPOSIS (Gardner syndrome) via APC mutations.
    • A patient with multifocal desmoids needs COLONOSCOPY and genetic assessment — the desmoid can be the presenting feature of FAP, and colorectal cancer is the real threat.
    • Both pathways converge on Wnt/β-catenin signalling.
    • The specific CTNNB1 mutation matters: S45F predicts a higher recurrence risk.
  • Risk factors:
    • Previous trauma or a surgical scar — desmoids classically arise in scars, including at previous desmoid excision sites.
    • Pregnancy and oestrogen exposure.
    • Young adulthood — peak 20–40 years, with a female predominance.
    • Common sites: abdominal wall (often post-partum), shoulder girdle, chest wall, thigh and the mesentery.
  • Management has shifted decisively toward ACTIVE SURVEILLANCE:
    • Initial active surveillance is now first-line, because a substantial proportion stabilise or regress spontaneously (roughly 20–30% regress, and over half stabilise).
    • Surgery is NO LONGER first-line.
    • Local recurrence after resection is HIGH (20–60%), and MARGIN STATUS CORRELATES POORLY WITH RECURRENCE — so radical resection is not justified and sacrificing a nerve or a limb for margins is wrong.
    • Surveillance means MRI at 3–6 month intervals initially, with intervention only for progression or symptoms.
    • This is a genuine practice change and is exactly what a "what is true about desmoid tumours" question is testing.
  • Second-line options when the lesion progresses or is symptomatic:
    • Systemic therapy: historically tamoxifen and NSAIDs (sulindac); then methotrexate–vinblastine; then tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sorafenib, pazopanib), both with randomised support.
    • Gamma-secretase inhibitors (nirogacestat) — a Notch-pathway agent with randomised phase 3 evidence, now the newest option.
    • Radiotherapy for symptomatic unresectable disease, with the usual reservations about long-term risk in a young patient.
    • Cryoablation and other percutaneous ablation for accessible extremity lesions.
    • Surgery is reserved for progressive, symptomatic, functionally threatening disease where a reasonable resection is possible.
  • Imaging:
    • MRI shows a T2-HYPOINTENSE, infiltrative lesion with a "FASCIAL TAIL".
    • A LOW T2 signal soft-tissue mass is a useful clue — most sarcomas are T2-bright, so low T2 signal narrows the differential considerably (desmoid, fibromatosis, PVNS/TGCT with haemosiderin, and scar).
    • Enhancement is variable and correlates loosely with cellularity; T2-hypointense, non-enhancing areas represent mature collagen and inactive disease.
    • Core biopsy is still required — the differential includes fibrosarcoma and low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma.
  • Related fibromatoses worth distinguishing:
    • Superficial fibromatoses — Dupuytren (palmar), Ledderhose (plantar), Peyronie; these are small, superficial and do not behave like deep desmoids.
    • Extra-abdominal desmoid (the extremity/girdle lesion above), abdominal wall desmoid (best prognosis, often post-partum) and intra-abdominal/mesenteric desmoid (FAP-associated, the most dangerous).
    • Infantile fibromatosis/fibrosarcoma in a child is a different entity with an ETV6-NTRK3 fusion and a good prognosis.

Bone-forming tumours (general)

The osteoid-producing family and how to separate them.

2 of 10 sittings2020, 2024S

  • The bone-forming family:
    • Osteoma — dense, cortical, skull and facial bones; associated with Gardner syndrome (with desmoids and colonic polyposis).
    • Osteoid osteoma — nidus <2 cm, NSAID-responsive night pain.
    • Osteoblastoma — >2 cm, more aggressive, posterior elements of the spine.
    • Osteosarcoma and its variants — malignant osteoid production.
  • The unifying histologic feature and how benign differs from malignant:
    • All produce OSTEOID by the tumour cells themselves.
    • Benign lesions show OSTEOBLASTIC RIMMING and organised trabeculae.
    • Osteosarcoma shows malignant pleomorphic cells producing LACE-LIKE osteoid with PERMEATION of pre-existing bone — the trabeculae are entrapped, not remodelled.
    • Permeation is the single most reliable low-power feature of malignancy.
  • The two malignant lesions most often mistaken for benign disease:
    • LOW-GRADE CENTRAL osteosarcoma — mistaken for fibrous dysplasia.
    • PAROSTEAL osteosarcoma — mistaken for osteochondroma or myositis ossificans.
    • MDM2/CDK4 immunohistochemistry or FISH distinguishes them — positive in both osteosarcoma variants, negative in the benign mimics.
    • The clinical rule: a "fibrous dysplasia" or "osteochondroma" that hurts, grows in an adult, or looks aggressive needs an expert opinion.
  • Bone island (enostosis) — a "don't touch" lesion:
    • A benign focus of compact (cortical) bone within cancellous bone.
    • Spiculated, brush-like margins that BLEND with surrounding trabeculae — it does not have a sharp border.
    • Typically NO uptake on bone scan — the helpful discriminator from a blastic metastasis, which is hot.
    • A giant bone island can be up to several centimetres; interval growth of more than ~25% over 6 months should prompt further work-up.
  • Blastic lesions in an adult — the differential:
    • Prostate and breast metastasis (the commonest by far).
    • Lymphoma (the "ivory vertebra").
    • Paget disease — with cortical thickening, trabecular coarsening and bone enlargement.
    • Bone island.
    • Osteosarcoma, including secondary osteosarcoma in Paget.

Enchondroma, Ollier & Maffucci

Benign intramedullary cartilage lesions and their syndromic, malignant-prone forms.

3 of 10 sittings2019, 2020, 2025

  • Enchondroma — the lesion:
    • A benign INTRAMEDULLARY hyaline cartilage lesion.
    • The HAND is the commonest site (proximal phalanx most often), where it is the most frequent bone tumour and often presents with a pathologic fracture.
    • Radiographs: a lucent lesion with punctate "ring and arc" chondroid calcification and endosteal scalloping.
    • In long bones it is usually an incidental finding in the proximal humerus or distal femur.
  • Managing a solitary enchondroma of the hand:
    • Treat a pathologic fracture FIRST — let it heal.
    • Then curette and graft if symptomatic; many need nothing further.
    • Malignant transformation of a SOLITARY HAND enchondroma is VERY RARE.
    • Chondrosarcoma of the hand is rare and, when it occurs, is usually in the setting of Ollier disease.
    • Features that would be alarming elsewhere — expansion, cortical thinning, pathologic fracture — are expected and benign in the hand.
  • Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome:
    • OLLIER DISEASE (enchondromatosis) — multiple, often UNILATERAL and asymmetric enchondromas, causing limb-length discrepancy and angular deformity.
    • MAFFUCCI SYNDROME = enchondromatosis PLUS soft-tissue HAEMANGIOMAS (spindle cell haemangiomas), which may be visible as bluish subcutaneous nodules and phleboliths on radiographs.
    • Maffucci carries the highest risk of malignancy.
  • The genetics — and the counselling answer:
    • Both are due to SOMATIC MOSAIC IDH1/IDH2 mutations.
    • They are therefore NOT INHERITED.
    • The classic genetic counselling answer is that the risk to offspring is NOT increased.
    • The same IDH mutations are found in sporadic conventional chondrosarcoma and are a potential therapeutic target.
  • Malignant transformation risk:
    • Roughly 25–40% in OLLIER disease.
    • Higher in MAFFUCCI — approaching 100% for some malignancy over a lifetime.
    • Maffucci also carries a risk of NON-SKELETAL malignancies — gliomas, visceral and ovarian tumours.
    • Lifelong surveillance is required; new pain or growth after skeletal maturity warrants imaging.
  • What to look for on surveillance imaging:
    • Cortical destruction.
    • Deep endosteal scalloping (more than two-thirds of cortical thickness).
    • A soft-tissue mass.
    • Interval growth.
    • Remember that axial and proximal lesions are far more concerning than hand lesions, and that histology alone cannot make the distinction.

Ewing sarcoma

The small round blue cell tumour of childhood.

4 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2020, 2023

  • Who and where:
    • The second commonest primary bone malignancy of children and adolescents, peak 5–25 years.
    • Strong predilection for Caucasian patients — it is distinctly rare in patients of African and East Asian descent.
    • DIAPHYSIS of long bones and the FLAT bones (pelvis, scapula, ribs).
    • A diaphyseal lesion in a child is Ewing until proven otherwise (osteosarcoma is metaphyseal).
  • Radiographic appearance:
    • Permeative, moth-eaten destruction with a wide zone of transition.
    • "ONION-SKIN" lamellated periosteal reaction (though a Codman triangle and spiculation also occur).
    • A large SOFT-TISSUE MASS, often out of proportion to the bony change — best seen on MRI.
  • The clinical mimic:
    • Ewing sarcoma mimics OSTEOMYELITIS: pain, fever, raised ESR/CRP and leukocytosis.
    • Every suspicious "osteomyelitis" should have tissue sent for HISTOLOGY.
    • Every tumour biopsy should be sent for CULTURE.
    • This reciprocal rule prevents both of the classic errors.
  • Molecular hallmark and immunohistochemistry:
    • t(11;22)(q24;q12) — EWSR1-FLI1 fusion, present in roughly 85%.
    • Variant translocations include EWSR1-ERG (t(21;22)).
    • CD99 (MIC2) POSITIVE on immunohistochemistry.
    • This distinguishes it from LYMPHOMA, which is CD45/LCA positive — the key small-round-blue-cell discriminator.
    • The small-round-blue-cell differential also includes rhabdomyosarcoma (desmin, myogenin), neuroblastoma (NSE) and small-cell carcinoma.
  • Treatment:
    • NEOADJUVANT multi-agent chemotherapy (VDC/IE — vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide alternating with ifosfamide and etoposide).
    • Then LOCAL CONTROL — wide surgical resection where feasible, radiotherapy where resection is not.
    • Then ADJUVANT chemotherapy.
    • Ewing is one of the FEW RADIOSENSITIVE bone sarcomas — but surgery gives better local control when the lesion is resectable, and avoids the secondary malignancy risk of radiotherapy in a child.
    • Chemotherapy transformed survival from under 10% to roughly 70% in localised disease.
  • Prognostic factors:
    • Presence of METASTASES at diagnosis — the worst factor.
    • PELVIC/AXIAL location.
    • Large tumour volume.
    • Poor chemotherapy response (percentage necrosis).
    • Older age and elevated LDH.
    • Localised disease has ~70% survival; metastatic disease ~20–30%. Isolated lung metastases do better than bone or marrow metastases.

Fibrous dysplasia & fibrous lesions

Fibrous dysplasia, non-ossifying fibroma and osteofibrous dysplasia.

3 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2021, 2025

  • Fibrous dysplasia — the lesion:
    • A developmental GNAS1 POST-ZYGOTIC (somatic) mutation — so it is not inherited.
    • Produces a "GROUND-GLASS" lytic lesion with a well-defined sclerotic rim — the "RIND SIGN".
    • Histology: irregular WOVEN bone trabeculae in a fibrous stroma — "alphabet soup" / "Chinese characters".
    • WITHOUT OSTEOBLASTIC RIMMING — the key discriminator from osteofibrous dysplasia (which has rimming) and from low-grade central osteosarcoma (which permeates and is MDM2-positive).
  • The named associations:
    • SHEPHERD'S CROOK deformity of the proximal femur — characteristic of polyostotic disease.
    • McCUNE-ALBRIGHT SYNDROME = polyostotic FD + café-au-lait macules with irregular "COAST OF MAINE" borders + endocrinopathy (precocious puberty).
    • (Contrast the smooth "coast of California" borders of neurofibromatosis.)
    • MAZABRAUD SYNDROME = fibrous dysplasia + intramuscular MYXOMAS.
  • Managing fibrous dysplasia — the fixation principle is the tested point:
    • Observe asymptomatic lesions; bisphosphonates reduce pain (they do not change the lesion).
    • When fixation is needed, use LOAD-SHARING INTRAMEDULLARY devices, NOT plates and screws.
    • The reasons: the abnormal bone will not incorporate graft; cortical/cancellous AUTOGRAFT is resorbed and replaced by dysplastic bone; and plates fail in the soft, deforming bone.
    • Cortical ALLOGRAFT is more durable than cancellous graft.
    • Correct deformity with osteotomy plus IM fixation — a valgus osteotomy with a cephalomedullary device for a shepherd's crook.
  • Non-ossifying fibroma (fibrous cortical defect):
    • An ECCENTRIC, CORTICALLY BASED lucent lesion with a sclerotic scalloped margin, in the metaphysis of a child.
    • A "DON'T TOUCH" lesion that resolves with skeletal maturity — it is present in up to 30% of children.
    • Treat only for a pathologic fracture, or prophylactically if it involves >50% of the bone width or is >33 mm.
    • JAFFE-CAMPANACCI syndrome — multiple NOFs with café-au-lait spots (and sometimes intellectual disability and gonadal anomalies).
  • Osteofibrous dysplasia and adamantinoma — a spectrum:
    • Osteofibrous dysplasia — the ANTERIOR TIBIAL CORTEX ("bowing of the tibia") in the first decade.
    • Histologically it HAS OSTEOBLASTIC RIMMING — the feature that separates it from fibrous dysplasia.
    • It is closely related to ADAMANTINOMA — the two sit on a spectrum, with an intermediate "OFD-like adamantinoma".
    • Biopsy and observation matter, because ADAMANTINOMA is a LOW-GRADE MALIGNANCY requiring WIDE RESECTION, whereas osteofibrous dysplasia is observed until maturity.
    • Adamantinoma has epithelial nests (cytokeratin-positive) within a fibrous stroma, occurs in an older age group, and metastasises to lung in ~15–20%.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2021, 2022

  • What it is:
    • Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) — called eosinophilic granuloma when limited to bone — is a CLONAL proliferation of Langerhans dendritic cells.
    • Most often in the FIRST DECADE.

    • Langerhans cells with grooved "COFFEE-BEAN" nuclei.
    • CD1a and S100 POSITIVE (and CD207/langerin).

    • A background EOSINOPHIL infiltrate.

    • Solitary eosinophilic granuloma — best prognosis.

    • Solitary bone lesions are usually OBSERVED — many heal spontaneously.

Primary and secondary lymphoma of bone — a great mimic.

Two osteoblastic lesions separated largely by size and behaviour.

3 of 10 sittings2019, 2021, 2024S

  • Osteoid osteoma — the lesion:
    • A nidus <1.5–2 cm with surrounding reactive sclerosis.
    • Most often the proximal femur/femoral neck and the tibial diaphysis.
    • Also the POSTERIOR ELEMENTS of the spine — a classic cause of PAINFUL SCOLIOSIS in an adolescent, with the lesion on the CONCAVITY of the curve (a painful, non-structural curve from muscle spasm).

    • NIGHT PAIN RELIEVED BY NSAIDs/aspirin — mediated by prostaglandin E2 produced by the nidus.

    • CT is the best study for the nidus (thin-slice, with the classic lucent nidus and central calcification).

    • MRI can be misleading — the surrounding oedema can look alarming and obscure the nidus.

    • The natural history is SPONTANEOUS RESOLUTION over years, so NSAIDs alone are a legitimate definitive treatment for a patient who tolerates them.

    • Open excision is reserved for lesions adjacent to the SPINAL CORD or SKIN, where RFA thermal injury is a concern (a margin of ~1 cm is needed).
    • Recurrence after RFA is around 5–10% and can be re-ablated.

    • Histologically similar to osteoid osteoma, but >2 cm.
    • More aggressive and expansile, with less reactive sclerosis.
    • Pain is NOT typically relieved by NSAIDs, and there is no strong night-pain pattern.
    • Predilection for the POSTERIOR ELEMENTS of the spine and the sacrum.
    • It does NOT regress spontaneously — so observation is not an option.
  • Treatment of osteoblastoma:
    • Intralesional curettage (extended, with adjuvant) or marginal excision.
    • Recurrence is ~10–20%.
    • The aggressive variant can be confused with OSTEOSARCOMA histologically — review by a bone pathologist is essential before treating it as benign.
    • Pre-operative embolisation may be used for large, vascular spinal lesions.

    • Langerhans cell histiocytosis.
    • Osteomyelitis.
    • Ewing sarcoma.
    • Leukaemia and lymphoma.
    • Neuroblastoma metastasis.
    • And, less likely under 5, osteosarcoma.
  • AGE is the single most useful discriminator:
    • Under 5 — LCH, leukaemia, neuroblastoma metastasis, infection.
    • 5–20 — Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, LCH, osteomyelitis.
    • Over 40 — metastasis, myeloma, lymphoma, chondrosarcoma.
    • Osteosarcoma is RARE under 5 and is usually the "least likely" answer in a toddler.
  • Systemic features overlap — and that overlap has a rule:
    • Fever, night sweats and raised inflammatory markers occur in BOTH Ewing sarcoma AND osteomyelitis.
    • Every "infection" that does not behave typically must be BIOPSIED.
    • Every biopsy must be sent for CULTURE, and every culture specimen sent for HISTOLOGY.
    • This reciprocal rule is the safeguard against both classic errors.
  • Leukaemia — do not miss it:
    • The commonest childhood malignancy.
    • Can present with bone pain, refusal to weight-bear, metaphyseal lucent bands, and disproportionate night pain.
    • The white cell count may be NORMAL or LOW — a normal WBC does not exclude it.
    • Always check the blood film and consider marrow assessment; thrombocytopenia and anaemia are clues.
  • Neuroblastoma:
    • Metastases in a child under 5 produce permeative lytic lesions with periosteal reaction.
    • Periorbital ecchymosis ("raccoon eyes") is the classic associated sign.
    • Check urine catecholamines (VMA/HVA) and obtain abdominal imaging for the primary.
    • Bone marrow involvement is common; MIBG scanning is the staging study.
  • The imaging and biopsy sequence in a child:
    • Plain radiographs FIRST — still the most diagnostic single study for a bone lesion.
    • Then MRI of the WHOLE bone (to define extent and detect skip lesions).
    • Then staging — CT chest, bone scan or PET, and marrow assessment.
    • Then BIOPSY, at the treating centre, planned by the surgeon who will do the definitive resection.

Radiation therapy & radiation-associated fracture

Radiosensitivity of tumours and the behaviour of irradiated bone.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2020, 2024S

  • Radiosensitivity ranking:
    • HIGHLY sensitive — lymphoma, myeloma, Ewing sarcoma, seminoma, small-cell lung.
    • INTERMEDIATE — breast, prostate.
    • Relatively RESISTANT — renal cell, melanoma, thyroid, sarcoma (osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma), GI adenocarcinoma.
    • The "least responsive to radiation" answer is almost always renal cell, melanoma or chondrosarcoma.
    • Stereotactic body radiotherapy has substantially improved control even in the resistant group, but conventional fractionated radiotherapy has not.
  • Radiation-associated (insufficiency) fracture:
    • Occurs in irradiated bone — most classically the FEMORAL NECK after PELVIC radiotherapy, and the pelvis and sacrum.
    • Typically TRANSVERSE.
    • Occurs at 1–5 years or later after treatment.
    • Heals slowly with a high NONUNION rate.
    • The surrounding soft tissue is poor, so wound healing is unreliable too.
    • Distinguish it from a pathologic fracture through recurrent tumour — MRI and, if in doubt, biopsy.
  • Why irradiated bone behaves badly, and what to do about it:
    • Impaired osteoblast function, reduced vascularity and reduced healing capacity.
    • Plan for LOAD-SHARING INTRAMEDULLARY fixation, a VASCULARISED graft, or ENDOPROSTHETIC replacement.
    • Rather than conventional plating and grafting, which fails in this bone.
    • Expect a prolonged union time and counsel accordingly; consider adjuncts (teriparatide has been used off-label).
  • Radiation-induced sarcoma — the Cahan criteria:
    • Arises WITHIN the radiation field.
    • After a latency of at least 3–4 years (usually much longer, 10–20 years).
    • With a DIFFERENT histology from the original tumour.
    • Most are osteosarcoma or UPS, and the prognosis is POOR — worse than for the equivalent de novo sarcoma.
    • This is the reason radiotherapy is AVOIDED in benign disease (for example giant cell tumour) and in children wherever possible.
  • Practical surveillance:
    • Prophylactic pinning of the femoral neck before pelvic radiotherapy is NOT routine.
    • But a low threshold for imaging new hip or groin pain in a previously irradiated patient is essential — MRI, not just a radiograph.
    • Any new mass or new pain in an old radiation field should be imaged and considered for biopsy.

Benign soft-tissue tumours

Recognising the common benign soft-tissue masses and their red flags.

1 of 10 sittings2024F

  • Lipoma — the commonest soft-tissue tumour by far:
    • Soft, mobile, subcutaneous.
    • Follows FAT signal on ALL MRI sequences, with COMPLETE suppression on fat-saturated images.
    • Deep, large (>10 cm), septated or heterogeneous fatty lesions must be treated as ALT/WDL until proven otherwise.
    • Intramuscular lipomas infiltrate and recur if incompletely excised.
  • The two commonest hand and wrist masses:
    • GANGLION CYST — the commonest; fluid-filled, transilluminates, T2 bright and non-enhancing except for a thin rim.
    • GIANT CELL TUMOUR OF TENDON SHEATH (localised TGCT) — the second commonest; a firm, painless nodule, characteristically LOW signal on BOTH T1 and T2 from haemosiderin.
    • That T1/T2-dark combination is a short differential: TGCT/PVNS, desmoid/fibromatosis, and scar.
  • Schwannoma versus neurofibroma — the distinction that changes the operation:
    • A SCHWANNOMA is ECCENTRIC to the nerve and can be SHELLED OUT preserving the nerve.
    • A NEUROFIBROMA is intimately involved with the nerve FASCICLES and cannot be excised without sacrificing the nerve.
    • Both may show a "target sign" (central low, peripheral high T2) and a "split fat sign" (a rim of fat around an intermuscular lesion).
    • NF1 raises concern for MALIGNANT PERIPHERAL NERVE SHEATH TUMOUR — suspect it with rapid growth, new pain, size >5 cm, or a new neurologic deficit; PET is useful, and the lifetime MPNST risk in NF1 is roughly 8–13%.
  • Two more with characteristic imaging:
    • HAEMANGIOMA — serpiginous vessels, PHLEBOLITHS on radiograph, and pain that varies with activity and position (worse with dependency).
    • ELASTOFIBROMA DORSI — periscapular (deep to the inferior scapula), bilateral in a third, with characteristic striated fat interleaved with fibrous tissue; typically in older women, and usually observed.
  • Red flags mandating referral and MRI BEFORE excision:
    • Size >5 cm.
    • Deep to fascia.
    • Rapid growth.
    • Pain.
    • Firmness or fixation to deep structures.
    • Any of these means "plan it as a sarcoma until proven otherwise", because an unplanned excision compromises the definitive operation and increases the extent of resection needed.

Benign surface lesions of bone (Nora lesion/BPOP, periosteal chondroma)

Surface lesions of the small bones — a common source of diagnostic confusion.

1 of 10 sittings2022

  • Nora lesion (bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation, BPOP):
    • A SURFACE lesion of the SMALL BONES of the HANDS and FEET — the proximal phalanx is most typical.
    • Occurs in young adults, often with no clear history of trauma.
    • Radiographically a well-defined OSSIFIED MASS on the cortical surface WITHOUT MEDULLARY CONTINUITY.
    • The absence of medullary continuity is what distinguishes it from an OSTEOCHONDROMA.
  • Imaging and histology:
    • MRI shows a cartilage-capped surface lesion, with a T2-bright cartilage cap arising from the PERIOSTEUM rather than from the medullary cavity.
    • Histology shows the characteristic "BLUE BONE" — basophilic, bizarrely calcified cartilage — with disorganised cartilage, bone and spindle cells.
    • It looks alarming but is benign, and has been mistaken for parosteal osteosarcoma.
  • Behaviour and treatment:
    • High local RECURRENCE rate — up to 50% after excision.
    • Complete excision including the underlying PERIOSTEUM and cortical base is recommended to reduce that.
    • It does NOT metastasise.
  • Differential for a surface lesion of a phalanx:
    • Osteochondroma — medullary continuity.
    • Periosteal chondroma — cortical scalloping with a sclerotic base and chondroid matrix; <3 cm.
    • BPOP (Nora lesion) — as above.
    • Subungual exostosis — distal phalanx, beneath the nail.
    • Florid reactive periostitis — a post-traumatic reactive process on the same spectrum as BPOP.
    • And, rarely, periosteal or parosteal osteosarcoma.
  • The practical warning:
    • Because the histology is worrisome, correlation with the RADIOGRAPHIC appearance is essential.
    • Consult a bone pathologist to avoid over-treatment — patients have had amputations for this benign lesion.
    • The same principle applies to myositis ossificans and to florid reactive periostitis.

Multiple myeloma & plasma cell disease

The commonest primary malignancy of bone.

1 of 10 sittings2022

  • Epidemiology:
    • Multiple myeloma is the COMMONEST PRIMARY MALIGNANT TUMOUR OF BONE in adults.
    • (Metastasis is more common overall but is not a primary bone tumour — the wording matters.)
    • Peak incidence in the 6th–7th decade.
  • Presentation — CRAB:
    • hyperCalcaemia.
    • Renal insufficiency.
    • Anaemia.
    • Bone lesions.
    • Radiographs show multiple PUNCHED-OUT lytic lesions WITHOUT sclerotic margins; the skull, spine, ribs and pelvis are typical.
    • Diffuse osteopenia with vertebral compression fractures is a common and easily missed presentation — myeloma is a differential for "osteoporosis" in a man or a younger woman.
  • The imaging point that is repeatedly tested:
    • Myeloma lesions are frequently COLD on technetium bone scan.
    • The reason: they are purely OSTEOLYTIC with SUPPRESSED osteoblastic activity, and bone scan images osteoblasts.
    • So a SKELETAL SURVEY, WHOLE-BODY LOW-DOSE CT, or whole-body MRI/PET is required — NOT a bone scan.
    • Whole-body low-dose CT has now largely replaced the skeletal survey.
  • Diagnosis:
    • SPEP and UPEP with immunofixation.
    • Serum FREE LIGHT CHAINS and β2-microglobulin (a prognostic marker).
    • Bone marrow biopsy showing >10% clonal plasma cells.
    • Bence-Jones proteinuria (free light chains in urine, missed by a standard dipstick).
    • Solitary plasmacytoma may precede myeloma by years — treat with radiotherapy and follow for progression.
  • Treatment:
    • Primarily MEDICAL: proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulators, anti-CD38 antibodies, and autologous stem cell transplant.
    • Bisphosphonates or denosumab for skeletal-related events (denosumab is preferred in renal impairment).
    • Radiotherapy for painful lesions — myeloma is HIGHLY RADIOSENSITIVE.
    • Surgery is for impending or actual pathologic fracture and for spinal instability; vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty for painful compression fractures.
  • The prognostic point that changes surgical planning:
    • Prognosis has improved markedly with modern therapy.
    • A patient with myeloma and a femoral lesion may have YEARS of life and deserves DURABLE fixation — not a minimal palliative construct.
    • Protect the whole bone, cement the defect, and expect the construct to outlast a long survival.

Myositis ossificans

Heterotopic bone in muscle — and the critical distinction from parosteal osteosarcoma.

1 of 10 sittings2023

  • The clinical picture:
    • Typically follows BLUNT TRAUMA — classically the thigh (quadriceps) or brachialis.
    • Presents as a firm, PAINFUL, ENLARGING mass 2–4 weeks after injury.
    • Sometimes with fever and a raised ALP.
    • It closely mimics a SARCOMA — clinically, radiographically early, and histologically early.
  • The ZONAL PHENOMENON — the diagnostic key:
    • Myositis ossificans matures from the PERIPHERY INWARD.
    • So the mineralisation is MOST DENSE at the PERIPHERY, with a LUCENT IMMATURE CENTRE.
    • There is a LUCENT CLEFT separating it from the underlying bone.
    • PAROSTEAL OSTEOSARCOMA is the OPPOSITE — densest CENTRALLY — and it is CONTINUOUS with/attached to the cortex.
    • CT demonstrates the zonal pattern better than plain films or MRI.
  • The timeline and the imaging strategy:
    • The lesion mineralises on radiographs at ~3–6 weeks and matures over 6–12 months, often shrinking.
    • SERIAL RADIOGRAPHS at 2–3 week intervals are far more useful than an early biopsy.
    • EARLY BIOPSY IS DANGEROUS — the immature proliferative centre looks histologically like a sarcoma, and this error has led to unnecessary amputations.
    • If tissue is unavoidable, sample the periphery and warn the pathologist of the clinical context.
  • Treatment:
    • Expectant — rest, gentle range of motion, and analgesia.
    • Excise ONLY if MATURE — a stable radiographic appearance, a normal ALP, and a quiescent bone scan, usually at ≥6–12 months.
    • AND only if symptomatic from impingement or restricted motion.
    • Early excision leads to recurrence.
    • Prophylaxis (indomethacin or single-dose radiation) may be used after excision in a high-risk patient.
  • The entity not to confuse it with:
    • Myositis ossificans PROGRESSIVA (fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, FOP) is a distinct and devastating genetic disorder.
    • ACVR1 mutation, with CONGENITAL HALLUX VALGUS / a short great toe as the diagnostic clue at birth.
    • BIOPSY AND SURGERY ARE CONTRAINDICATED because they provoke flares and accelerate heterotopic ossification.
    • Even intramuscular injections and dental blocks can trigger a flare.

Pigmented villonodular synovitis / TGCT

Diffuse and localised tenosynovial giant cell tumour.

2 of 10 sittings2018, 2023

  • The name and the two forms:
    • PVNS is now termed TENOSYNOVIAL GIANT CELL TUMOUR (TGCT).
    • LOCALISED — the commonest tumour of the hand and tendon sheath; nodular, with a low recurrence rate.
    • DIFFUSE — intra-articular, classically the KNEE, then hip and ankle, with a HIGH recurrence rate.
  • It is a neoplasm, not an inflammatory process:
    • Driven by a CSF1 gene translocation.
    • This produces a "LANDSCAPE EFFECT" — a minority of neoplastic cells recruiting a large population of non-neoplastic MACROPHAGES that make up the bulk of the lesion.
    • That biology is exactly what the CSF1R inhibitors target.
  • Clinical presentation:
    • A young adult with recurrent ATRAUMATIC HAEMORRHAGIC (brown/serosanguinous) effusions.
    • Swelling and stiffness, with a boggy synovium.
    • Radiographs may show periarticular EROSIONS on BOTH SIDES of the joint with PRESERVED JOINT SPACE and NO OSTEOPENIA.
    • Preserved joint space and normal bone density distinguish it from inflammatory arthritis, which narrows the joint and causes periarticular osteopenia.
  • MRI is diagnostic:
    • Nodular synovial masses.
    • LOW signal on BOTH T1 and T2, from haemosiderin.
    • "BLOOMING" artefact on GRADIENT-ECHO sequences — the classic finding, and the reason to request that sequence when you suspect it.
    • Diffuse disease often extends into the popliteal fossa and posterior compartment — image the whole joint before planning surgery.
  • Treatment:
    • COMPLETE SYNOVECTOMY for diffuse disease — arthroscopic ± open, and often combined ANTERIOR and POSTERIOR in the knee, because posterior disease is what gets left behind.
    • Local excision for nodular (localised) disease.
    • Recurrence is high in diffuse disease — up to 50%.
    • Adjuvant radiosynoviorthesis and CSF1R INHIBITORS (pexidartinib) for refractory or unresectable disease.
    • Malignant transformation is rare. End-stage joint destruction is treated with arthroplasty, with synovectomy at the same time.

Tumour imaging & general principles

Choosing and interpreting imaging in musculoskeletal oncology.

1 of 10 sittings2024F

  • Plain radiographs remain the single most useful study for characterising a bone lesion:
    • ZONE OF TRANSITION — narrow = indolent; wide or permeative = aggressive. This is the most important single feature.
    • PERIOSTEAL REACTION — solid/thick = slow; lamellated ("onion-skin"), spiculated ("sunburst") or a Codman triangle = aggressive.
    • MATRIX — chondroid = rings and arcs, popcorn; osteoid = cloud-like, dense; fibrous = ground-glass.
    • LOCATION within the bone — epiphysis / metaphysis / diaphysis, and central / eccentric / cortical / surface. Location alone narrows the differential enormously.
    • And the patient's AGE, which is the other half of the diagnosis.
  • MRI — what it adds:
    • Local extent, soft-tissue involvement, marrow replacement, neurovascular relationships and SKIP LESIONS.
    • Image the ENTIRE involved bone, including BOTH adjacent joints — this is how skip lesions are found.
    • Contrast distinguishes solid from cystic/necrotic tissue and identifies the best BIOPSY TARGET (biopsy the enhancing solid component, not the necrotic centre).
    • Always obtain the MRI before biopsy — post-biopsy haematoma and oedema make it uninterpretable.
  • CT, bone scan and PET:
    • CT best shows matrix mineralisation, cortical integrity, and the NIDUS of an osteoid osteoma — and is required for staging the chest.
    • BONE SCAN screens the whole skeleton for polyostotic disease and metastases (remember myeloma is cold).
    • PET-CT assesses metabolic activity, response to chemotherapy, and occult metastases, and can guide biopsy to the most active area.
  • A specificity trap:
    • FLUID-FLUID LEVELS on MRI are NOT specific for aneurysmal bone cyst.
    • They occur in telangiectatic osteosarcoma, giant cell tumour, chondroblastoma, simple bone cyst, fibrous dysplasia, and in secondary ABC arising within another lesion.
    • Which is why every "ABC" needs tissue.
  • Lodwick grading and the "don't touch" list:
    • Lodwick IA geographic with a sclerotic rim → IB geographic, well-defined without a rim → IC geographic, ill-defined → II moth-eaten → III permeative. It correlates with aggressiveness.
    • "DON'T TOUCH" lesions: non-ossifying fibroma, bone island, myositis ossificans, avulsive cortical irregularity (distal femoral cortical desmoid), and an osteochondroma with medullary continuity.
    • Recognising these avoids an unnecessary biopsy of a lesion whose only danger is the surgeon.
