Odontoid & upper cervical fractures

The classifications, why type II fails to unite, the elderly treatment debate, and the contraindications to each fixation option.

9 of 10 sittings2017, 2019, 2020, 2021 (x4), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x2), 2024F (x3), 2025

  • Anderson & D'Alonzo classification of odontoid fractures:
    • Type I — an avulsion of the TIP at the alar ligament insertion. Rare; may indicate occipitocervical instability, so always assess the craniocervical junction rather than dismissing it.
    • Type II — through the BASE of the dens at its junction with the body. The commonest and the problem fracture: a watershed vascular zone with a small cross-sectional area of cancellous bone, hence a high nonunion rate.
    • Type III — extending into the cancellous body of C2. Heals well in a collar or halo because of the large cancellous surface area.
    • Grauer's subclassification of type II by fracture-line obliquity guides whether an anterior screw is feasible.
  • Risk factors for nonunion of a type II fracture:
    • Displacement >4–6 mm.
    • Angulation >10°.
    • POSTERIOR displacement (worse than anterior).
    • Age >50, and especially >65.
    • Comminution at the fracture site.
    • Delayed presentation or delayed treatment, and smoking.
    • Nonunion rates in the elderly treated non-operatively approach 30–60%.
  • The elderly type II odontoid fracture — a well-defined evidence area:
    • Non-operative treatment in a hard collar is a legitimate and commonly chosen option, accepting a high rate of stable fibrous nonunion, which is usually asymptomatic and requires no further treatment.
    • Surgery (posterior C1-C2 fusion) gives higher union rates but carries substantial perioperative morbidity in this population; the AOSpine geriatric odontoid studies show better pain and disability scores with surgery at the cost of early complications.
    • A HALO VEST IS A POOR OPTION IN THE ELDERLY — associated with markedly increased mortality, pneumonia, dysphagia, pin complications and poor tolerance. This is the classic "all are reasonable options EXCEPT" answer.
    • Decide on the patient's physiology and function, not on the radiograph alone.
  • Anterior odontoid screw fixation — and its contraindications:
    • Its attraction is that it preserves C1-C2 rotation, which accounts for roughly 50% of cervical rotation — valuable in a younger patient.
    • Contraindicated with an OBLIQUE fracture line running anterosuperior to posteroinferior (i.e. parallel to the intended screw path) — the screw will shear the fracture rather than compress it.
    • Comminution of the dens or the C2 body.
    • Osteoporosis — poor screw purchase.
    • A disrupted transverse atlantal ligament — the C1-C2 complex is then unstable regardless of whether the dens unites.
    • Chronic nonunion (>6 months).
    • A barrel chest, short neck or fixed thoracic kyphosis preventing the necessary caudocranial trajectory.
    • Pathologic bone.
  • Posterior C1-C2 fixation:
    • Magerl transarticular screws with a posterior wiring and graft construct — biomechanically excellent, the historical gold standard.
    • Harms/Goel C1 lateral mass–C2 pedicle screw-rod constructs — more forgiving, and they allow reduction intra-operatively.
    • The principal contraindication to transarticular screws is an aberrant/high-riding VERTEBRAL ARTERY in the C2 isthmus, present in roughly 20% of patients. A CT angiogram or careful CT assessment is mandatory before planning. This is the answer to "what is a contraindication to C1-C2 transarticular screw fixation".
    • Also contraindicated: an irreducible C1-C2 subluxation (the transarticular screw cannot reduce the joint — it must be reduced first) and severe osteoporosis.
    • Expect to lose roughly 50% of cervical rotation after C1-C2 fusion; counsel the patient about driving and shoulder-checking.
  • Structures at risk with upper cervical screws:
    • Ventral (anterior) penetration of a C1 LATERAL MASS screw endangers the INTERNAL CAROTID ARTERY, which lies immediately anterior to the C1 lateral mass — along with the hypoglossal nerve. A repeated question.
    • The C2 nerve root and its venous plexus cross the C1-C2 joint and are the usual source of bleeding and of post-operative occipital neuralgia.
    • C2 pedicle/pars screws endanger the vertebral artery medially and inferiorly.
  • Atlas (C1 / Jefferson) fractures:
    • Stability depends entirely on the TRANSVERSE ATLANTAL LIGAMENT, not on the number of arch fractures.
    • Rule of Spence — combined lateral mass overhang >6.9 mm on the open-mouth odontoid view implies transverse ligament rupture and instability.
    • Atlanto-dens interval >3 mm in adults (>5 mm in children) also indicates transverse ligament insufficiency.
    • Dickman type II — a BONY avulsion of the ligament from the lateral mass — can heal in a halo or collar.
    • Dickman type I — a MID-SUBSTANCE ligamentous rupture — will not heal and requires C1-C2 fusion.
    • MRI is the study that distinguishes the two.
  • Hangman's fracture (traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis) — Levine & Edwards:
    • Type I — <3 mm translation, no angulation. Stable; treat in a collar.
    • Type II — >3 mm translation with significant angulation, from a flexion-distraction component. Reduce (with careful traction in extension) and treat in a halo or collar; surgery if reduction fails or is not maintained.
    • Type IIA — severe ANGULATION with minimal translation. TRACTION IS CONTRAINDICATED — it distracts the disc and worsens the deformity. Treat with compression and extension in a halo.
    • Type III — with bilateral facet dislocation at C2-C3. Unstable; requires open reduction and fusion (the facets cannot be reduced closed).
    • Most hangman's fractures heal non-operatively — the pars fractures are through cancellous bone with a wide surface area, and the canal is capacious at C2.
  • Paediatric differences:
    • In children under ~7, odontoid fractures occur through the DENS SYNCHONDROSIS (the neurocentral synchondrosis, which fuses at 5–7 years) and heal readily with closed reduction — usually in extension — and halo or Minerva immobilisation.
    • A displaced type II odontoid fracture in an adolescent who has been non-compliant with a collar and shows displacement and nonunion at 12 weeks needs surgical stabilisation — posterior C1-C2 fusion.
    • Remember the normal paediatric variants that mimic injury: pseudosubluxation of C2 on C3 (up to 4–5 mm, with an intact Swischuk spinolaminar line), incomplete ossification, and an increased ADI up to 5 mm.

Spinal cord injury: Classification (ASIA) & incomplete syndromes

Scoring the examination correctly, the incomplete syndromes and their prognosis, and the two management questions that keep recurring.

10 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018 (x2), 2019 (x4), 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023 (x3), 2024S (x2), 2024F (x3), 2025 (x2)

  • ASIA Impairment Scale — learn the exact wording:
    • A — COMPLETE. No motor or sensory function preserved in the sacral segments S4-S5.
    • B — SENSORY incomplete. Sensory but not motor function below the neurologic level, including S4-S5.
    • C — MOTOR incomplete, with MORE than half of the key muscles below the neurologic level graded LESS than 3.
    • D — MOTOR incomplete, with at least half of the key muscles below the level graded 3 or MORE.
    • E — NORMAL (in a patient with prior deficits that have resolved).
  • Sacral sparing is the defining feature of an INCOMPLETE injury:
    • Voluntary anal contraction (motor), perianal S4-S5 light touch or pinprick (sensory), or deep anal pressure.
    • The rectal examination is mandatory and is exactly what separates ASIA A from ASIA B.
    • Anatomically, the sacral fibres are the most peripheral in the corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts, so they are the last to be lost in a central lesion.
  • Motor scoring — the arithmetic behind the "score of 50" question:
    • 10 key muscles on each side — 5 upper limb (C5 elbow flexors, C6 wrist extensors, C7 elbow extensors, C8 finger flexors, T1 finger abductors) and 5 lower limb (L2 hip flexors, L3 knee extensors, L4 ankle dorsiflexors, L5 long toe extensors, S1 ankle plantarflexors).
    • Each graded 0–5, giving a maximum total motor score of 100 (50 upper limb + 50 lower limb).
    • A motor score of EXACTLY 50 = full grade-5 power in all upper limb myotomes and NO power in the lower limbs — the complete C8/T1-level tetraplegic.
    • To score GREATER than 50, the patient must have some preserved LOWER LIMB motor function in addition to full upper limb power.
  • Zone of partial preservation (ZPP):
    • Applies ONLY to ASIA A (complete) injuries.
    • It describes the dermatomes and myotomes CAUDAL to the neurologic level that retain SOME innervation.
    • It is recorded as the most caudal segment with any sensory or motor function on each side.
    • If there is sacral sparing, the injury is incomplete and the term does not apply.
  • Spinal shock versus neurogenic shock — do not conflate them:
    • SPINAL shock is a neurologic phenomenon: transient areflexia and flaccidity. The injury cannot be accurately classified until it resolves. Return of the bulbocavernosus reflex (typically 24–48 hours) marks the end of spinal shock.
    • NEUROGENIC shock is a haemodynamic phenomenon: HYPOTENSION WITH BRADYCARDIA from loss of sympathetic outflow (injuries T6 and above), with warm, well-perfused peripheries.
    • Neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of EXCLUSION after haemorrhage is ruled out.
    • A patient with a complete cervical cord injury and a closed femur fracture who is hypotensive with a HEART RATE of 110 after 2 L of crystalloid has HAEMORRHAGIC shock — the tachycardia is the discriminator.
  • Incomplete syndromes ranked by prognosis, best to worst:
    • Brown-Séquard > Central cord > Posterior cord > Anterior cord > Conus/Cauda equina.
    • Brown-Séquard has the BEST prognosis — roughly 75–90% ambulate.
    • Anterior cord syndrome has the WORST — roughly 10–20% recovery.
  • CENTRAL CORD SYNDROME — the commonest incomplete syndrome:
    • Classic setting: an elderly patient with cervical spondylosis who falls and sustains a HYPEREXTENSION injury, often with NO fracture.
    • UPPER extremity weakness GREATER than lower extremity weakness, worst in the HANDS, with variable sensory loss, sacral sparing and bladder dysfunction.
    • The anatomic basis is the somatotopic arrangement of the lateral corticospinal tract, with cervical/upper limb fibres most MEDIAL.
    • Most recover enough to ambulate, but HAND function often remains impaired.
    • Recovery order: lower extremities → bladder → proximal upper extremity → hand intrinsics (last and least).
    • Management has shifted: rather than universal observation, early (<24 h) decompression is favoured where there is persistent compression and a plateau or deterioration in the examination.
  • BROWN-SÉQUARD (cord hemisection) — typically a penetrating injury:
    • IPSILATERAL motor weakness below the level (lateral corticospinal tract, which has already decussated in the medulla).
    • IPSILATERAL loss of proprioception and vibration (dorsal columns, which decussate in the medulla).
    • CONTRALATERAL loss of pain and temperature, beginning 1–2 levels BELOW the lesion (spinothalamic tract, which decussates within 1–2 segments of entry in the anterior white commissure).
    • Best prognosis of the incomplete syndromes.
  • The other syndromes:
    • ANTERIOR cord — loss of motor function and pain/temperature with PRESERVED proprioception, vibration and deep touch (dorsal columns spared). From anterior spinal artery injury or a retropulsed fragment. Worst prognosis.
    • POSTERIOR cord — rare; loss of proprioception and vibration with preserved motor and pain/temperature; a wide-based ataxic gait.
    • CONUS MEDULLARIS (T12–L2 bony level) — a MIXED upper and lower motor neuron picture, symmetric, with EARLY and severe bowel/bladder dysfunction and saddle anaesthesia, and relatively less radicular pain.
    • CAUDA EQUINA — a pure LOWER motor neuron lesion, ASYMMETRIC, with severe radicular pain, later bowel/bladder involvement, and areflexia. It is a peripheral nerve injury and has better recovery potential.
  • Methylprednisolone in acute SCI:
    • High-dose steroid (the NASCIS II/III protocols) is NO LONGER RECOMMENDED as standard of care.
    • The evidence of benefit rests on post-hoc subgroup analysis, and the trials demonstrated increased pneumonia, sepsis, GI bleeding and wound complications.
    • Current AANS/CNS guidelines state steroids are not recommended; some guidance describes a 24-hour infusion started within 8 hours as an option with the harms explicitly discussed.
    • Steroids are contraindicated in PENETRATING spinal cord injury.
  • What DOES matter in acute management:
    • Maintain a mean arterial pressure of 85–90 mmHg for the first 7 days to optimise cord perfusion — this usually requires ICU-level vasopressor support.
    • Early (<24 hour) surgical decompression improves neurologic recovery — the STASCIS study (Fehlings, Canadian-led) showed roughly a two-fold odds of ≥2-grade AIS improvement at 6 months.
    • Avoid hypoxia and hypotension in the field; immobilise, but remove the patient from a spine board early to prevent pressure injury.
    • Anticipate autonomic dysreflexia in injuries at or above T6 once spinal shock resolves — a noxious stimulus below the level (usually bladder or bowel) causes headache, severe hypertension and reflex bradycardia. Treat by sitting the patient up and removing the stimulus first.

Lumbar disc herniation & sciatica

The nomenclature, which root a herniation actually compresses, the natural history, and when surgery genuinely changes the outcome.

7 of 10 sittings2019 (x2), 2020, 2021 (x3), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • Nomenclature — essential and frequently tested (Fardon/NASS lexicon):
    • BULGE — a generalised, circumferential extension of the disc margin beyond the endplate involving >25% (90–360°) of the circumference, WITHOUT focal displacement of nucleus pulposus. "A generalised prominence of the annulus without focal nucleus displacement" is the definition.
    • PROTRUSION — a focal herniation (<25% of the circumference) where the BASE is WIDER than the dome.
    • EXTRUSION — the DOME is WIDER than the base; it has herniated through the annulus.
    • SEQUESTRATION — a free fragment with no continuity with the parent disc.
    • "Contained" versus "uncontained" refers to whether the posterior longitudinal ligament is intact.
  • Which root is compressed — the single most tested concept:
    • A PARAMEDIAN/POSTEROLATERAL herniation (the commonest, because the PLL is weakest there) compresses the TRAVERSING (descending) root.
    • So an L4-5 PARAMEDIAN herniation compresses the L5 root.
    • A FAR LATERAL / FORAMINAL herniation compresses the EXITING root.
    • So an L4-5 FAR LATERAL herniation compresses the L4 root — producing quadriceps weakness, a diminished patellar reflex and anteromedial thigh/leg pain.
    • A massive central herniation compresses everything below it → cauda equina syndrome.
  • Root-by-root findings:
    • L4 — anteromedial thigh and leg pain; weak quadriceps and tibialis anterior; diminished patellar reflex; positive femoral stretch test.
    • L5 — lateral leg and dorsum of the foot; weak EHL and tibialis anterior (foot drop) and weak hip abduction (gluteus medius); no reliable reflex.
    • S1 — posterior leg and lateral foot; weak plantarflexion; diminished Achilles reflex.
    • L3 — anterior thigh, weak quadriceps and hip flexion, patellar reflex.
    • Foot drop is L5 until proven otherwise, most commonly from a paramedian L4-5 herniation. The peripheral differential is a common peroneal palsy at the fibular neck (which spares hip abduction and inversion).
  • Examination tests:
    • Straight leg raise tensions the lower lumbar roots (L5, S1); positive between 30–70° with reproduction of radicular (not back) pain. Sensitive, not specific.
    • Crossed straight leg raise — specific, not sensitive; it indicates a large central or axillary herniation.
    • Femoral nerve stretch (reverse SLR) tensions the upper lumbar roots (L2–L4).
    • A patient with BOTH a positive SLR and a positive femoral stretch test has involvement of both upper and lower lumbar roots, suggesting an upper lumbar (L3-4) or a large L4-5 far-lateral lesion.
  • Natural history — the key counselling point:
    • The majority of radicular symptoms improve substantially within 6–12 weeks with non-operative care.
    • Large EXTRUDED and SEQUESTERED herniations resorb MORE readily than small contained protrusions — the exposed nucleus provokes an inflammatory, neovascular, macrophage-mediated resorption. Counter-intuitive and frequently tested.
    • Radicular pain is generated by both mechanical compression and chemical inflammation (phospholipase A2, TNF-α) from the nucleus.
  • SPORT — what it actually showed:
    • Weinstein, JAMA 2006: discectomy versus non-operative care for lumbar disc herniation.
    • AS-TREATED analyses showed surgery produced greater and faster improvement in pain and function.
    • INTENTION-TO-TREAT analyses showed no statistically significant difference, because of substantial crossover in both directions.
    • By 2–4 years both groups improved substantially.
    • The honest summary: surgery produces FASTER relief; the long-term difference narrows.
  • Surgical indications:
    • Cauda equina syndrome — an emergency.
    • A progressive or severe motor deficit.
    • Persistent, disabling radicular pain that has failed 6–12 weeks of appropriate non-operative treatment, with a CONCORDANT MRI finding.
    • A patient with 8 months of chronic, debilitating sciatica who has failed conservative treatment is an appropriate candidate — waiting longer does not improve the outcome, and prolonged symptom duration (>6–12 months) is associated with a WORSE surgical result.
    • An isolated, painless, longstanding foot drop is unlikely to recover with surgery; a new or progressive one should be decompressed.
    • Back pain alone is not an indication for discectomy.
  • Cauda equina syndrome:
    • Saddle anaesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction, bilateral leg pain and weakness, and decreased anal tone.
    • Urinary RETENTION with overflow incontinence is the most consistent finding — and a post-void residual is the single most useful bedside test. Complete retention (CES-R) has a worse prognosis than incomplete (CES-I).
    • Urgent MRI and decompression — outcomes are better with earlier surgery (traditionally within 48 hours, and sooner is better).
    • Document the rectal examination and the post-void residual, and document the time.
  • Non-operative and interventional options:
    • Epidural steroid injection provides short-term (weeks to a few months) relief of radicular pain but does not alter the long-term course or the eventual need for surgery.
    • NSAIDs, activity modification (not bed rest) and physiotherapy are first-line; oral steroids give a small functional benefit without pain benefit.
    • Gabapentinoids have not been shown to help acute sciatica.
    • A transforaminal injection can also serve as a diagnostic block when the imaging shows multilevel pathology.
  • Complications and revision:
    • Recurrent herniation occurs in roughly 5–10%; risk rises with a large annular defect and with obesity and smoking.
    • Recurrent herniation ENHANCES only peripherally on gadolinium MRI; post-operative SCAR enhances DIFFUSELY — the key distinction on a post-operative study.
    • Discectomy for a recurrence has similar outcomes to primary discectomy; fusion is not routinely required unless there is instability or a second recurrence.
    • Incidental durotomy is the commonest intra-operative complication — repair it, and consider a period of flat bed rest.

Anterior cervical discectomy & fusion / disc arthroplasty

The approach anatomy and its named nerve injuries, what happens as levels are added, and who should and should not get an arthroplasty.

7 of 10 sittings2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S (x2), 2025

  • The Smith-Robinson interval:
    • A transverse skin incision, then the interval MEDIAL to the sternocleidomastoid and the CAROTID SHEATH, and LATERAL to the strap muscles, trachea and oesophagus.
    • So the carotid sheath (carotid artery, internal jugular vein, vagus nerve) is retracted LATERALLY and stays OUTSIDE the dissection — the answer to "is the approach inside or outside the carotid sheath".
    • The omohyoid crosses at roughly C6 and may be divided.
    • Confirm the level radiographically before discectomy — wrong-level surgery is a real risk in the cervical spine.
  • Structures at risk — the named injuries:
    • Recurrent laryngeal nerve — hoarseness. The LEFT RLN has a longer, more predictable course in the tracheo-oesophageal groove (it loops under the aortic arch), whereas the right loops under the subclavian and is more variable — hence a left-sided approach is often preferred at lower levels. Retractor pressure and endotracheal cuff pressure both contribute.
    • Superior laryngeal nerve (external branch) — at risk at the C3-4 level; it supplies cricothyroid, so injury causes loss of high-pitch phonation and vocal fatigue — the "professional singer can no longer hit high notes" presentation.
    • Hypoglossal nerve — at C2-3; tongue deviation toward the side of injury.
    • Sympathetic chain, on the anterolateral surface of longus colli — HORNER SYNDROME (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis). Stay medial and subperiosteal on longus colli; the chain converges medially at lower levels.
    • Oesophagus — perforation is uncommon but devastating (mediastinitis); suspect it with fever, dysphagia, crepitus or a delayed wound issue.
    • Vertebral artery — lateral to the uncovertebral joints; stay within the uncinate processes.
    • Thoracic duct — on the LEFT at the cervicothoracic junction.
  • Dysphagia:
    • The commonest post-operative complaint after ACDF — up to 50% early, with most resolving by 6–12 months.
    • Related to retraction time, the number of levels, plate profile and prevertebral soft-tissue swelling, plus older age and female sex.
    • Mitigation: periodic retractor release, a low-profile or zero-profile implant, and steroid use in longer multilevel cases.
  • Multi-level versus single-level ACDF — the essential comparison:
    • Pseudarthrosis rises substantially with each added level: single ~5–10%; two-level ~15–20%; three-level ~25–30%+.
    • Dysphagia, swallowing complications and airway compromise all increase.
    • So fusion rates FALL and complication rates RISE with each added level.
    • Three-level and above is where a posterior approach, a combined approach, or a corpectomy is considered — a single-level corpectomy has a higher fusion rate than a two-level discectomy, but a multilevel corpectomy has a higher graft-dislodgement risk than multilevel discectomies.
    • Smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor for cervical pseudarthrosis.
  • When an anterior-only construct is NOT enough:
    • An ACDF provides anterior column support and a tension band only if the posterior elements can resist tension and the vertebral body can hold the graft.
    • A LAMINAR or FACET fracture at the level to be fused is the classic relative contraindication — an anterior-only construct will fail, and a posterior or combined approach is needed.
    • Also: a comminuted or teardrop vertebral BODY fracture that cannot support the graft, significant posterior ligamentous complex disruption with facet fractures, an irreducible dislocation, and poor bone quality.
  • Cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) — the evidence:
    • Multiple FDA IDE trials and their long-term follow-ups show CDA is at least equivalent, and in several trials superior, to ACDF for 1- and 2-level degenerative disease at 7–10 years.
    • Lower rates of subsequent surgery at the index and adjacent levels.
    • The mechanism claimed is reduced adjacent-segment loading — though how much adjacent-segment degeneration is natural history versus fusion-induced remains debated (Hilibrand's ~3%/year, 25% at 10 years figure comes from fusion cohorts).
    • It is not an operation for axial neck pain.
  • Contraindications to cervical disc replacement — the high-yield list:
    • FACET ARTHROPATHY at the index level — the single most cited contraindication, because the arthroplasty preserves motion through a degenerate, painful facet.
    • Significant segmental kyphosis or instability.
    • Osteoporosis.
    • Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament.
    • Ankylosing spondylitis or another inflammatory arthropathy.
    • Severe spondylosis with loss of disc height or autofusion (there is no motion to preserve).
    • Active infection, and known metal allergy.
    • Severe AXIAL NECK PAIN as the primary complaint.
    • Heterotopic ossification around the implant is the commonest late radiographic finding and can silently ankylose the segment.
  • Lumbar total disc arthroplasty follows the same logic:
    • The best predictor of a good outcome is a young patient with single-level discogenic pain, PRESERVED FACET JOINTS, no instability or spondylolisthesis, no significant osteoporosis, and a normal BMI.
    • Facet arthropathy is the principal contraindication and the strongest predictor of a poor outcome.
    • The anterior retroperitoneal approach carries its own risks: vascular injury (the left iliac vein at L4-5) and retrograde ejaculation from superior hypogastric plexus injury — discuss this with any male patient.
    • Revision of a lumbar disc arthroplasty is formidable, which is a major practical argument against it.

Cervical myelopathy: Diagnosis & surgical decision-making

Recognising it, the upper motor neuron signs (and the impostors), and choosing anterior versus posterior from alignment and the number of levels.

7 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024S, 2024F (x3), 2025

  • Presentation:
    • Degenerative cervical myelopathy is the commonest cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults over 55.
    • Gait disturbance and loss of balance — often the earliest symptom, and the one patients under-report.
    • Hand clumsiness and loss of fine motor control — difficulty with buttons, coins and handwriting.
    • Diffuse, non-dermatomal numbness of the hands.
    • Bowel and bladder dysfunction — LATE.
    • Neck pain is variable and often ABSENT — its absence does not exclude myelopathy.
    • The presentation of neck pain with hand weakness and poor balance in a patient over 60 is cervical myelopathy until proven otherwise; the next step is MRI of the cervical spine to define the level, degree of compression and cord signal change.
  • Upper motor neuron signs:
    • Hyperreflexia and clonus.
    • Babinski (extensor plantar) response.
    • Hoffmann sign — flicking the middle fingernail produces thumb/index flexion.
    • Finger escape (Wartenberg) sign — the small finger drifts into abduction when the hand is held extended.
    • Inverted radial reflex — tapping the brachioradialis produces finger flexion.
    • Lhermitte sign — an electric shock down the spine on neck flexion.
    • A broad-based, unsteady gait; a positive Romberg; a slow 30-metre walk test.
  • The impostors — which findings are NOT upper motor neuron signs:
    • The SPURLING test is a test for RADICULOPATHY, not myelopathy — a positive Spurling is the classic "which is NOT an upper motor neuron sign of myelopathy" answer.
    • Atrophy and fasciculation are LOWER motor neuron findings.
    • Absent reflexes at the level of the compression (a lower motor neuron finding at that root) can coexist with hyperreflexia below it — a mixed picture.
    • The differential includes ALS (no sensory findings, prominent fasciculation), multiple sclerosis, B12 deficiency/subacute combined degeneration, normal-pressure hydrocephalus and peripheral neuropathy.
  • Natural history — why this drives the decision to operate:
    • Myelopathy is generally progressive in a stepwise fashion, with periods of stability punctuated by deterioration.
    • Neurologic function that is lost is not reliably regained — the goal of surgery is to HALT PROGRESSION, with variable recovery.
    • Therefore surgery is recommended for MODERATE-TO-SEVERE or PROGRESSIVE myelopathy rather than watching.
    • Mild, non-progressive myelopathy may be observed with close follow-up and a clear plan to operate on deterioration (the AO Spine guideline offers surgery or supervised rehabilitation for mild disease).
    • A patient with cord compression on MRI but NO myelopathic signs or symptoms is not an operative candidate — though the risk of developing myelopathy is real, especially with cord signal change or a congenitally narrow canal.
  • Grading severity — mJOA:
    • Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association score: MILD ≥15, MODERATE 12–14, SEVERE <12 (out of 18).
    • It is the framework used by the AO Spine guidelines and by most modern trials.
    • Nurick grade is the older, gait-based scale (0–5).
    • Predictors of a worse surgical outcome: longer duration of symptoms, older age, more severe baseline mJOA, and T2 cord signal change with T1 hypointensity (the latter indicating established myelomalacia).
  • Choosing the approach:
    • ANTERIOR (ACDF or corpectomy) is favoured for: 1–2 level disease; kyphotic or straight alignment; and anterior/ventral compression (a disc-osteophyte complex or focal OPLL).
    • POSTERIOR (laminoplasty, or laminectomy and fusion) is favoured for: multilevel (≥3 levels) disease with PRESERVED LORDOSIS; congenital stenosis; and posterior compression (buckled ligamentum flavum).
    • The mechanism matters: a posterior decompression works by letting the cord DRIFT BACKWARDS, which cannot happen in a kyphotic spine — the cord stays draped over the anterior pathology.
    • The advantage of the anterior approach that the posterior does not have is the ability to DIRECTLY remove ventral compressive pathology AND to restore/correct cervical lordosis — the answer to "what does the anterior approach offer that the posterior does not".
    • Posterior approaches avoid dysphagia and the anterior neurovascular structures but cause more axial neck pain and posterior muscle morbidity.
  • OPLL — a specific and repeatedly tested scenario:
    • OPLL with myelopathy and PRESERVED LORDOSIS is best treated POSTERIORLY — laminoplasty, or laminectomy and fusion.
    • Two reasons: an anterior approach through extensive OPLL carries a high risk of dural tear and CSF leak (the ossified ligament is frequently adherent to, or has replaced, the dura), and the cord can drift posteriorly when lordosis is preserved.
    • A multilevel ACDF is the WRONG answer for multilevel OPLL with preserved lordosis.
    • The K-line (a line from the midpoint of the canal at C2 to that at C7) formalises this: if the OPLL does not cross the K-line (K-line positive), a posterior approach will work; if it crosses it (K-line negative), posterior drift is insufficient and an anterior or combined approach is needed.
    • Anterior "floating" of the OPLL mass, rather than resection, is the technique used to reduce dural tear risk when anterior surgery is unavoidable.
  • C5 palsy — the classic post-operative complication:
    • The commonest specific neurologic complication after laminoplasty or posterior laminectomy and fusion, occurring in roughly 5–10%.
    • Presents with deltoid and biceps weakness with lateral shoulder numbness, typically 2–5 days post-operatively — i.e. delayed, after an initially normal examination.
    • The mechanism is tethering/traction of the C5 root as the cord drifts posteriorly — the C5 root is short, exits most horizontally, and sits at the apex of the lordosis.
    • Most recover over weeks to months with observation and therapy; imaging is done to exclude a compressive cause (haematoma, malpositioned implant).
    • This is the answer both to "the commonest nerve root injury with laminoplasty" and to the post-operative deltoid weakness scenario after a posterior C2-T1 decompression and fusion.
    • Prophylactic foraminotomy at C4-5 reduces the incidence in some series.

Spine infection: Osteomyelitis, discitis & epidural abscess

The imaging pattern that separates infection from tumour and from TB, the organisms by host, and when antibiotics alone are not enough.

7 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • How adult vertebral osteomyelitis develops:
    • It is typically HAEMATOGENOUS, seeding the ANTERIOR vertebral body adjacent to the ENDPLATE via the segmental arteries (the metaphyseal equivalent, with sluggish end-arterial flow).
    • It then spreads ACROSS the disc to the adjacent vertebra.
    • Hence the classic MRI pattern: TWO ADJACENT vertebral bodies with DESTRUCTION OF THE INTERVENING DISC and endplate erosion, with high T2/STIR signal and gadolinium enhancement.
    • Disc space involvement is the hallmark of PYOGENIC infection.
    • In children the disc is still vascularised, so primary discitis occurs; in adults the disc is avascular and is involved secondarily.
  • Organisms by host:
    • Staphylococcus aureus is the commonest overall (~50%), followed by streptococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci and gram-negatives.
    • Intravenous drug user → S. aureus (still commonest) AND Pseudomonas aeruginosa — the classic pairing.
    • Sickle cell disease → Salmonella.
    • Genitourinary source or instrumentation → E. coli and gram-negatives.
    • Post-procedural → S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci.
    • Immunocompromised/endemic exposure → TB, Brucella, fungi.
  • Distinguishing infection from TUMOUR — a repeated exam task:
    • INFECTION CROSSES the disc space and DESTROYS it, and typically involves TWO CONTIGUOUS vertebrae with an adjacent paraspinal or epidural collection.
    • METASTASIS SPARES the disc, often involves the PEDICLE (the "winking owl" sign — an absent pedicle on the AP radiograph), and can be multilevel and NON-contiguous.
    • So a septic patient with a urinary source, a lytic vertebral body lesion, and an MRI showing disc involvement and endplate destruction has INFECTION; the same lesion sparing the disc with pedicle involvement is metastasis.
    • Where doubt remains, biopsy — and send tissue for both microbiology and histology, always.
  • Spinal tuberculosis (Pott disease) — the important exception:
    • TB characteristically SPARES THE DISC, because the organism lacks proteolytic enzymes.
    • It spreads SUBLIGAMENTOUSLY beneath the anterior longitudinal ligament to involve MULTIPLE CONTIGUOUS levels, and can produce skip lesions.
    • It produces large, well-defined paravertebral and psoas abscesses with calcification and thin, smooth rim enhancement (a pyogenic abscess has a thick, irregular rim).
    • It causes ANTERIOR vertebral body destruction with progressive KYPHOSIS — the gibbus deformity.
    • It follows an indolent course with constitutional symptoms; the thoracic spine is the commonest site.
    • The presentation of HIV with a vertebral body lesion, a large prevertebral abscess and PRESERVED DISC HEIGHT is TB.
  • TB treatment is primarily MEDICAL:
    • Prolonged multi-drug antitubercular chemotherapy — typically 4 drugs initially (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol), for a total of 9–18 months.
    • Surgery is indicated for: neurologic deficit, significant kyphotic deformity or instability, a large abscess requiring drainage, failure of medical therapy, and the need for diagnostic tissue.
    • Chemotherapy is not optional and is the mainstay — "surgery alone" and "short-course antibiotics" are the standard wrong answers.
    • Neurologic recovery in Pott paraplegia is often excellent with chemotherapy alone if the cord compression is from abscess rather than bone.
  • Diagnosis:
    • ESR and CRP are elevated in the great majority and are the most useful screening and monitoring tests; CRP falls first and tracks the response.
    • The white cell count is frequently normal and is unreliable.
    • Blood cultures are positive in ~50–70% — obtain them before antibiotics. Two positive blood cultures with typical MRI findings can obviate biopsy.
    • MRI with gadolinium is the imaging study of choice (sensitivity and specificity >90%); image the whole spine because skip lesions occur.
    • CT-guided biopsy when blood cultures are negative — and hold antibiotics before biopsy unless the patient is septic. Repeat the biopsy if the first is negative (yield only ~50–60%).
    • Look for the source: echocardiography for endocarditis, urine, skin, dental.
  • Treatment of pyogenic spinal infection:
    • Organism-directed IV antibiotics for ~6 weeks (followed by oral therapy), with bracing, in a neurologically intact patient with a stable spine.
    • Total duration is typically 6–12 weeks; the OVIVA trial supports early oral switch for bone and joint infection in appropriately selected patients.
    • Surgical indications: neurologic deficit; epidural abscess with compression; instability or progressive deformity; failure of medical management (persistent sepsis or rising CRP); intractable pain; and the need for tissue diagnosis.
    • Instrumentation in the presence of active infection is acceptable — modern series show it does not preclude clearance, and stability aids eradication; titanium is preferred.
  • Spinal epidural abscess:
    • The classic (but frequently incomplete) triad of back pain, fever and neurologic deficit, progressing through radicular pain → weakness → paralysis.
    • It is a neurosurgical emergency — urgent MRI of the WHOLE spine (skip lesions are common) and decompression with antibiotics.
    • Delay to decompression after the onset of a deficit is the strongest predictor of a poor outcome — deficits present for more than 24–36 hours rarely recover fully.
    • Non-operative management with antibiotics alone may be considered ONLY in a neurologically intact patient with a small abscess, a known organism, and the ability to monitor closely — and with a low threshold to operate on any deterioration.
    • Risk factors: diabetes, IV drug use, immunosuppression, dialysis, and recent spinal instrumentation or injection.

Adult spinal deformity & sagittal balance

The spinopelvic parameters, the realignment targets, how much correction each osteotomy buys, and the two commonest failure modes.

6 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2021 (x2), 2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • The parameters — which are fixed and which are positional:
    • PELVIC INCIDENCE (PI) is a FIXED, MORPHOLOGIC parameter — the angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and a line from that midpoint to the femoral head centre. It does NOT change with position (or with surgery).
    • PI = PT + SS (pelvic tilt + sacral slope).
    • PT and SS are POSITIONAL — the pelvis compensates for sagittal imbalance by RETROVERTING: increasing PT and decreasing SS.
    • Sagittal vertical axis (SVA) — the horizontal offset of a plumb line from the C7 centroid to the posterosuperior corner of S1.
    • Other compensatory mechanisms, in order of recruitment: pelvic retroversion → knee flexion → ankle dorsiflexion → thoracic hypokyphosis.
  • Realignment targets (Schwab/SRS classification):
    • PI − LL (lumbar lordosis mismatch) within ±10°.
    • Pelvic tilt <20°.
    • SVA <5 cm.
    • These correlate strongly with health-related quality of life; PI−LL mismatch and PT are the parameters most associated with disability (ODI).
    • Age-adjusted targets are now used — older patients tolerate (and may do better with) less aggressive correction.
  • Working an example:
    • PI 50, LL 25, SVA 10 cm → PI−LL mismatch of 25° with severe positive sagittal imbalance.
    • This requires approximately 25–30° of lordosis restoration to reach target.
    • Roughly 1 cm of SVA correction is gained per 1° of lordosis added at the lumbar apex — a useful planning heuristic.
  • Osteotomy selection — the correction achieved per level is the exam content:
    • Smith-Petersen osteotomy (SPO / Ponte) — resection of the POSTERIOR column (facets and posterior elements), hinging on the DISC. Gives ~10° per level, and requires a MOBILE anterior disc.
    • SPOs are additive — four levels (say L2–L5) gives roughly 40°, with less morbidity per level than a single big osteotomy.
    • Pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) — a THREE-COLUMN wedge resection through the pedicle and vertebral body. Gives ~30° at a single level, but with substantially greater blood loss, longer operative time, and higher neurologic and pseudarthrosis risk. It is the option when the anterior column is fused or ankylosed.
    • Vertebral column resection (VCR) — the most powerful and the most morbid; for rigid angular and coronal deformity.
    • An anterior column realignment (ACR) with anterior longitudinal ligament release is the modern lower-morbidity alternative for restoring lordosis.
  • The "least invasive option" question:
    • For a patient with a previous L3-S1 fusion, PI 50, LL 25 and SVA 10 cm, the least invasive way to restore sagittal balance is MULTI-LEVEL SMITH-PETERSEN (PONTE) OSTEOTOMIES through the MOBILE segments above the fusion.
    • A PSO achieves the same correction at one level but with much greater morbidity.
    • A laminectomy achieves NO correction, and a vertebrectomy is far more invasive.
    • The general rule: use the least powerful osteotomy that achieves the target, and prefer several small ones over one large one where the discs are mobile.
  • Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and failure (PJF):
    • The commonest complication of long fusions — PJK is radiographic (>10° increase in the angle between the UIV and UIV+2), PJF is symptomatic and structural (fracture, implant pull-out, myelopathy) and requires revision.
    • Risk factors: OVER-correction, a large PI−LL correction, fusion to the sacrum/pelvis, older age, osteoporosis, disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex at the upper instrumented vertebra, high BMI, and a UIV at the thoracolumbar junction.
    • Mitigation: preserve the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments at the UIV; avoid over-correction in the elderly (age-adjusted targets); use transitional/tapered rods, hooks at the UIV, or cement augmentation; and choose a UIV above T5 or well below the thoracolumbar junction.
    • Under-correction is not protective either — residual imbalance is itself a cause of revision.
  • Pelvic fixation:
    • Fusion to the sacrum requires pelvic fixation to protect the S1 screws and reduce lumbosacral pseudarthrosis.
    • S2-alar-iliac (S2AI) screws versus conventional iliac screws: S2AI screws start more MEDIALLY and CAUDALLY, so they sit in line with the S1 screws in a lower-profile construct, need less soft-tissue dissection, and have a lower rate of symptomatic implant prominence and reoperation for hardware removal.
    • Biomechanically they are comparable or better, with lower rates of screw loosening; they do cross the sacroiliac joint.
  • Flatback syndrome — the historical lesson:
    • After Harrington rod instrumentation, which DISTRACTED the lumbar spine and abolished lordosis.
    • Presents decades later with back pain, an inability to stand upright, and fatigue from constant hip and knee flexion.
    • Treatment is a lordosing osteotomy.
    • The principle it teaches — never distract the lumbar spine; compress to create lordosis — underlies all modern deformity instrumentation.

Lumbar spinal stenosis & degenerative spondylolisthesis

Distinguishing neurogenic from vascular claudication, the benign natural history, and the one question that keeps coming back — when to add a fusion.

4 of 10 sittings2017, 2020, 2021, 2024S (x2)

  • Neurogenic claudication — the hallmark:
    • Bilateral buttock and leg pain, heaviness and paraesthesia, usually poorly localised.
    • Worse with STANDING and WALKING (extension) — extension buckles the ligamentum flavum and narrows the canal by up to 40%.
    • Relieved by SITTING or FORWARD FLEXION — the "shopping cart sign"; patients can cycle further than they can walk.
    • The examination is characteristically normal at rest.
  • Distinguishing it from VASCULAR claudication:
    • Vascular claudication is relieved by standing STILL, not by flexion.
    • It has a reproducible, fixed walking distance, and is WORSE walking UPHILL (neurogenic is better uphill, because the patient leans forward).
    • Absent pulses, hair loss, cool skin, and a reduced ankle-brachial index.
    • The FLEXION relief is the discriminator; the two can and do coexist in the same elderly patient.
  • Natural history is relatively benign:
    • Most patients remain stable or improve; rapid deterioration is uncommon.
    • There is no urgency to operate in the absence of a progressive deficit or cauda equina.
    • This supports an initial trial of non-operative care — physiotherapy, activity modification, analgesia and, for radicular pain, an epidural injection.
    • It is also the honest counselling point for a patient who is not sure about surgery: waiting does not usually cause harm.
  • SPORT for stenosis and degenerative spondylolisthesis:
    • Weinstein, NEJM 2008: surgical decompression produced significantly greater improvement in pain and function than non-operative care, maintained at 2 and 4 years.
    • The benefit was particularly evident in degenerative spondylolisthesis.
    • Symptom duration matters — better results in those operated on before very prolonged symptoms.
    • As with the disc-herniation arm, crossover blunted the intention-to-treat result.
  • When to ADD A FUSION to a decompression — the highest-yield question:
    • DEGENERATIVE SPONDYLOLISTHESIS with stenosis — the accepted answer to "a common reason to perform a fusion when doing a lumbar stenosis decompression".
    • Radiographic instability on dynamic films — >4–5 mm translation or >10° angulation.
    • Degenerative scoliosis with coronal or sagittal imbalance.
    • Recurrent stenosis at a previously decompressed level.
    • Iatrogenic instability created by the decompression — removal of >50% of the facets bilaterally, complete removal of one facet, or resection of the pars.
  • What is NOT an indication for fusion:
    • Central canal stenosis alone, without instability or spondylolisthesis — decompression alone is appropriate, and this is the usual distractor.
    • A mild degenerative lumbar curve (~20°) without imbalance does not by itself mandate fusion.
    • Age alone, multi-level disease alone, or the presence of back pain alone.
  • The fusion evidence is genuinely nuanced:
    • Ghogawala (NEJM 2016) found fusion added benefit (SF-36 physical component) for degenerative spondylolisthesis, at the cost of more blood loss, longer stay and higher cost.
    • Försth (Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study, NEJM 2016) found NO added benefit of fusion over decompression alone, including in the spondylolisthesis subgroup, at greater cost and morbidity.
    • The modern position: decompression alone is appropriate for many stable, low-grade degenerative spondylolistheses, with fusion reserved for demonstrable instability, deformity, or predominant back pain.
    • Facet orientation, disc height and the presence of an effusion on MRI are being used to identify the "unstable" subgroup.
  • The asymptomatic or back-pain-only degenerative spondylolisthesis:
    • For a 20% L4-5 slip with back pain and no leg pain: the natural history is favourable, progression of the slip is uncommon and does not correlate with symptoms.
    • Treatment is physiotherapy, core strengthening, activity modification and analgesia.
    • Bracing is not effective, and prophylactic fusion is not indicated.
    • The slip magnitude does not predict symptoms or outcome.
  • Why degenerative spondylolisthesis behaves as it does:
    • Characteristically at L4-5, in women over 50, associated with sagittally oriented facets and with generalised ligamentous laxity (and diabetes).
    • Critically, the PARS IS INTACT — distinguishing it from isthmic spondylolisthesis.
    • Because the pars is intact, the whole vertebral ring translates forward, so the canal is narrowed by the posterior arch as well as by the disc → the stenosis is severe. In isthmic spondylolisthesis the posterior elements are left behind, so the canal is actually widened and the compression is foraminal.
    • The slip rarely progresses beyond grade I–II.

Occipitocervical dissociation & craniocervical injury

The measurements that make the diagnosis, the one thing you must never do, and the associated condyle and vertebral artery injuries.

4 of 10 sittings2019, 2021, 2024F, 2025

  • What it is:
    • Atlanto-occipital / occipitocervical dissociation (AOD) is a highly unstable LIGAMENTOUS injury of the craniocervical junction, from a high-energy hyperextension/distraction mechanism (pedestrian struck, high-speed collision).
    • The injured structures are the tectorial membrane, the alar ligaments and the apical ligament — the bone is often normal.
    • Proportionally more common in CHILDREN — horizontally oriented occipital condyles, ligamentous laxity, and a relatively large head.
    • It is often immediately fatal; survivors frequently have lower cranial nerve deficits, brainstem injury and vascular injury.
  • Why it is missed:
    • Missed on plain radiographs in a large proportion of cases — the lateral cervical film is unreliable at the craniocervical junction.
    • Maintain a high index of suspicion in any high-energy injury, particularly with an unexplained neurologic deficit, a cranial nerve palsy, or a large prevertebral soft-tissue shadow.
    • Prevertebral soft tissue should be <6 mm at C2 and <22 mm at C6 ("6 at 2, 22 at 6").
    • A patient who survives to hospital may be neurologically intact — do not be reassured by a normal examination.
  • The measurements:
    • Basion-Dens Interval (BDI) >12 mm and Basion-Axial Interval (BAI) >12 mm — Harris' "rule of 12s".
    • Powers ratio >1.0 indicates ANTERIOR dissociation — the ratio of (basion to posterior arch of C1) divided by (opisthion to anterior arch of C1). A ratio <0.9 suggests posterior dissociation.
    • Condylar-C1 interval (CCI) >2 mm on CT is the MOST SENSITIVE measurement and is the modern standard.
    • So an ANTERIOR occipitocervical dissociation is identified by a Powers ratio >1.0 (the basion translating anteriorly relative to the posterior arch of C1) together with an increased BDI.
    • MRI demonstrates the ligamentous injury (tectorial membrane, alar ligaments) and is the confirmatory study, as well as showing cord and epidural haematoma.
  • Management — and the absolute rule:
    • AOD is grossly unstable and requires OCCIPITOCERVICAL FUSION.
    • TRACTION IS ABSOLUTELY CONTRAINDICATED — it will distract the already-dissociated joint and can cause catastrophic neurologic deterioration or death. This is the single most important point.
    • Immobilise in a rigid collar or halo with meticulous attention to alignment, avoiding ANY distraction, while awaiting definitive fixation.
    • Beware of positioning: turning, transferring and intubating the patient are the moments of danger; use in-line stabilisation and consider awake fibreoptic intubation.
  • Work-up before surgery in the polytrauma patient:
    • MRI of the craniocervical junction and cervical spine — to define the ligamentous injury and cord status.
    • CT angiogram to assess the vertebral arteries.
    • Maintain rigid immobilisation without traction throughout.
    • Early stabilisation improves outcomes and facilitates nursing, mobilisation and pulmonary care in a polytrauma patient.
  • Occipital condyle fractures (Anderson & Montesano):
    • Type I — impaction (comminuted, from axial load). Stable.
    • Type II — extension of a basilar skull fracture through the condyle. Usually stable (the alar ligament and tectorial membrane are intact).
    • Type III — ALAR LIGAMENT AVULSION of the condyle. Potentially UNSTABLE — assess specifically for AOD.
    • Most are treated in a collar; a type III with craniocervical instability requires occipitocervical fusion.
    • Suspect lower cranial nerve (IX–XII) injury — the hypoglossal canal runs through the condyle (Collet-Sicard syndrome).
  • Vertebral artery injury:
    • Common with craniocervical and cervical fracture patterns. Screen with CT angiography for: fractures through the TRANSVERSE FORAMEN; subluxation or dislocation; fractures of C1–C3; and any unexplained neurologic deficit (the modified Denver criteria).
    • Most are ASYMPTOMATIC — the contralateral vertebral artery compensates through the circle of Willis.
    • Antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelet or anticoagulation) is the mainstay, balanced against bleeding risk from other injuries; endovascular treatment is reserved for pseudoaneurysm or ongoing embolisation.
    • Bilateral injury, or occlusion of a DOMINANT vessel, risks posterior circulation (Wallenberg/cerebellar) stroke — this is the scenario with real morbidity.
    • Practical anatomy: the vertebral artery enters the transverse foramen at C6 and runs within the foramina to C1, then crosses the posterior arch of C1 in the groove for the vertebral artery — the reason posterior C1 dissection must stay within 1.5 cm of the midline.

Rheumatoid & inflammatory cervical spine

The three patterns of rheumatoid instability, the measurement that predicts cord injury, and why the ankylosed spine is a different and more dangerous problem.

4 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2022, 2023

  • Three patterns of rheumatoid cervical involvement, in order of frequency:
    • Atlantoaxial instability (~65%) — pannus destroying the transverse ligament. Usually anterior; posterior and lateral (rotatory) subluxation also occur.
    • Basilar invagination / cranial settling (~20%) — the odontoid migrating SUPERIORLY through the foramen magnum as the C1-C2 lateral masses erode. The most dangerous, because it compresses the brainstem and can cause sudden death.
    • Subaxial subluxation (~15%) — often multilevel, producing a "staircase" deformity; more common after a C1-C2 fusion transfers load caudally.
  • The measurements:
    • Anterior atlanto-dens interval (AADI) >3 mm is abnormal; >10 mm suggests complete ligamentous disruption (transverse plus alar).
    • The POSTERIOR atlanto-dens interval (PADI) — the space available for the cord — is the MORE IMPORTANT prognostic measure.
    • PADI <14 mm predicts neurologic compromise and is a strong indication for surgery; PADI >14 mm essentially excludes cord compression.
    • For basilar invagination use the Ranawat (centre of C2 pedicle to the McGregor line, <13 mm abnormal), Redlund-Johnell and Clark station measurements — the eroded odontoid tip makes the classic McRae/Chamberlain lines unreliable.
    • Subaxial subluxation is significant at >4 mm translation or >20% of the vertebral body width, or a subaxial canal diameter <14 mm.
  • The practical safety point — the highest-value item in this card:
    • Rheumatoid patients are frequently ASYMPTOMATIC despite severe instability — their limited mobility masks myelopathic symptoms, and pain is attributed to their peripheral joints.
    • Flexion-extension cervical radiographs are MANDATORY before any general anaesthetic in a patient with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis.
    • Catastrophic cord injury has occurred during intubation and positioning.
    • The same applies before elective arthroplasty, hand surgery or any prone procedure — a point examiners return to repeatedly.
    • If instability is present, plan awake fibreoptic intubation with in-line stabilisation.
  • Indications for surgery and what to do:
    • Neurologic deficit or myelopathy; PADI <14 mm; basilar invagination; subaxial subluxation with canal compromise; and intractable pain.
    • C1-C2 fusion for atlantoaxial instability.
    • Occipitocervical fusion for basilar invagination (± transoral or posterior odontoid resection if there is irreducible ventral compression).
    • Pannus REGRESSES after successful fusion — resection of the pannus is usually unnecessary once motion is eliminated. A frequently tested point.
    • Nonunion rates are higher than in non-rheumatoid patients (poor bone, steroids, DMARDs) — plan generous bone graft and rigid fixation, and manage biologics per the ACR/AAHKS schedule.
  • The ANKYLOSED spine (ankylosing spondylitis) — a fundamentally different problem:
    • AS transforms the spine into a long, rigid, OSTEOPOROTIC lever arm.
    • Even trivial trauma produces a highly unstable, THREE-COLUMN fracture — usually a transverse fracture through the ankylosed disc space, most commonly at the CERVICOTHORACIC junction.
    • Neurologic injury and EPIDURAL HAEMATOMA are common, and deterioration can be delayed.
    • Delayed diagnosis is frequent, because the fracture is hard to see on plain films of a fused, deformed spine — CT of the WHOLE spine is mandatory in any AS patient with new pain after trauma, and MRI if there is a deficit or if CT is negative but pain persists.
    • Roughly 10% have a second, non-contiguous fracture.
  • Managing the AS spine fracture:
    • Immobilise in the patient's PRE-INJURY DEFORMED POSITION.
    • NEVER force the neck into a "neutral" collar or lay the patient flat — this will displace the fracture and injure the cord. Support the head on pillows in its habitual position.
    • Fixation requires LONG, MULTILEVEL POSTERIOR instrumentation — typically three levels above and three below — because short constructs fail in the long lever arm.
    • Traction is dangerous for the same reason as in AOD.
    • Halo immobilisation alone has a high failure rate; surgery is usually preferred.
  • Cervicothoracic osteotomy for AS kyphosis:
    • Usually a C7 pedicle subtraction osteotomy performed AWAKE (C7 is below the vertebral artery's entry at C6 and has a capacious canal).
    • The well-described complication is BILATERAL C8 NERVE ROOT PALSY — numbness and pain along the ulnar border of both hands with intrinsic weakness — from foraminal narrowing and root traction during the correction.
    • It is the classic post-operative finding after cervicothoracic osteotomy and usually improves with time.
    • Wide foraminotomy at C7-T1 during the osteotomy reduces the risk.
  • DISH — the same fracture behaviour without the inflammation:
    • Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis = flowing anterior ossification of ≥4 contiguous vertebrae with PRESERVED DISC SPACES and NO sacroiliac joint involvement (the features that distinguish it from AS).
    • It is typically right-sided in the thoracic spine (the aorta prevents left-sided ossification).
    • It produces the same brittle, long-lever-arm fracture behaviour and requires the same caution — whole-spine CT, immobilisation in the pre-injury position, and long posterior constructs.
    • DISH is associated with diabetes and the metabolic syndrome, and with dysphagia from large anterior osteophytes.

Subaxial cervical trauma: Facet injuries & flexion-distraction

Percentage translation and what it predicts, the closed reduction and pre-reduction MRI debate, and when an anterior construct is not enough.

5 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023

  • Unilateral versus bilateral facet dislocation:
    • UNILATERAL facet dislocation produces approximately 25% anterolisthesis of the vertebral body.
    • It typically causes a nerve ROOT deficit at the level of the dislocated facet (the exiting root is compressed in the foramen), commonly with preserved spinal cord function.
    • Mechanism is flexion with rotation; the patient's head is often held rotated away.
    • BILATERAL facet dislocation produces ~50% anterolisthesis and is far more likely to cause a SPINAL CORD injury.
    • Look for the "bow-tie" or "double diamond" sign on the lateral radiograph (the rotated facets), and widened interspinous distance.
  • Applying the root anatomy:
    • In the cervical spine, the root exits ABOVE its correspondingly numbered pedicle down to C7, so a C6-C7 dislocation compresses the C7 root.
    • A C6-C7 unilateral locked facet dislocation causes a C7 radiculopathy — weak triceps and wrist flexors/finger extensors, a diminished triceps reflex, and numbness of the middle finger.
    • In a C6-C7 dislocation, the LOWEST nerve root that may retain function is C6 — the root exiting above the level of injury. Roots at and below the level are at risk.
  • Closed reduction — and the pre-reduction MRI debate:
    • Closed reduction with awake traction is SAFE and effective in the AWAKE, ALERT, COOPERATIVE, EXAMINABLE patient — because serial neurologic examination during progressive weight application detects deterioration immediately.
    • Technique: Gardner-Wells tongs, start with ~10–15 lb and add in increments with a radiograph and neurologic examination after each; up to a third of body weight may be needed. Sedation and muscle relaxation help.
    • A PRE-REDUCTION MRI IS MANDATORY in the OBTUNDED or NON-EXAMINABLE patient, and in any patient who cannot be examined during reduction — to exclude a disc herniation that could be driven into the canal by reduction.
    • In the awake, examinable patient, prompt reduction should NOT be delayed for MRI — time to decompression matters more than the small risk.
    • Stop if the patient deteriorates, if there is over-distraction (>1–2 mm of disc space widening), or if reduction is not achieved — then go to open reduction.
  • Definitive treatment:
    • For the best long-term functional outcome after a reduced unilateral facet fracture-dislocation, the answer is SURGICAL STABILISATION AND FUSION.
    • Most commonly a single-level ACDF — it allows discectomy, restores alignment and lordosis, and has a high fusion rate; or a posterior fusion.
    • Halo immobilisation alone has an unacceptably high rate of late instability, loss of reduction and recurrent deformity (up to 20–60% failure) and is not the best option for long-term function.
    • The SLIC score (Subaxial Injury Classification) formalises the decision: morphology (compression 1, burst 2, distraction 3, rotation/translation 4) + discoligamentous complex (intact 0, indeterminate 1, disrupted 2) + neurologic status (intact 0, root 1, complete 2, incomplete 3, +1 for ongoing compression with a deficit). ≤3 non-operative, 4 discretionary, ≥5 operative.
  • When an ACDF is inadequate for a subaxial injury:
    • Significant POSTERIOR ELEMENT fracture (lamina or facet fractures) — the classic relative contraindication, because an anterior-only construct has nothing to work against.
    • Severe posterior ligamentous disruption needing a posterior tension band.
    • An irreducible dislocation — the facets must be reduced from behind.
    • Multi-level injury, and poor bone quality or an ankylosed spine.
    • These favour a posterior or combined (360°) approach.
  • The traumatic central cord scenario:
    • An older patient with a hyperextension injury, profound hand weakness with relatively preserved legs, NO fracture on CT, and MRI showing multilevel cord compression is a central cord syndrome on a spondylotic background.
    • If the deficit is not improving, the treatment is surgical DECOMPRESSION.
    • Where the compression is multilevel with straight or lordotic alignment and too many levels for an ACDF, the answer is posterior laminectomy and instrumented fusion (or laminoplasty), within 24 hours.
    • Early (<24 h) decompression improves outcomes (STASCIS).
  • Clearing the cervical spine — the rules that surround these injuries:
    • Canadian C-Spine Rule (Stiell, Canadian, high sensitivity): imaging is required if there is a high-risk factor (age ≥65, a dangerous mechanism, or paraesthesia in the extremities); if there is no low-risk factor allowing safe assessment of range of motion (simple rear-end collision, sitting in the department, ambulatory at any time, delayed onset of neck pain, absence of midline tenderness); or if the patient cannot actively rotate the neck 45° each way.
    • CT is the imaging study of choice in adults; plain radiographs are inadequate.
    • In the obtunded patient with a normal high-quality CT, most modern guidance permits collar removal, with MRI reserved for a persistent deficit or a suspicion of ligamentous injury.

Thoracolumbar burst & flexion-distraction (chance) fractures

The posterior ligamentous complex as the decision point, the Canadian no-brace evidence, and the abdominal injury you must not miss.

4 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2022, 2024F

  • The single most important determination in a burst fracture:
    • Thoracolumbar burst fractures involve the anterior and middle columns with retropulsion (failure of the posterior vertebral body wall is what distinguishes a burst from a compression fracture).
    • The integrity of the POSTERIOR LIGAMENTOUS COMPLEX (PLC) determines management — supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum and facet capsules.
    • Best assessed on MRI (STIR sequence), which shows oedema and discontinuity.
    • Radiographic surrogates: widening of the interspinous distance, facet diastasis, >50% vertebral body height loss, and >30° kyphosis.
    • A palpable interspinous gap on examination is a useful bedside sign.
  • TLICS (Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity score):
    • Morphology — compression 1, burst 2, translation/rotation 3, distraction 4.
    • PLC integrity — intact 0, suspected/indeterminate 2, disrupted 3.
    • Neurologic status — intact 0, nerve root 2, complete cord 2, incomplete cord or cauda equina 3.
    • Score ≤3 → non-operative; 4 → surgeon's discretion; ≥5 → operative.
    • Note that an INCOMPLETE neurologic injury scores HIGHER than a COMPLETE one, because it has more to gain from decompression.
    • The newer AO Spine TL classification (A compression, B tension band, C translation, with N neurologic and M modifiers) is increasingly used and maps onto the same logic.
  • Brace versus no brace — the Canadian evidence:
    • Bailey, Dvorak et al. (Spine J 2014) showed that treatment WITHOUT a brace — early mobilisation with a "no-brace" protocol — is EQUIVALENT to TLSO bracing in a stable burst fracture without neurologic deficit.
    • Equivalent pain, function and radiographic outcomes at 3 months and at 1–2 years, with lower cost, greater comfort and earlier mobilisation.
    • The eligibility criteria matter: the trial included stable burst fractures with kyphosis <35°, NO neurologic deficit, and an INTACT PLC.
    • Bracing remains reasonable for comfort but is not required for healing or for maintaining alignment.
    • This is a repeated and important Canadian trial — know it by name.
  • Epidemiology and the canal-compromise fallacy:
    • Most occur at the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2) — the transition from the rigid, rib-stabilised thoracic spine to the mobile lumbar spine, with a change in facet orientation from coronal to sagittal.
    • They result from axial compression, and are more common in young men from high-energy mechanisms (falls from height, motor vehicle collisions).
    • The degree of canal compromise correlates POORLY with neurologic deficit — the injury happens at the moment of maximum displacement, not at the residual position.
    • Canal remodelling occurs over time (roughly 50% of the retropulsed fragment resorbs over 1–2 years), even without surgery.
    • Canal compromise alone is NOT an indication for surgery.
  • Flexion-distraction (Chance) fracture — the mechanism and the association:
    • A TENSION failure of the posterior and middle columns, with the axis of rotation anterior to the anterior longitudinal ligament.
    • Classically from a LAP-BELT (seat-belt) mechanism in a motor vehicle collision.
    • The hallmark association is INTRA-ABDOMINAL INJURY — bowel, mesenteric and pancreatic — in up to 50%, together with a transverse abdominal wall ecchymosis (the "seat-belt sign").
    • ALWAYS image the abdomen and involve general surgery — the abdominal injury is what kills these patients, and hollow viscus injury can be occult on the initial CT.
  • Treating a Chance injury:
    • BONY Chance fractures (entirely through bone, with an intact disc and ligaments) can heal in hyperextension casting or bracing — bone heals, ligament does not.
    • Ligamentous or bony-ligamentous flexion-distraction injuries (with PLC disruption on MRI) will NOT heal with bracing and require POSTERIOR instrumented fusion.
    • Short-segment posterior COMPRESSION instrumentation is highly effective, because the injury is a pure tension failure and compression closes it — one level above and one below usually suffices.
    • The presentation of an L1 flexion-distraction injury with T2 signal change in the interspinous ligaments is therefore an operative injury.
  • Paediatric Chance fractures:
    • Typically lap-belt injuries in children too large for a car seat but too small for the shoulder belt to fit correctly (roughly 4–9 years).
    • The same intra-abdominal injury association applies — and is if anything higher.
    • Remodelling potential is greater, so more are managed in a hyperextension cast or brace; the same PLC principle still decides.
    • Look for the seat-belt sign on the abdominal wall and a neurologic examination that may be difficult to obtain.
  • Surgical options for an operative burst fracture:
    • Posterior short-segment pedicle screw fixation ± instrumentation of the fractured level, is the workhorse; it corrects kyphosis by ligamentotaxis and indirect reduction.
    • Anterior corpectomy and strut grafting for significant anterior column deficiency or where direct decompression of a retropulsed fragment is required with an incomplete deficit.
    • Percutaneous instrumentation is increasingly used for the neurologically intact patient who needs stabilisation without decompression.
    • In the ankylosed spine (AS/DISH) the same fracture behaves entirely differently — long constructs, and never a brace.

Cervical radiculopathy & degenerative spondylosis

What actually compresses the nerve root in the degenerative cervical spine.

2 of 10 sittings2020, 2022

  • What actually compresses the root in degenerative spondylosis:
    • The UNCOVERTEBRAL JOINT (joint of Luschka) osteophyte is the commonest cause of nerve ROOT compression — it projects into the anterior aspect of the neuroforamen.
    • Facet (zygapophyseal) joint hypertrophy compresses the root POSTERIORLY.
    • A soft disc herniation is more common in younger patients.
    • The uncovertebral osteophyte is the classic answer for the older patient with degenerative spondylosis.
    • Loss of disc height also narrows the foramen vertically, and the uncinate processes are what stop a lateral disc from reaching the vertebral artery.
  • Anatomy of the cervical root exit — a common trap:
    • Cervical roots exit ABOVE the correspondingly numbered pedicle, down to C7 (which exits above the T1 pedicle); C8 exits BELOW the C7 pedicle, and from T1 down the roots exit below their numbered pedicle.
    • So a C5-6 disc or foraminal lesion compresses the C6 root.
    • And a C6-7 lesion compresses the C7 root.
    • This differs from the lumbar spine, where a paramedian herniation takes the traversing (lower-numbered-level) root.
  • Root patterns:
    • C5 — deltoid and shoulder abduction; lateral shoulder sensation; biceps reflex.
    • C6 — biceps and wrist extension; thumb and index sensation; brachioradialis reflex.
    • C7 — triceps, wrist flexion and finger extension; middle finger sensation; triceps reflex.
    • C8 — finger flexion; ring and small finger sensation; no reliable reflex.
    • T1 — hand intrinsics; medial forearm sensation.
  • Examination tests:
    • Spurling's test — extension, ipsilateral lateral bending and axial compression, reproducing radicular arm pain.
    • It has HIGH specificity but LOW sensitivity — a positive test is meaningful; a negative test does not exclude radiculopathy.
    • The shoulder abduction relief sign — placing the hand on the head relieves the pain — is also specific for cervical radiculopathy.
    • Distinguishing cervical from shoulder pain: radicular pain travels below the elbow with dermatomal numbness and is reproduced by neck position, whereas shoulder pain is localised to the deltoid region, is reproduced by arm position and impingement testing, and is relieved by a subacromial block.
  • Natural history is favourable — the essential counselling point:
    • The majority of cervical radiculopathy resolves or substantially improves within weeks to a few months.
    • Non-operative management: physiotherapy, NSAIDs, activity modification, traction, and selective nerve root or epidural steroid injection.
    • A soft cervical collar for a short period may help acutely but should not be prolonged.
  • Surgical indications and options:
    • Persistent, disabling radicular pain failing 6–12 weeks of appropriate non-operative treatment with a concordant imaging finding.
    • A progressive motor deficit.
    • Any evidence of myelopathy — this changes the calculus entirely.
    • ACDF — also corrects alignment and treats central pathology; the most versatile option.
    • Cervical disc arthroplasty — for a young patient with a soft disc and preserved facets.
    • Posterior foraminotomy — motion-preserving, ideal for a lateral soft disc or foraminal osteophyte without kyphosis or central pathology; avoid it if the spine is kyphotic or the pathology is central.
  • The mimics — always consider:
    • Shoulder pathology (rotator cuff, adhesive capsulitis) and peripheral nerve entrapment (carpal or cubital tunnel) — and remember the "double crush" phenomenon.

Isthmic spondylolisthesis & pars defects

The lytic pars defect in adults.

4 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x3), 2020, 2022

  • The anatomy and why it matters:
    • Isthmic spondylolisthesis results from a defect (lysis) in the PARS INTERARTICULARIS, almost always at L5-S1.
    • The vertebral body, pedicles and transverse processes translate forward while the POSTERIOR ARCH (lamina and spinous process) REMAINS BEHIND.
    • This is the key anatomic difference from DEGENERATIVE spondylolisthesis, where the pars is intact and the whole ring translates.
    • Because the posterior arch is left behind, the canal is effectively decompressed and CENTRAL stenosis is uncommon.
  • The consequence is FORAMINAL, not central, stenosis:
    • The exiting root is compressed between the downward-migrating pedicle, the fibrocartilaginous pars mass, and the disc.
    • A lytic L5-S1 spondylolisthesis therefore produces an L5 radiculopathy — dorsum of the foot and great toe, weak EHL and dorsiflexion.
    • The typical patient has back pain with bilateral posterior thigh pain, worse with EXTENSION, plus an L5 radicular pattern below the knee.
    • Hamstring tightness and a shortened stride ("pelvic waddle") are characteristic in the high-grade adolescent slip.
  • Classification and prognosis:
    • Wiltse classification (I dysplastic, II isthmic, III degenerative, IV traumatic, V pathologic, VI post-surgical) and Meyerding grading (I <25%, II 25–50%, III 50–75%, IV 75–100%, V spondyloptosis).
    • The SLIP ANGLE (lumbosacral kyphosis) is a better predictor of progression and clinical outcome than the percentage slip.
    • A dome-shaped/rounded sacrum, a trapezoidal L5, and a high pelvic incidence also predict progression.
  • Adult isthmic spondylolisthesis behaves differently from the adolescent form:
    • It is usually a long-standing childhood defect that becomes symptomatic in adulthood as the disc degenerates.
    • Progression of the slip in adulthood is uncommon.
    • Disc degeneration at the slip level is the usual source of new symptoms, not further translation.
    • Reassurance about progression is therefore appropriate in an adult.
  • Management:
    • Non-operative first — physiotherapy, core and hamstring stretching, activity modification and analgesia — with good results in most.
    • Surgery for persistent, disabling symptoms: decompression — specifically FORAMINAL decompression, since the pathology is foraminal — combined with FUSION (posterolateral or interbody).
    • Decompression ALONE in isthmic spondylolisthesis risks destabilising the segment and increasing the slip, because the pars is already deficient — so fusion is generally added in the adult.
    • Interbody support (TLIF/PLIF/ALIF) restores foraminal height directly and improves fusion rates at L5-S1.
  • Reduction versus in-situ fusion in high-grade slips — a genuine controversy:
    • Reduction improves sagittal alignment and lumbosacral kyphosis but carries a significant risk of L5 NERVE ROOT injury (up to 20–30%) — the L5 root is stretched over the sacral dome.
    • In-situ fusion has lower neurologic risk but a higher pseudarthrosis rate and no deformity correction.
    • Partial reduction of the SLIP ANGLE with interbody support is the common compromise — correcting the kyphosis matters more than correcting the translation.
    • Intra-operative neuromonitoring is mandatory during any reduction.
  • Direct pars repair:
    • Reserved for the YOUNG patient with a symptomatic pars defect, MINIMAL OR NO slip, a HEALTHY disc, and a defect ABOVE L5.
    • Techniques: Buck screw, Scott wiring, or a hook-screw (Morscher) construct.
    • Its attraction is that it preserves the motion segment.
    • Adding posterior instrumentation to a pars repair improves fusion rates and provides immediate stability, but sacrifices motion at the segment — the trade-off being tested.

Vertebral artery injury

Screening, natural history and management of blunt vertebral artery injury.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2022, 2023

  • How common, and why most are silent:
    • Blunt vertebral artery injury (VAI) occurs in up to 20–30% of cervical spine fractures with specific patterns — far more common than historically appreciated.
    • The majority are ASYMPTOMATIC, because the contralateral vertebral artery and the circle of Willis provide collateral flow.
    • That is the single most important fact — and the reason screening, not symptoms, finds them.
    • When they do declare, it is usually as a delayed posterior circulation stroke days later, which is precisely what screening aims to prevent.
  • Screening criteria (Denver / modified Denver) — the patterns that mandate CT angiography:
    • Any fracture extending into the TRANSVERSE FORAMEN — the classic and strongest indication.
    • Subluxation or dislocation of the cervical spine.
    • Fractures of C1–C3.
    • Any cervical fracture with a neurologic deficit unexplained by the imaging.
    • Le Fort II/III facial fractures.
    • Basilar skull fracture involving the carotid canal.
    • Severe traumatic brain injury with GCS <6.
    • A cervical seat-belt sign or a hanging mechanism.
  • Imaging:
    • CT angiography is the screening study of choice, with sensitivity and specificity now approaching conventional angiography on modern multidetector scanners.
    • Catheter angiography remains the gold standard and is used when intervention is planned.
    • MR angiography is an alternative but is less sensitive for low-grade intimal injury.
  • Biffl grading:
    • I — luminal irregularity or dissection with <25% narrowing.
    • II — dissection with an intimal flap or >25% narrowing.
    • III — pseudoaneurysm.
    • IV — occlusion.
    • V — transection with active extravasation.
  • Treatment of most VAI is MEDICAL:
    • Antithrombotic therapy — antiplatelet (aspirin) or systemic anticoagulation — to prevent thromboembolic stroke, balanced against the bleeding risk from other injuries.
    • Aspirin is generally as effective as heparin with a lower bleeding risk, and is the practical choice in the polytrauma patient.
    • Endovascular intervention (stenting, coiling) is reserved for pseudoaneurysm, active extravasation or a symptomatic lesion.
    • Repeat imaging at 7–10 days guides duration; many low-grade injuries heal.
  • VAI as a complication of cervical spine SURGERY:
    • C1-C2 transarticular screws — an aberrant/high-riding vertebral artery in the C2 isthmus, present in ~20%, is the key pre-operative consideration. Obtain a CT or CT angiogram and plan accordingly.
    • C2 pedicle screws, and lateral mass screws placed too medially or anteriorly.
    • Anterior cervical decompression extending laterally beyond the uncovertebral joints — the uncinate process is the landmark that protects the artery.
    • Posterior C1 dissection more than ~1.5 cm from the midline, where the artery lies in the groove on the posterior arch.
  • Intra-operative management of a vertebral artery injury — the sequence:
    • Tamponade with a haemostatic agent and direct pressure.
    • Complete the procedure WITHOUT placing a screw on the contralateral side — a bilateral injury is catastrophic.
    • Obtain a post-operative angiogram, with endovascular management as required.
    • Do NOT blindly attempt to ligate or explore the vessel — you will make it worse and you cannot see it.
    • Consider leaving the screw in place if it is tamponading the injury, and manage endovascularly.
  • The outcome that matters:
    • A unilateral vertebral artery occlusion is usually well tolerated.
    • Bilateral injury, or occlusion of a DOMINANT vessel, risks brainstem and posterior circulation infarction — the feared outcome (Wallenberg/lateral medullary syndrome, cerebellar infarct).
    • The left vertebral artery is dominant in roughly half of people; know which side is dominant before operating.

Complications of spine surgery: Dural tear, CSF leak & wound

Recognising and managing an incidental durotomy and its sequelae.

2 of 10 sittings2019, 2025

  • How often it happens:
    • Incidental durotomy occurs in ~1–5% of primary lumbar decompressions and up to 10–17% of revision procedures.
    • More common with revision surgery, ossified ligamentum flavum or OPLL, ankylosing spondylitis, dural ectasia (Marfan), and in the elderly with thin, adherent dura.
    • A recognised and repaired durotomy has no effect on long-term outcome; an unrecognised one does.
  • Repair:
    • Primary repair whenever possible — a running or interrupted 6-0 non-absorbable suture (Prolene) with a taper-point needle.
    • Augment with a dural sealant or fibrin glue, and a patch (fascia, muscle, collagen matrix) where the defect cannot be closed directly.
    • Enlarge the laminectomy if needed to see the edges — repairing through a keyhole fails.
    • Meticulous MULTILAYER FASCIAL CLOSURE is the single most important step in preventing a persistent leak.
  • Testing the repair:
    • The standard intra-operative test is a VALSALVA MANOEUVRE — or reverse Trendelenburg with sustained inspiratory pressure — performed by the anaesthetist while the surgeon watches the repair for leakage.
    • It raises the CSF pressure and demonstrates any residual defect.
    • This is the answer to "the best way to test for a leak after dural repair".
  • Post-operative management after a repaired durotomy:
    • Flat bed rest for 24–48 hours — though the evidence is weak and early mobilisation is increasingly favoured for a well-repaired dura.
    • Adequate hydration and avoidance of straining; stool softeners.
    • A subfascial drain should generally be AVOIDED, or kept on GRAVITY (not suction) after a durotomy — suction can propagate the leak.
  • Recognising a post-operative CSF leak — the classic scenario:
    • Three days after a lumbar decompression: severe POSITIONAL headache (worse upright, relieved lying flat), nausea, and a subfascial drain that was quiet on day 2 suddenly draining large volumes of clear fluid.
    • This is a CSF leak — the drain is now draining CSF.
    • The correct management is to STOP the suction and REMOVE the drain — a drain maintains a low-pressure fistula — then lie the patient flat with bed rest and hydration, and monitor.
    • If the leak persists: a lumbar subarachnoid drain, or return to theatre for exploration and repair.
    • An epidural blood patch is an option for a persistent low-pressure headache without a wound collection.
  • The critical error:
    • Leaving a SUCTION drain in place — it perpetuates the fistula, risks a pseudomeningocele, and increases the risk of meningitis.
    • Beta-2 transferrin testing confirms the fluid is CSF if there is doubt.
    • Do not simply "wait and see" a high-output leak with a headache.
  • Consequences of an unrecognised or persistent leak:
    • Pseudomeningocele and positional headache.
    • Wound dehiscence and CSF fistula.
    • Meningitis and arachnoiditis.
    • Rarely, intracranial hypotension with subdural haematoma — the catastrophic outcome, from downward brain sag tearing bridging veins.
  • Wound complications and infection:
    • Risk factors: diabetes, obesity, smoking, malnutrition, prolonged operative time, instrumentation and revision surgery.
    • Intrawound vancomycin powder reduces gram-positive surgical site infection in spine surgery, with a documented shift toward gram-negative organisms.
    • Deep infection with retained instrumentation is generally treated with débridement, irrigation and antibiotic suppression WITH IMPLANT RETENTION until fusion, rather than immediate hardware removal — stability aids eradication.
    • Remove the implants only once the fusion is solid, or if the infection cannot be controlled.

Complications of spine surgery: Positioning & nerve palsy

Positioning-related nerve injuries in spine surgery.

1 of 10 sittings2025

  • Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve palsy (meralgia paraesthetica) after prone positioning:
    • A common and under-recognised complication of PRONE positioning for posterior spinal surgery.
    • Presents with numbness, burning and dysaesthesia over the ANTEROLATERAL THIGH.
    • NO motor deficit — the nerve is purely sensory.
  • The mechanism — the answer to "why does this happen":
    • DIRECT COMPRESSION of the nerve against the ANTERIOR SUPERIOR ILIAC SPINE by the pads/bolsters of the prone frame (Wilson frame, Jackson table, or chest and hip rolls).
    • The nerve passes just medial to the ASIS, under the inguinal ligament, and is very superficial there — so it is trapped between the pad and the bone.
    • Contributing factors: prolonged operative time, obesity, hip flexion, and excessive pressure or malpositioned hip bolsters.
  • Prevention and prognosis:
    • Position the hip pads LATERAL to the ASIS on the iliac crest, not over it.
    • Use adequate padding, avoid excessive hip flexion, and minimise operative time.
    • The prognosis is good — most resolve over weeks to months with reassurance and, if needed, neuropathic agents.
  • The other positioning complications of prone spine surgery — worth knowing as a list:
    • POST-OPERATIVE VISUAL LOSS (POVL) from ISCHAEMIC OPTIC NEUROPATHY — the most feared. Associated with prolonged prone positioning, large blood loss, hypotension, anaemia and a head-down position. Prevention: head neutral or slightly elevated, no direct globe pressure, and maintenance of perfusion pressure and haemoglobin.
    • Brachial plexus and ulnar neuropathy — from arm abduction >90°, external rotation, or pressure at the elbow. Tuck the arms or position them in the "superman" position with the shoulders <90°.
    • Abdominal compression — raises epidural venous pressure and increases intra-operative bleeding; ensure the abdomen hangs free.
    • Pressure sores at the chin, chest, iliac crest and knees.
    • Compartment syndrome of the lower limbs in prolonged cases, particularly in the knee-chest position.
  • Lateral (transpsoas) lumbar interbody approaches — a distinct set of risks:
    • Lumbar plexus and genitofemoral nerve injury with thigh numbness, and hip flexor (psoas) weakness.
    • Highest at L4-5, where the plexus lies most anterior within the psoas.
    • Directional EMG neuromonitoring is MANDATORY for a transpsoas approach.
    • Most thigh symptoms are transient, but a genuine plexus injury can be permanent.
  • Anterior lumbar approaches:
    • Retrograde ejaculation from superior hypogastric plexus injury at L5-S1 — hence blunt dissection and NO monopolar cautery in that region in men, and an explicit pre-operative discussion.
    • Vascular injury — the left common iliac vein at L4-5 is the structure at greatest risk; have an access surgeon and a plan.
    • Ureteric injury, and lymphocele from disruption of lymphatics.
  • The medicolegal point:
    • Always document a careful PRE-OPERATIVE neurologic examination.
    • Attributing a pre-existing deficit to positioning after the fact is a common medicolegal problem — and an undocumented pre-operative examination makes it indefensible.
    • Document the positioning, padding and the time spent prone.

Intraoperative neuromonitoring & spinal cord monitoring

Modalities, alert criteria and the response protocol.

1 of 10 sittings2025

  • Multimodal monitoring is the standard — know what each modality watches:
    • Somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) monitor the DORSAL COLUMNS (posterior cord, posterior spinal artery territory) and are more resistant to anaesthesia.
    • Transcranial motor evoked potentials (tcMEPs) monitor the CORTICOSPINAL TRACTS (anterior cord, anterior spinal artery territory) and are the most sensitive for impending motor injury.
    • Free-running and triggered EMG monitors nerve roots and pedicle screw placement — a triggered threshold below ~6–8 mA suggests a medial breach.
    • SSEPs alone can miss an isolated anterior cord (motor) injury — which is why MEPs became mandatory for deformity work.
  • Alert criteria:
    • SSEPs — a >50% reduction in AMPLITUDE or a >10% increase in LATENCY.
    • tcMEPs — a >50–75% reduction in amplitude; many centres use an all-or-none or >75% criterion.
    • A 70% drop in MEPs after instrumentation is a MAJOR alert requiring immediate action, not observation.
    • Compare with the patient's own baseline, established after positioning and before incision.
  • Response protocol — the checklist to recite:
    • (1) Confirm the signal is real — check leads and connections, and confirm no anaesthetic change has occurred. Halogenated agents and neuromuscular blockade abolish MEPs; a bolus of propofol or a switch to an inhaled agent is a common cause of a false alert.
    • (2) Optimise physiology — RAISE THE MEAN ARTERIAL PRESSURE (to >85–90 mmHg), correct anaemia (transfuse), and correct hypothermia, hypocapnia and hypovolaemia.
    • (3) Remove the mechanical insult — release the correction/distraction, remove the rods, and check implant position, removing any suspect screws.
    • (4) If signals do not return, perform a Stagnara WAKE-UP TEST.
    • (5) Consider steroids and post-operative imaging.
  • What usually causes a significant MEP drop during deformity correction:
    • A combination of the CORRECTION MANOEUVRE — distraction and derotation causing cord stretch and vascular compromise — and RELATIVE HYPOTENSION with cord ischaemia.
    • It is usually NOT direct screw impingement, though that must be excluded.
    • This is why the first therapeutic actions are to RAISE THE BLOOD PRESSURE and RELEASE THE CORRECTION.
    • The cord's blood supply is most vulnerable in the mid-thoracic watershed, which is why thoracic deformity carries the highest risk.
  • Anaesthetic requirements:
    • Total intravenous anaesthesia (propofol plus an opioid).
    • NO neuromuscular blockade after intubation.
    • Minimal or no halogenated agent — volatile agents suppress MEPs in a dose-dependent way.
    • Failure to communicate this to anaesthesia is a common cause of unusable signals — agree it at the team brief.
  • What monitoring can and cannot do:
    • Monitoring reduces but does not eliminate neurologic injury.
    • It is standard of care for deformity correction, intramedullary tumour surgery, and any procedure where the cord or roots are at risk.
    • A loss of signals that does not recover after the full protocol should prompt ABANDONMENT of the correction and a wake-up test — and, if a deficit is confirmed, return to theatre for implant removal and decompression.
    • Staging the correction and returning another day is a legitimate and often correct decision.
  • A trap worth knowing:
    • Positioning-related brachial plexus and ulnar nerve injury is a recognised cause of an UPPER LIMB SSEP change during a deformity case.
    • Check arm position before assuming a spinal cause — an isolated upper limb change with normal lower limb signals is a positioning problem, not a cord problem.

Lumbar total disc arthroplasty

Indications, contraindications and outcomes.

1 of 10 sittings2024F

  • What it is intended to do:
    • Lumbar total disc replacement (TDR) treats discogenic low back pain while PRESERVING segmental motion.
    • The theoretical advantage over fusion is reduced adjacent segment degeneration.
    • It is an operation for back pain, not for radiculopathy or stenosis.
  • The single best predictor of a good outcome is patient selection:
    • A YOUNG patient.
    • SINGLE-LEVEL, purely DISCOGENIC pain.
    • PRESERVED FACET JOINTS.
    • No instability or spondylolisthesis, and no significant osteoporosis.
    • Normal alignment, with minimal or no radicular symptoms.
    • A normal BMI, and failure of at least 6 months of good non-operative treatment.
  • Contraindications — the essential list:
    • FACET ARTHROPATHY at the index level — the most important. Motion is preserved through a degenerate, painful joint, which guarantees persistent pain.
    • Spondylolisthesis or any segmental instability.
    • Spondylolysis / pars defect.
    • Osteoporosis or metabolic bone disease.
    • Significant scoliosis or deformity.
    • Central or lateral recess stenosis requiring posterior decompression.
    • Previous posterior decompression compromising the posterior elements.
    • Infection, morbid obesity, and allergy to implant materials.
  • Outcomes:
    • The FDA IDE trials showed TDR to be NON-INFERIOR to fusion at 2 and 5 years for single-level degenerative disc disease.
    • Faster early recovery and comparable satisfaction.
    • The long-term promise of reduced adjacent segment disease has NOT been convincingly demonstrated in the LUMBAR spine — the evidence is considerably stronger in the cervical spine.

    • Approach-related VASCULAR injury — the anterior retroperitoneal approach places the great vessels at risk, and revision through scarred tissue is extremely hazardous. An access surgeon is standard.
    • Retrograde ejaculation from superior hypogastric plexus injury, especially at L5-S1 in men — use blunt dissection and avoid monopolar cautery, and consent for it explicitly.
    • Implant subsidence, migration and wear.
    • Heterotopic ossification with spontaneous fusion — which silently negates the purpose of the implant.
    • Persistent pain from an unrecognised facet source.
  • The revision problem:
    • Revision of a failed lumbar TDR is a major undertaking.
    • The difficulty of anterior revision is a legitimate consideration in the INITIAL decision.
    • The implant's durability must outlast the patient's remaining life expectancy, or the revision problem becomes real — which is a strong argument for caution in a very young patient, the same group who otherwise seem ideal.
  • The practical reality:
    • The narrow indication set means TDR is appropriate for a SMALL MINORITY of patients with back pain.
    • The commonest error is offering it to a patient with multilevel disease, facet arthropathy, or a predominantly psychosocial pain presentation.
    • Screen for yellow flags and unrealistic expectations as carefully as for facet arthropathy.

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture & augmentation

The commonest osteoporotic fracture and its management.

1 of 10 sittings2022

  • Epidemiology — and the fact that gets tested:
    • Vertebral compression fractures are the most common osteoporotic fracture.
    • The majority occur in postmenopausal women, most commonly at the thoracolumbar junction (T12–L1).
    • The majority — roughly two thirds — are ASYMPTOMATIC and are discovered incidentally on imaging obtained for another reason. This is the classic "which is true" answer.

    • Anterior column failure only, with an intact middle and posterior column and an intact posterior ligamentous complex.

    • A "compression fracture" WITH a neurologic deficit, retropulsion, or posterior element involvement is NOT a simple osteoporotic VCF and requires further work-up — reclassify it as a burst fracture or a pathologic lesion.
  • It is a SENTINEL event — the most important and most-omitted intervention:
    • A VCF confers a five-fold increased risk of a subsequent vertebral fracture and a two- to three-fold increased risk of hip fracture.
    • It is associated with increased mortality, and with progressive kyphosis, restrictive lung disease and early satiety.
    • EVERY patient requires osteoporosis assessment and treatment — BMD, fracture-risk assessment (FRAX/CAROC), calcium and vitamin D, and pharmacologic therapy.
    • This is the most important intervention and the one most often omitted.
  • Always exclude a pathologic fracture — the red flags:
    • Age <55, or no significant osteoporosis.
    • Involvement above T5.
    • A lesion involving the PEDICLE, or a paraspinal soft-tissue mass.
    • Weight loss, night pain, or a known malignancy.
    • The differential includes METASTASIS (more common overall) and MULTIPLE MYELOMA (the commonest primary malignancy causing vertebral collapse).
    • Obtain an MRI, and biopsy if there is doubt; a bone marrow oedema pattern confined to the vertebral body with a preserved pedicle favours a benign fracture.
  • Initial management is non-operative:
    • Analgesia, early mobilisation and physiotherapy, with a brace for comfort only if needed.
    • Most improve substantially within 6–12 weeks.
    • Avoid prolonged bed rest — it accelerates bone loss, deconditioning and delirium in exactly the population least able to tolerate it.
    • Calcitonin has been used for acute pain but the evidence is weak.
  • Vertebral augmentation — the evidence is genuinely mixed:
    • Two 2009 NEJM sham-controlled trials (Buchbinder; Kallmes) found NO benefit of vertebroplasty over a sham procedure.
    • The VAPOUR trial (2016) found benefit in patients with SEVERE pain of RECENT onset (<6 weeks).
    • Current position: augmentation may be considered for severe, refractory pain of relatively acute onset that has failed conservative management.
    • It is NOT indicated for chronic or asymptomatic fractures.
  • Complications of augmentation:
    • Cement extravasation — into the canal (cord or root compression), into the disc (raising the risk of adjacent-level fracture), and into the venous system → PULMONARY CEMENT EMBOLISM.
    • Adjacent-level fracture, attributed to the stiffness mismatch created by the cemented level.
    • Kyphoplasty has a LOWER cement leakage rate than vertebroplasty, because the balloon creates a cavity allowing lower-pressure injection of more viscous cement; clinical outcomes are broadly similar.
  • When surgery is required:
    • Neurologic deficit.
    • Progressive kyphotic deformity with pain and functional loss.
    • Instability.
    • Nonunion with vertebral osteonecrosis — Kümmell disease, recognised by an intravertebral vacuum cleft on CT or a fluid sign on MRI.
    • Cement augmentation of the PEDICLE SCREWS is essential when instrumenting osteoporotic bone, along with longer constructs and more fixation points.

Scheuermann kyphosis

The rigid adolescent thoracic kyphosis.

1 of 10 sittings2024F

  • Diagnostic criteria (Sorensen):
    • Anterior wedging of ≥5° at THREE OR MORE CONSECUTIVE vertebrae.
    • Thoracic kyphosis >45–50° that is RIGID — it does not correct on hyperextension.
    • Associated features: Schmorl nodes, endplate irregularity, disc space narrowing, and a compensatory hyperlordosis of the lumbar and cervical spine.
    • Hamstring tightness is almost universal.
  • Two forms:
    • THORACIC (classic) Scheuermann — apex T7–T9; presents with deformity and mid-thoracic pain.
    • THORACOLUMBAR/LUMBAR (atypical) Scheuermann — apex T11–L2; more common in athletes and manual labourers; presents predominantly with PAIN rather than deformity.
  • How LUMBAR Scheuermann differs — the recurring question:
    • Less kyphotic deformity.
    • More strongly associated with axial back pain and with repetitive loading / athletic activity.
    • Characteristically shows Schmorl nodes, endplate irregularity and disc narrowing.
    • WITHOUT the marked anterior vertebral wedging seen in the thoracic form.
    • The classic ≥5° wedging of three consecutive vertebrae is a THORACIC criterion and is NOT typically found in lumbar Scheuermann — the answer to "what is not commonly found in lumbar Scheuermann".
  • Distinguish from POSTURAL kyphosis:
    • Postural kyphosis is FLEXIBLE — it corrects on hyperextension and on prone extension.
    • It has NO vertebral wedging and NO endplate changes.
    • Rigidity plus radiographic wedging is the discriminator.
  • Natural history:
    • Most patients do well; the deformity progresses during the adolescent growth spurt and then stabilises.
    • Back pain is common in adolescence but the long-term functional prognosis is generally good — patients work and function normally.
    • Curves >75–100° may cause restrictive pulmonary compromise; below that, cardiopulmonary function is preserved.
    • Neurologic compromise is rare, from an associated thoracic disc herniation at the apex.
  • Non-operative treatment:
    • Observation for curves <60°.
    • Bracing (Milwaukee, or a modified TLSO) for curves 60–80° in a skeletally IMMATURE patient (Risser ≤2/3) with at least 1 year of growth remaining.
    • The brace works by extension and requires flexibility, so it has limited success in rigid curves.
    • Physiotherapy and extension exercises for pain and postural strengthening — the mainstay for lumbar Scheuermann.
  • Surgical indications and technique:
    • Curves >75–80° at skeletal maturity.
    • Progressive deformity.
    • Intractable pain unresponsive to non-operative care.
    • Neurologic compromise (rare).
    • Modern treatment is posterior instrumented fusion with multiple Ponte (Smith-Petersen) osteotomies.
    • Correct to approximately 50% — over-correction risks proximal AND distal junctional kyphosis.
    • The distal fusion level must include the FIRST LORDOTIC DISC and the STABLE SAGITTAL VERTEBRA to prevent distal junctional kyphosis — the commonest complication. Proximally, include the proximal end vertebra.

Spine biomechanics & instrumentation

Rod and construct mechanics in spinal instrumentation.

2 of 10 sittings2018 (x4), 2024S

  • Rod stiffness — the two determinants:
    • Geometry: the area moment of inertia is proportional to the RADIUS TO THE FOURTH POWER (r⁴) for a solid cylindrical rod.
    • So a small increase in rod diameter produces a large increase in stiffness — a 6.35 mm rod is roughly 1.7× stiffer than a 5.5 mm rod of the same material.
    • Material modulus: cobalt-chrome > stainless steel > titanium > PEEK.
    • So a cobalt-chrome rod of the same diameter is substantially stiffer than titanium; titanium has the advantage of better imaging compatibility and a modulus closer to bone.
  • The stiffness–biology trade-off:
    • A stiffer construct provides better deformity correction and resists loss of correction.
    • But it increases stress shielding at the fusion mass, and increases stress at the ENDS of the construct — raising the risk of proximal junctional kyphosis and adjacent segment failure.
    • A higher-stiffness construct means LESS motion of the fusion mass, not more — the classic false statement in the spinal-construct question.
    • Beyond the yield point, a rod that has been contoured has been plastically deformed and is notched and fatigue-weakened — repeated in-situ bending is a real cause of rod fracture.
  • Increasing the stability of a construct in OSTEOPOROTIC bone — the high-yield list:
    • CEMENT (PMMA) AUGMENTATION of the pedicle screws — the single most effective measure.
    • Increase the number of fixation points and levels instrumented, spreading the load.
    • Larger-diameter and longer screws, maximising the bone–screw interface.
    • Bicortical purchase.
    • Hybrid constructs — adding hooks or sublaminar bands to screws.
    • UNDERTAP the pedicle (or do not tap at all), which increases pull-out strength.
    • Crosslinks, and extending the construct to the pelvis when fusing to the sacrum.
    • Increasing rod diameter alone does NOT solve the problem — failure in osteoporotic bone occurs at the BONE–SCREW interface, not in the rod.
  • Cement augmentation in a spine fracture (vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty):
    • The principal risk is CEMENT EXTRAVASATION — into the canal (cord or root compression), into the disc (increasing the risk of adjacent-level fracture), and into the venous system → PULMONARY CEMENT EMBOLISM.
    • Kyphoplasty creates a cavity with a balloon, allowing more viscous cement to be injected at lower pressure, and has a LOWER rate of cement leakage than vertebroplasty — though clinical outcomes are broadly similar.
    • Adjacent-level fracture after augmentation is well described and is attributed to the stiffness mismatch created by the cemented level.
    • Use a high-viscosity cement, inject under live fluoroscopy, and stop at the first sign of posterior leak.
  • Screw pull-out strength — what it actually depends on:
    • Bone mineral density — the dominant factor, and the one you cannot change intra-operatively.
    • Screw OUTER diameter, thread depth and pitch (the core diameter governs bending strength, not pull-out).
    • Length of purchase — aim for 80% of the vertebral body depth.
    • Technique — undertapping increases pull-out strength; repeated insertion and removal decreases it substantially.
    • A convergent (triangulated) screw trajectory improves construct pull-out beyond individual screw strength.
  • Crosslinks:
    • They improve TORSIONAL (rotational) stability substantially.
    • They add little to flexion-extension or lateral bending stiffness.
    • Most useful in a short construct after a three-column injury or a corpectomy.
