Acetabular fracture: Classification, approach & outcomes

Letournel classification, the radiographic lines, approach selection and prognostic factors.

10 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2020 (x3), 2021 (x3), 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x3), 2024F, 2025

  • Read the six lines on the AP pelvis first, then name the fracture:
    • Iliopectineal line → anterior column; ilioischial line → posterior column.
    • Anterior wall, posterior wall, teardrop, and the roof/sourcil complete the six.
    • A disrupted iliopectineal line with a posterior wall fragment = anterior column with posterior hemitransverse, or an associated both-column pattern.
  • Judet views — the mnemonic is obturator = anterior column + posterior wall:
    • Obturator oblique (rolled 45° toward the injured side) → anterior column and posterior wall.
    • Iliac oblique (rolled 45° away) → posterior column and anterior wall.
  • Letournel: five elementary + five associated patterns:
    • Elementary — posterior wall, posterior column, anterior wall, anterior column, transverse.
    • Associated — posterior column + posterior wall, transverse + posterior wall, T-type, anterior column/wall + posterior hemitransverse, associated both-column.
  • The associated both-column (ABC) fracture — the "floating acetabulum":
    • Defined by the "spur sign" on the obturator oblique — intact iliac wing still attached to the SI joint, with no articular surface remaining attached to the axial skeleton.
    • The only pattern that can achieve secondary congruence — fragments rotate around the head to give a congruent hip without anatomic reduction.
    • This is why selected ABC fractures in the elderly can be treated non-operatively.
  • Approach selection:
    • Posterior wall / posterior column → Kocher-Langenbeck.
    • Anterior wall / anterior column → ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa).
    • Associated both-column → usually ilioinguinal/anterior intrapelvic (the best single approach).
    • Quadrilateral plate work (e.g. an elderly ABC pattern) → anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa), which gives direct medial access.
    • Transverse/T-type → depends on the dominant displacement; extended iliofemoral for complex patterns (high HO rate).
  • Quality of articular reduction is the strongest predictor of outcome (Matta):
    • Anatomic reduction (<2 mm step) is the single most important modifiable factor.
    • Restoring congruity of the weightbearing dome matters most.
  • Poor prognostic factors:
    • Age >55–60; femoral head damage/impaction; posterior wall comminution or a fragment >40–50%.
    • Initial hip dislocation (especially delayed reduction); non-anatomic reduction (>2 mm); dome impaction; associated femoral neck fracture; delay to surgery >2–3 weeks.
    • An ipsilateral femoral shaft fracture is NOT itself a poor prognostic factor for the acetabulum — a common distractor.
  • Posterior wall fixation — indications:
    • Instability on examination under anaesthesia is the definitive test.
    • Fragment size: >50% always unstable; <20% usually stable; 20–50% requires an EUA.
    • Also: incongruent joint, intra-articular fragment, marginal impaction.
    • Posterior wall fractures have the worst outcomes of the elementary patterns despite looking simple — head injury, marginal impaction and sciatic nerve injury.
  • Complications:
    • Heterotopic ossification highest with the extended iliofemoral, then Kocher-Langenbeck — consider indomethacin or single-dose radiation.
    • Sciatic nerve (peroneal division) at risk posteriorly — keep the hip extended and the knee flexed.
  • The elderly acetabulum: higher conversion to THA (~20–30% at 2 years in some series), poorer function and higher perioperative morbidity — acute or delayed THA (± fixation) is increasingly favoured.

Femoral shaft fracture: Nailing, rotation & ipsilateral neck fracture

Nailing technique, malrotation, the fracture table, and the missed femoral neck fracture.

8 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022 (x4), 2024S (x2), 2024F (x2), 2025

  • Reamed, statically locked IM nailing is the gold standard — union >95%.
    • Reaming improves union in the femur and tibia (SPRINT) without a clinically significant increase in pulmonary complications in the resuscitated patient.
  • Malrotation is common and functionally significant (up to 20–30% have >15°). Ways to judge rotation intra-operatively:
    • Lesser trochanter profile vs the contralateral side (template pre-op with the patella facing forward) — the most used.
    • Cortical step sign and the diameter difference sign.
    • Linea aspera alignment; clinical hip rotation with the knee flexed.
    • Comparing the fluoroscopic knee (posterior condylar axis) with the hip on the same image.
  • Reading a post-op rotational CT:
    • "Left retroverted 10°, right anteverted 15°" = a 25° difference — the values add when one side is retroverted.
    • That is clinically significant internal malrotation → derotation osteotomy or nail revision if symptomatic.
  • Fracture (traction) table — the classic complications:
    • Internal malrotation (the traction boot internally rotates the distal fragment).
    • Pudendal nerve palsy from the perineal post; sciatic/peroneal palsy.
    • Compartment syndrome of the contralateral (well) leg from the hemilithotomy position.
    • Practical difficulty assessing rotation and length.
    • A fracture table does not cause fat embolism syndrome — a common distractor.
  • Start-point errors:
    • Piriformis entry — too anterior risks anterior cortical blowout of the neck and increased hoop stress; too posterior risks the femoral neck blood supply and posterior comminution.
    • Trochanteric entry — too lateral causes a varus malreduction (medial cortical impingement); too medial risks the neck.
  • Distal anterior cortical perforation with an antegrade nail:
    • Caused by mismatch between the nail's radius of curvature (~1.5–3.0 m) and the greater anterior bow of the patient's femur — nails are straighter than many femora (elderly, Asian patients).
    • A posteriorly placed start point contributes.
  • Ipsilateral femoral neck + shaft fracture — the classic trap:
    • Occurs in ~1–9% of femoral shaft fractures and is missed in 20–50% of cases.
    • Typically a vertical, minimally displaced basicervical/transcervical fracture in a young patient.
    • Always get a dedicated internal-rotation AP of the hip (and CT) in high-energy shaft fractures, and inspect the hip on post-nailing images.
  • The neck fracture takes priority — it determines the outcome (AVN/nonunion far outweigh a shaft nonunion):
    • Options: a reconstruction (cephalomedullary) nail, or — preferred by many — independent neck fixation (cannulated screws or a sliding hip screw) plus a retrograde nail for the shaft, allowing unconstrained optimal neck fixation.
    • An iatrogenic neck fracture during nailing requires immediate recognition and neck fixation before completing the shaft.
  • Cannabis use is associated with increased anaesthetic requirements, higher post-op pain scores and greater opioid consumption — a relevant preoperative history.

Open fracture management: Antibiotics, débridement & timing

Gustilo classification, antibiotic choice and timing, irrigation, and soft-tissue coverage.

7 of 10 sittings2018 (x4), 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2023 (x4), 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Gustilo-Anderson — definitively classified in theatre after débridement, not in the emergency department:
    • I — wound <1 cm, clean, minimal soft-tissue damage.
    • II — wound 1–10 cm, moderate soft-tissue damage, no significant contamination.
    • IIIA — extensive soft-tissue damage but adequate coverage; includes any segmental fracture, farmyard injury, high-energy or gunshot injury regardless of wound size.
    • IIIB — inadequate coverage requiring a flap, with periosteal stripping.
    • IIIC — arterial injury requiring repair.
  • Antibiotics are the single most important intervention — and timing is the key variable:
    • Give within 1 hour (certainly within 3 hours) of injury.
    • Delay beyond 3 hours is a stronger predictor of infection than the time to débridement.
  • Antibiotic choice:
    • Type I/II → first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin).
    • Type III → add gram-negative cover (aminoglycoside, or increasingly piperacillin-tazobactam / ceftriaxone).
    • Farm/soil → add penicillin (or metronidazole) for clostridial cover.
    • Fresh water → Aeromonas hydrophila (fluoroquinolone or 3rd-gen cephalosporin). Salt water → Vibrio (doxycycline + 3rd-gen cephalosporin).
    • Duration: 24 hours after wound closure (24–72 h for type III) — prolonged courses do not help.
  • Timing of débridement — the "6-hour rule" is not supported:
    • Débridement within 24 hours is acceptable for most open fractures provided antibiotics were early (FLOW, LEAP).
    • Still urgent: gross contamination, vascular compromise, compartment syndrome, farmyard/marine injuries.
  • FLOW trial (Canadian-led, NEJM 2015) — irrigation solution and pressure:
    • Normal saline was superior to castile soap (soap had a higher reoperation rate).
    • Low (gravity) pressure was equivalent to high pressure, trending better.
    • Answer: normal saline at low/gravity pressure; high-pressure lavage damages bone and drives contaminant deeper.
  • Soft-tissue coverage timing:
    • Delay beyond ~7 days markedly increases deep infection and flap failure (the exam answer).
    • Godina's classic work favoured <72 hours, which remains ideal.
  • Local antibiotic delivery: antibiotic bead pouches and intrawound vancomycin powder — reduces gram-positive deep infection but shifts flora toward gram-negative organisms (the standard answer).
  • Other points:
    • Primary closure of a clean type I/II wound after adequate débridement is acceptable and may reduce infection.
    • Address tetanus prophylaxis; NPWT is a temporising dressing, not a substitute for coverage.
  • Pregnancy with an open tibia: avoid tetracyclines (fetal teeth/bone), fluoroquinolones (cartilage) and aminoglycosides (oto/nephrotoxicity — relative). Cephalosporins and penicillins are safe.

Polytrauma: Damage control orthopaedics & timing of fixation

Resuscitation endpoints, DCO versus early appropriate care, and the tertiary survey.

9 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018, 2020 (x4), 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • The three strategies:
    • DCO — temporary spanning external fixation, haemorrhage/contamination control, deferring definitive fixation until resuscitated.
    • Early total care (ETC) — definitive fixation within 24 hours.
    • Early appropriate care (EAC), the modern synthesis — definitive fixation of pelvis, acetabulum, femur and spine within 36 hours provided the patient is adequately resuscitated, judged by physiology rather than time or ISS.
  • Resuscitation endpoints (Vallier's EAC criteria) — memorise the numbers:
    • Lactate <4.0 mmol/L
    • pH ≥7.25
    • Base excess ≥ −5.5 mmol/L
    • Meeting these → definitive fixation within 36 hours reduces complications. Failing them → DCO.
  • Applying the numbers in practice:
    • 16 h post-injury, off pressors, lactate 3.5, base deficit 4.2, pH 7.3 → resuscitated → definitive fixation of pelvis, femur and spine.
    • pH 7.2, lactate 7, base excess −11 → not resuscitated → damage control (ex-fix, binder/packing, continue resuscitation).
  • Indications for DCO:
    • Persistent haemodynamic instability or coagulopathy; the lethal triad (hypothermia <35°C, acidosis, coagulopathy).
    • Severe chest injury/pulmonary contusion with hypoxia; severe TBI (avoid hypotension).
    • Massive transfusion requirement; prolonged anticipated operative time in a marginal patient.
  • The "second hit" is the conceptual basis — the injury primes the systemic inflammatory response and a prolonged bloody operation delivers a second insult (ARDS, MODS, death). Reamed femoral nailing in an under-resuscitated patient with a severe chest injury is the classic example.
  • Damage control resuscitation accompanies DCO:
    • Permissive hypotension until haemorrhage control; balanced 1:1:1 transfusion; limit crystalloid.
    • TXA within 3 hours (CRASH-2); aggressive rewarming.
  • Massive transfusion of citrated blood causes hypocalcaemia (citrate chelates ionised calcium):
    • This is the cause of arrhythmia and hypotension during massive transfusion — treat with IV calcium.
    • Hyperkalaemia (stored cells), hypothermia and acidosis are the other contributors.
  • The tertiary survey:
    • Systematic head-to-toe re-examination with review of all imaging, within 24 hours and repeated when the patient is awake.
    • Missed injuries occur in 10–40% of polytrauma patients, most commonly extremity fractures — the tertiary survey is the single most effective detection tool.
    • Missed injuries are MORE common with night-time admission, reduced GCS and higher injury severity — the classic false statement is that rates are the same at night.
  • Bilateral femur fractures carry substantially higher mortality, ISS, ARDS, fat embolism and transfusion requirement — a marker of high energy and a lower threshold for damage control.

Femoral neck fracture: Young & elderly

The young neck fracture as an emergency, the elderly arthroplasty debate, and the Canadian trials.

7 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2021 (x3), 2022, 2023 (x3), 2024S, 2025

  • The young patient (<50–60) with a displaced neck fracture is a surgical emergency:
    • Priorities are urgent anatomic reduction and stable internal fixation to preserve the head — arthroplasty is not the answer in this age group.
    • AVN 20–30%, nonunion 10–30%; the strongest modifiable factor is quality of reduction.
    • A capsulotomy to decompress the intracapsular haematoma is often added.
  • Vertically oriented (Pauwels III) fractures see high shear → use a construct that resists shear: a fixed-angle device (sliding hip screw + anti-rotation screw, or a cephalomedullary device) rather than three cannulated screws alone.
  • In the elderly, displaced intracapsular fractures get arthroplasty, not fixation — fixation carries a 30–40% reoperation rate.
    • FAITH (Canadian-led): sliding hip screw vs cannulated screws in fractures amenable to fixation — no overall difference in reoperation, SHS better in specific subgroups (displaced, basicervical, smokers).
  • HEALTH trial (Canadian-led, NEJM 2019) — THA vs hemiarthroplasty, age >50:
    • No significant difference in the primary outcome (secondary hip procedures) at 24 months.
    • THA gave modestly better function and quality of life, with a higher dislocation rate.
    • Synthesis: THA for the active, cognitively intact, independent patient (especially with pre-existing arthritis); hemiarthroplasty for the lower-demand, frail or cognitively impaired patient.
    • So a frail, walker-dependent patient with significant cardiac comorbidity and IADL dependence → cemented hemiarthroplasty, not THA and not fixation.
  • Cement the stem:
    • WHiTE 5 (NEJM 2022) and registries: cemented stems reduce periprosthetic fracture and improve early function/QoL with no increase in mortality.
    • The commonest reason for revision of an uncemented hemiarthroplasty is periprosthetic fracture.
    • NICE/AAOS favour cementation, with awareness of bone cement implantation syndrome.
  • Valgus-impacted (non-displaced) fracture in an elderly patient:
    • Usually in-situ fixation (cannulated screws) with good results, though ~10–20% displace.
    • "Stable enough to mobilise without fixation" is the classic false statement.
    • Posterior tilt >20° on the lateral predicts failure of fixation.
  • Timing and anticoagulation:
    • HIP ATTACK: accelerated surgery (<6 h) vs standard showed no difference in 90-day mortality, but reduced delirium, pain and length of stay.
    • The AAOS CPG supports surgery within 24–48 hours — reduces mortality, pain and complications.
    • Clopidogrel: do not delay surgery. Waiting 5–7 days increases mortality, pneumonia and length of stay; the extra blood loss is modest. Consider general rather than regional anaesthesia.
  • Femoral neck stress fracture (young athlete/recruit):
    • Tension side (superolateral) is unstable → prophylactic screw fixation.
    • Compression side (inferomedial) → protected weightbearing; fix if >50% of the neck width or an effusion is present.

Humeral shaft fracture & radial nerve palsy

Functional bracing, indications for fixation, plating technique and the radial nerve.

6 of 10 sittings2020, 2021, 2023 (x3), 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Functional (Sarmiento) bracing — union ~90–95% (the quoted figure is ~94.5%):
    • So "union rate is over 95%" is false, and "most non-op cases go to nonunion" is grossly false.
    • Acceptable alignment: <20° apex anterior/posterior, <30° varus/valgus, <3 cm shortening, <15° malrotation.
  • Reasons to convert from bracing to ORIF:
    • Inability to maintain acceptable alignment; unacceptable angulation/shortening.
    • A fracture that distracts (particularly transverse); brace intolerance/non-compliance; obesity preventing effective bracing.
    • Delayed union/nonunion; proximal-third or very distal fractures the brace cannot control.
    • A radial nerve palsy at presentation is NOT an indication to convert — the classic "all EXCEPT".
  • Indications for primary operative fixation:
    • Open fracture, vascular injury, associated forearm fracture ("floating elbow").
    • Polytrauma needing upper-limb weightbearing; ipsilateral brachial plexus injury; bilateral humeral fractures.
    • Pathologic, segmental, or intra-articular extension.
    • A secondary (post-manipulation) radial nerve palsy; failure of non-operative treatment.
  • Plating technique:
    • Compression plate with at least 6–8 cortices (3–4 bicortical screws) each side.
    • Spiral/oblique fracture → interfragmentary lag screw perpendicular to the fracture plane, neutralised with a plate — this creates the most compression and is the best fixation for a spiral shaft fracture.
  • Far-cortical gapping — a repeated question (humerus and femur):
    • A perfectly straight plate on a transverse fracture compresses the near cortex and gaps the far cortex.
    • The correction is to slightly over-bend (pre-bend) the plate at the fracture site so tightening compresses the far cortex first.
  • Radial nerve palsy accompanies 8–15% of humeral shaft fractures:
    • Most common with middle- and distal-third fractures — the Holstein-Lewis distal-third spiral fracture is classic.
    • >85–90% are neurapraxias that recover spontaneously, typically beginning at 3–4 months.
  • Management of a primary palsy = observation:
    • Splint to prevent contracture; EMG at ~6 weeks (fibrillations) and 3 months.
    • Explore if no recovery by 3–4 months, or immediately for an open fracture, vascular injury, or a palsy that develops after manipulation/fixation.
  • Order of recovery is proximal to distal:
    • Brachioradialis first → ECRL → supinator → ECU → EDC → EPL/EIP last.
  • Nonunion:
    • Hypertrophic = adequate biology, inadequate stability → compression plating (± lag screw), no bone graft needed.
    • Atrophic → débridement, compression plating and autologous bone graft.

Distal femur fracture & periarticular fixation

Tuning the locked-plate construct, nonunion, deforming forces, and the geriatric distal femur.

7 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2020, 2021 (x2), 2022, 2023, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • The locked plate must be tuned biologically, not just mechanically:
    • A plate that is too stiff over a short working length produces asymmetric motion (near cortex moves, far cortex does not) → nonunion.
    • To promote healing: longer plate with a longer working length, empty screw holes near the fracture, fewer and more widely spaced screws, titanium rather than stainless steel, and far-cortical/dynamic locking screws.
  • Contributors to nonunion (AO/OTA type C):
    • Obesity, diabetes, smoking, open fracture, infection.
    • Excessively stiff construct with a short working length; a stainless-steel plate; gapping/distraction; inadequate medial support in a comminuted metaphysis.
    • An anatomic reduction with cortical contact and a balanced construct is NOT a cause — the classic "all EXCEPT".
  • Technique points:
    • Place the plate parallel to the distal femoral articular surface.
    • Check the K-wire for the central distal screw on the lateral view — parallel to the joint line, centred in the condyles, avoiding the notch and trochlea.
    • A common error is a plate placed too anterior or in flexion → extension malunion.
  • Know the deforming forces (the "golf-club" deformity):
    • Gastrocnemius pulls the distal fragment into extension (apex posterior/recurvatum).
    • Adductors pull the shaft medially → varus.
    • Anticipate with a bump under the knee, a femoral distractor, or temporary joysticks.
  • Varus malunion is the classic late presentation (hip and knee pain at ~1 year) — from inadequate medial support, an unstable comminuted medial column, and early weightbearing.
  • The geriatric distal femur — fixation vs distal femoral replacement (DFR):
    • DFR allows immediate unrestricted weightbearing and avoids nonunion — favoured for a loose/failed knee prosthesis, unreconstructible articular comminution, very poor bone quality.
    • Fixation (dual plating, or retrograde nail + lateral plate) preserves bone stock and native anatomy — favoured when reconstruction is feasible.
    • Comparative studies show similar mortality, with faster mobilisation after DFR.
  • Periprosthetic distal femur fracture around a TKA (Rorabeck):
    • Type I/II (well-fixed component) → ORIF (lateral locking plate, or retrograde nail if the femoral component has an open box — confirm implant compatibility first).
    • Type III (loose component) → revision arthroplasty.
    • Nonunion after periprosthetic ORIF in an elderly patient (infection excluded) → usually revision to a DFR, avoiding further prolonged non-weightbearing.

Intertrochanteric & subtrochanteric femur fracture

Implant selection, the tip-apex distance, and the subtrochanteric reduction problem.

6 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019, 2021 (x2), 2022 (x2), 2024S (x2), 2025 (x2)

  • Implant selection turns on stability and the lateral wall:
    • Stable (AO/OTA 31-A1) → sliding hip screw (SHS) or cephalomedullary nail (CMN) — equivalent results, lower cost with SHS.
    • Unstable → must use a CMN: reverse obliquity (31-A3), subtrochanteric extension, deficient lateral wall, transverse patterns.
    • A sliding hip screw in a reverse-obliquity fracture medialises the shaft and fails.
  • Tip-apex distance (TAD):
    • Sum of tip-to-apex distances on AP + lateral, corrected for magnification.
    • Target <25 mm; TAD >25 mm is the strongest independent predictor of cut-out.
    • Optimal screw position is central-central or centre-inferior; superior and anterior is the worst.
  • Distal locking of a long CMN:
    • Static locking controls length and rotation — standard in unstable and subtrochanteric patterns.
    • Trade-off: it prevents controlled fracture-site compression/impaction, which in a stable pattern can promote nonunion — so short nails in stable fractures are often dynamically locked or unlocked.
  • Complications of cephalomedullary nailing:
    • Screw cut-out is commonest — driven by TAD and varus malreduction.
    • Varus malreduction; anterior perforation of the distal femur (radius-of-curvature mismatch); iatrogenic shaft fracture at the nail tip; lateral thigh pain.
    • AVN of the head is rare after an intertrochanteric fracture and is NOT a typical complication — the usual "all true EXCEPT".
  • Subtrochanteric deforming forces — know them cold:
    • Proximal fragment: flexed (iliopsoas), abducted (gluteus medius/minimus), externally rotated (short external rotators).
    • Distal fragment: adducted and shortened (adductors).
    • Nailing without deliberate correction → varus and procurvatum (apex anterior) malreduction.
  • Getting and holding the subtrochanteric reduction:
    • A lateral start point is wrong — start slightly medial/at the trochanteric tip and correct the proximal-fragment flexion.
    • Use blocking (Poller) screws, a percutaneous clamp or ball-spike pusher, a unicortical plate or cerclage cable, hip-flexed positioning (lateral, radiolucent table), and Schanz-pin joysticks.
    • Accept nothing worse than neutral — varus malreduction is the leading cause of nonunion.
  • Recognise the atypical (bisphosphonate-related) fracture:
    • Transverse subtrochanteric fracture, thick diaphyseal cortices, lateral cortical beaking, a bowed femur, trivial mechanism.
    • Impaired healing biology compounds the technical problem of an uncorrected varus/flexion deformity.
  • Risk factors for subtrochanteric nonunion: varus malreduction (dominant technical factor), gapping/distraction, smoking, atypical fracture, open fracture, inadequate fixation. A well-reduced fracture with cortical contact is not a risk factor.
  • Very dense bone in a young patient → osteopetrosis: expect obliterated canals, drill/reamer breakage, delayed union and iatrogenic fracture — use sharp new drills, copious irrigation (thermal necrosis), and consider plate fixation.

Pelvic ring injury: Classification, resuscitation & fixation

Young-Burgess, haemorrhage control, and the LC-1 stability question.

8 of 10 sittings2018, 2019, 2020, 2021 (x2), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Young-Burgess, by mechanism:
    • APC-I symphyseal widening <2.5 cm; APC-II >2.5 cm with anterior SI disruption, posterior SI intact (the "open book"); APC-III complete hemipelvis disruption.
    • LC-I sacral compression fracture on the side of impact; LC-II crescent fracture (iliac wing + posterior SI disruption); LC-III "windswept" (LC one side, APC the other).
    • VS — complete vertical displacement.
  • Bleeding by pattern:
    • APC-II/III and VS bleed most — pelvic volume increases, posterior venous plexus and internal iliac branches tear.
    • LC injuries have more associated head/abdominal injuries and generally less pelvic haemorrhage.
  • Haemorrhage control sequence:
    • Binder/sheet at the level of the GREATER TROCHANTERS (not the iliac crests).
    • Balanced blood products + TXA; identify the source (FAST/CT).
    • Angioembolisation for arterial bleeding; preperitoneal packing for venous/bony bleeding; external fixation or C-clamp for mechanical stability.
    • ~85–90% of pelvic bleeding is venous/bony and only 10–15% arterial — which is why binders and packing matter so much.
  • The LC-1 controversy — a repeatedly tested item:
    • Many LC-1 injuries are stable and treated non-operatively with early weightbearing, but a subset are occultly unstable.
    • Examination under anaesthesia with dynamic stress fluoroscopy is the accepted way to determine stability.
    • "You should not stress test an LC-1 because it will displace the fracture" is FALSE — displacement on stress simply reveals instability that was already there.
  • Indicators of an unstable LC-1: complete sacral fracture, displacement >10 mm on stress, inability to mobilise/weightbear for pain, displacement on serial films. Fixation (percutaneous iliosacral screws ± anterior fixation) allows earlier mobilisation.
  • Symphyseal injuries: widening >2.5 cm implies disruption of the sacrotuberous, sacrospinous and anterior SI ligaments → anterior plating, usually with posterior fixation for APC-III.
  • Associated urologic injury:
    • Pubic diastasis + blood at the meatus / abnormal retrograde urethrogram → urology consultation and suprapubic catheterisation, NOT repeated urethral catheterisation attempts.
    • Intraperitoneal bladder rupture → operative repair; extraperitoneal can often be managed with catheter drainage alone.
  • Scrutinise the AP pelvis for:
    • Symphyseal and SI widening; sacral arcuate line disruption.
    • L5 transverse process avulsion (iliolumbar ligament — an indirect sign of vertical instability); ischial spine (sacrospinous) and sacrotuberous avulsions; hemipelvis asymmetry.
    • Inlet view = anterior/posterior displacement; outlet view = superior/inferior displacement.
  • Paediatric pelvic ring fractures are usually stable and heal rapidly, and are rarely associated with catastrophic haemorrhage — but they mark high energy with many associated injuries. Triradiate cartilage injury risks acetabular growth arrest with progressive dysplasia.

Tibial shaft fracture & intramedullary nailing

The reaming debate, entry point, proximal-third deformity and blocking screws.

5 of 10 sittings2019 (x2), 2020 (x2), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024F (x2)

  • Reamed vs unreamed — SPRINT (Canadian-led):
    • Closed tibial shaft fractures → reamed nailing showed a benefit (reduced reoperation/events).
    • Open fractures → no significant difference.
    • Reaming improves union and allows a larger nail; the historical concern about endosteal blood supply and fat embolism has not translated into worse tibial outcomes.
  • Benefits of nailing vs closed treatment:
    • Better maintenance of alignment, earlier weightbearing, faster return to function, lower malunion and shortening.
    • It does NOT reduce the incidence of compartment syndrome — the classic "all benefits EXCEPT".
    • It introduces anterior knee pain (up to 50%).
  • Entry point is the single most important technical detail:
    • Ideal: AP — just medial to the lateral tibial spine; lateral — at the anterior edge of the plateau, in line with the anterior cortex, immediately adjacent to the articular margin.
    • Too lateral → valgus; too medial → varus; too anterior → apex-posterior (procurvatum); too posterior → apex-anterior and risk to posterior neurovascular structures.
  • Proximal-third fractures deform into apex-anterior (procurvatum) and valgus:
    • Cause: pull of the patellar tendon/quadriceps (extension) on the proximal fragment plus the anterior compartment/pes, and an anteriorly placed start point.
    • Correct with a semi-extended (suprapatellar or parapatellar) technique, a more lateral, proximal-anterior start point, and blocking (Poller) screws; a unicortical plate or femoral distractor also help.
  • Blocking (Poller) screws narrow the effective canal in the wide metaphysis, redirect the nail and add stability — place the screw on the concave side of the deformity (posterior in the proximal fragment for procurvatum, lateral for valgus).
  • Distal-third fracture with residual valgus after nailing → plate the fibula (restores lateral length/alignment), use a blocking screw, apply a unicortical plate, or clamp/distract before passing the nail. Simply exchanging for a larger nail does not reliably correct an established deformity.
  • Nailing vs plating for extra-articular distal tibia: nailing has fewer wound/infection problems; plating controls alignment better in very distal fractures; union rates are comparable — "nailing has a higher nonunion rate" is a common false statement.
  • Compartment syndrome is most common with tibial shaft fractures — nailing does not prevent it; maintain post-operative vigilance.

Hip dislocation & femoral head fracture (Pipkin)

Urgent reduction, blocks to reduction, and the Pipkin classification.

7 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • A traumatic hip dislocation is a surgical emergency — reduce within 6 hours:
    • AVN ~5% with reduction under 6 hours, rising to 20–50%+ beyond 6–12 hours.
    • Posterior dislocation accounts for ~90%.
  • Blocks to closed reduction (posterior):
    • Incarcerated osteochondral or femoral head fragment; inverted/interposed labrum.
    • Piriformis/short external rotators; a buttonholed femoral head through the capsule.
    • An associated femoral head or acetabular wall fracture.
  • Post-reduction CT is mandatory for every hip dislocation:
    • Identifies incarcerated fragments, marginal impaction, and head/acetabular fractures invisible on plain films.
    • Do not accept a "concentric" plain film as adequate; asymmetric joint-space widening suggests interposed tissue.
  • Pipkin classification of femoral head fractures:
    • Type I — inferior to the fovea (below the weightbearing surface) — better prognosis, often excised.
    • Type II — superior to the fovea (involves the weightbearing surface) — requires anatomic fixation.
    • Type III — either type plus a femoral neck fracture — the worst prognosis, highest AVN risk.
    • Type IV — either type plus an acetabular fracture.
  • Fix vs excise: fix a large fragment involving the weightbearing (superior) head that blocks concentric reduction or forms a substantial part of the articular surface (Pipkin II); small inferior (Pipkin I) fragments are frequently excised.
  • Approach for a Pipkin II:
    • Anterior (Smith-Petersen) — direct access to the anterosuperior head, and it does not further compromise the posterior retinacular (medial femoral circumflex) supply, already injured by a posterior dislocation.
    • Internervous interval: sartorius/rectus femoris (femoral nerve) vs tensor fascia lata (superior gluteal nerve).
    • The alternative is a surgical hip dislocation (Ganz) with a trochanteric flip.
  • Common Pipkin misconceptions:
    • Type I does NOT have a worse prognosis than type II — it is better (below the weightbearing surface).
    • AVN risk is driven by the dislocation and an associated neck fracture (type III), not by the head fragment itself.
    • Not all femoral head fractures require fixation.
  • Sciatic nerve injury in ~10–20% of posterior dislocations:
    • Peroneal division most affected — more lateral, more tethered, less connective tissue.
    • Most are neurapraxic; document the exam before and after reduction.
    • A new palsy after reduction mandates imaging to exclude an entrapped fragment.
  • Anterior dislocations are less common, associated with femoral head impaction/indentation and chondral injury, with a lower rate of sciatic injury but the same urgency.

Tibial pilon fracture

Staged management, approach selection and soft-tissue complications.

4 of 10 sittings2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x2)

  • The pilon is a soft-tissue injury with an associated fracture — that framing dictates everything. Immediate ORIF through swollen tissue historically produced wound breakdown and infection approaching 40%.
  • The staged protocol (Sirkin/Patterson):
    • Stage 1 — spanning external fixation within 24 hours (± fibular ORIF for length/alignment), with CT obtained after the fixator is applied (far more useful once length is restored).
    • Stage 2 — definitive ORIF at 7–21 days, once swelling resolves and the skin wrinkles return.
  • Measures that reduce soft-tissue complications:
    • Staged/delayed definitive fixation; spanning external fixation; careful incision planning with adequate skin bridges; minimally invasive techniques; elevation.
    • Early definitive (immediate) ORIF is the intervention that INCREASES complications — the classic "all reduce complications EXCEPT".
  • Skin bridges: the historical minimum is 7 cm between incisions. Combining an anteromedial tibial incision with a direct lateral fibular incision creates the narrowest bridge and the highest rate of skin necrosis.
  • Anterolateral approach — the superficial peroneal nerve limits proximal extension (it emerges from the fascia ~10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the lateral malleolus).
  • Rüedi & Allgöwer principles for the articular reconstruction:
    • (1) Restore fibular length and alignment; (2) reconstruct the tibial articular surface; (3) bone graft the metaphyseal defect; (4) buttress the medial tibia.
    • Modern practice modifies this with staged treatment and locked plating.
  • High-energy open pilon: cannot be treated with immediate definitive internal fixation. After external fixation the options are delayed ORIF, definitive ring fixator (Ilizarov), or primary ankle arthrodesis for unreconstructible articular damage.
  • Locked vs non-locked plates distally — compute rates, not raw numbers: 5/15 vs 5/20 = 33% vs 25%, so the locked group had the higher hardware-removal rate (bulkier, prominent over subcutaneous bone).
  • Outcomes: post-traumatic ankle arthritis is common even after a perfect reduction; counsel that recovery takes 1–2 years and return to full pre-injury function is uncommon.
  • Imaging in pregnancy: a single extremity radiograph delivers a negligible fetal dose (<0.01 mGy); no single diagnostic study approaches the 50 mGy threshold — obtain necessary imaging, shielding where it does not obscure the field.

Tibial plateau fracture

Schatzker classification, reduction parameters, approaches and associated injuries.

7 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2019, 2020, 2021 (x2), 2022, 2024F, 2025

  • Schatzker classification (a Canadian/Toronto contribution):
    • I lateral split; II lateral split-depression; III pure lateral depression.
    • IV medial plateau — the highest-energy pattern despite the low number.

    • Associated cruciate and collateral injury, peroneal nerve injury and popliteal artery injury.
    • Palpable pulses do not exclude an intimal injury — obtain ABIs and serial vascular examination.
  • Which structures are NOT injured — read the imaging description carefully:
    • In a medial fracture-dislocation with lateral depression, the medial joint line and MCL are characteristically pristine.
    • The medial plateau fails in compression while the lateral side fails in tension → it is the LCL/posterolateral corner and cruciates that tear.
  • Parameters for an acceptable reduction:
    • Articular step-off <2 mm (tolerance debated; up to 4–5 mm of depression may be accepted in low-demand patients).
    • Mechanical axis/coronal alignment — the most important determinant of long-term outcome, a stronger predictor than articular step-off. A knee left in varus does poorly regardless of a perfect articular reduction.
    • Condylar width and tibial slope (sagittal alignment).
    • Restoring fibular length is NOT a parameter of tibial plateau reduction.
  • Lateral plating biomechanics: for a split-depression fracture, subchondral "raft" screws immediately beneath the elevated surface, supported by a lateral locking plate, best resist re-depression — the number and position of the raft screws matters more than the plate. Calcium phosphate cement (higher compressive strength than calcium sulfate) reduces subsidence.
  • Posteromedial fragments are a marker of instability and cannot be controlled from a lateral plate — they need a posteromedial buttress plate via the Lobenhoffer approach (prone or floppy lateral).
  • Posterior approach gives direct access to posterior column fragments; the popliteal neurovascular bundle is protected by retracting the medial head of gastrocnemius laterally — the answer for a posterior shear fragment.
  • Soft-tissue management & complications:
    • High-energy bicondylar (Schatzker V/VI) → staged: spanning external fixation, definitive fixation at 7–14 days (same principle as the pilon).
    • Actively exclude compartment syndrome in Schatzker IV, V and VI — a dislocation-equivalent with vascular injury and reperfusion is the highest-risk scenario.
    • Intrawound vancomycin powder reduces deep gram-positive infection but shifts flora toward gram-negatives.

  • Function: the patella is a sesamoid within the extensor mechanism that increases the quadriceps moment arm, improving extensor efficiency by ~30%. Preserve it — total patellectomy reduces extension strength by 30–50%.
  • Treatment by pattern:
    • Non-displaced (<2 mm step, <3 mm separation) with an intact extensor mechanism (can straight-leg raise) → brace/cylinder cast in extension, early weightbearing.
    • Displaced, or an incompetent extensor mechanism → ORIF.
  • Modified anterior tension-band wiring:
    • Converts the quadriceps tensile force into compression at the articular surface during flexion.
    • Requires a simple transverse pattern with an intact opposite cortex.
    • The dominant complication is symptomatic hardware — reoperation/removal in 30–60%. This is the single most important practical fact.
  • The modern shift:
    • Cannulated screws with a tension band through the screws — biomechanically superior to K-wires with wire.
    • Suture-based tension bands (heavy non-absorbable suture instead of metal) — markedly less hardware irritation, comparable strength.
    • Plate fixation (mesh or low-profile anterior plates) for comminuted patterns — better biomechanics and lower removal rates.
  • Comminuted fractures are NOT suitable for a simple tension band — they collapse. Use a plate, or interfragmentary screws plus a tension band, or a partial patellectomy for an unreconstructible pole.
  • Partial patellectomy technique: reattach the tendon close to the articular surface — attaching it anteriorly tilts the remaining fragment and causes articular incongruity and anterior knee pain.
  • Outcomes: even after anatomic reduction, patellofemoral arthritis, anterior knee pain, stiffness and quadriceps weakness are common. Aim for <2 mm articular reduction, assessed directly (small arthrotomy or skyline fluoroscopy).
  • Periprosthetic patella fracture after TKA:
    • Most are treated non-operatively if the extensor mechanism is intact and the component well fixed — surgical outcomes are poor with high complication rates.
    • Surgery is reserved for extensor mechanism disruption or a loose patellar component (usually component removal and patelloplasty rather than revision).
  • Rehabilitation emphasises early protected range of motion (stiffness is a major cause of disability); weightbearing in full extension in a brace is generally permitted immediately.

Fat embolism syndrome & ARDS

Pathophysiology, diagnosis, and the interventions that actually reduce embolic load.

4 of 10 sittings2018, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2024F

  • Fat embolism syndrome presents 24–72 hours after a long bone fracture — the classic triad:
    • Hypoxaemia / respiratory distress.
    • Neurologic dysfunction (confusion, agitation, coma).
    • Petechial rash (axillae, conjunctivae, chest, neck) — the most specific but least common sign, in ~20–50%.
    • Supportive: thrombocytopenia, anaemia, fever, tachycardia (Gurd and Wilson criteria).
  • Risk factors:
    • Long bone fractures (femur above all), multiple fractures, bilateral femur fractures.
    • Closed fractures carry a higher risk than open — the intramedullary pressure is contained.
    • Young patients with high-pressure marrow; reamed intramedullary nailing.
  • What actually drives ARDS in a polytrauma patient with a femur fracture:
    • Severity of the associated chest injury (pulmonary contusion) is the strongest factor, then shock/hypoperfusion and massive transfusion.
    • Prolonged/reamed nailing in an under-resuscitated patient (the "second hit").
    • It is the chest injury and degree of shock — not the femur fracture in isolation.
  • Reducing the embolic load during nailing — the three commonly cited methods:
    • Vent the medullary canal (distal vent hole to release intramedullary pressure).
    • Smaller-diameter, sharp reamer heads with slow, gentle advancement.
    • Irrigate/lavage the canal; avoid a tight reamer-to-nail fit; use an unreamed or smaller nail in the compromised patient.
  • Early fracture stabilisation reduces FES and ARDS compared with prolonged traction — a foundational argument for early fixation, provided the patient is resuscitated.
  • Treatment is supportive:
    • Oxygen, ventilatory support (including PEEP), haemodynamic support, correction of hypovolaemia.
    • Corticosteroids may have a prophylactic role but are not established treatment; heparin and alcohol infusions are historical.
    • Most recover fully; mortality ~5–15%.
  • Differential:
    • Pulmonary embolism — usually later, normal neurologic exam, no rash.
    • Cerebral fat embolism — unexplained decline in consciousness with a normal head CT; MRI shows a "starfield" pattern on diffusion-weighted imaging.

Vascular injury & limb ischaemia in trauma

Hard and soft signs, the ABI, and the sequence of repair.

4 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2020 (x2), 2021, 2022

  • Hard signs — mandate immediate exploration (or on-table angiography):
    • Pulsatile bleeding; expanding or pulsatile haematoma; palpable thrill or audible bruit.
    • Signs of ischaemia — the 6 Ps: pulselessness, pallor, paraesthesia, pain, paralysis, poikilothermia.
  • Ankle-brachial index (ABI) is the key screening tool in blunt injury:
    • ≥0.9 with a normal exam effectively excludes a surgically significant arterial injury → serial observation.
    • <0.9 mandates CT angiography or exploration.
    • Palpable pulses do NOT exclude an intimal injury, which may thrombose hours later — hence serial examination in knee dislocation and medial plateau fracture-dislocation.
  • Sequence in the ischaemic limb — restore perfusion first:
    • (1) Reduce the fracture/dislocation — this alone frequently restores flow; always reduce before declaring a vascular injury.
    • (2) If ischaemia persists, place a temporary intraluminal shunt.
    • (3) Skeletal stabilisation (usually external fixation).
    • (4) Definitive vascular repair. (5) Fasciotomy.
  • Damage control in the unstable polytrauma patient: laparotomy takes precedence for the life-threatening injury — shunt the limb, apply an external fixator, and return for definitive care once resuscitated.
  • Compartment syndrome after revascularisation is the rule, not the exception — perform prophylactic four-compartment fasciotomy when ischaemic time approaches or exceeds 4–6 hours.
  • Vessels that matter:
    • Popliteal artery — tethered at the adductor hiatus and soleal arch, hence vulnerable in knee dislocation (~20–30% injury rate).
    • Brachial artery — supracondylar humerus fractures, elbow dislocation.
    • Superficial femoral artery in the adductor canal; anterior/posterior tibial in the leg.
  • Anatomy check: a stab wound to the anteromedial thigh at the upper/middle third junction (adductor canal) endangers the superficial femoral artery, femoral vein and saphenous nerve — the sciatic nerve is posterior and least at risk.
  • Imaging: duplex ultrasound and CT angiography have largely replaced conventional angiography; on-table angiography when the patient is too unstable to travel.

Gunshot wounds & ballistic injury

Ballistics, wound severity and management.

3 of 10 sittings2020, 2023, 2024S

  • Kinetic energy = ½mv² — so velocity matters far more than mass:
    • High velocity >2000 ft/s (military/hunting rifles) vs low velocity <2000 ft/s (most handguns).
    • A close-range shotgun behaves as a high-energy injury — the whole mass is delivered as a unit.
  • Mechanisms of tissue injury:
    • Permanent cavity — tissue directly crushed by the projectile.
    • Temporary cavity — radial stretch that then collapses; its size is proportional to the energy transferred (the key concept).
    • Low-elasticity tissue (bone, liver, brain) is damaged most by cavitation.
  • What increases energy transfer: tumbling/yaw, fragmentation, deformation (hollow-point), a longer tissue path, higher velocity.
    • A through-and-through wound means the projectile retained energy and left the body — so LESS energy was transferred than if it came to rest inside.
  • Low-velocity gunshot fractures — the key management point:
    • Local wound care, tetanus prophylaxis, a short course (24–48 h) of antibiotics, and treat the fracture on its own merits.
    • They do NOT routinely require formal operative débridement — generally managed as closed fractures.
  • High-velocity injuries require formal, often serial, operative débridement, are treated as Gustilo III open fractures, and frequently need staged reconstruction.
  • Surgery regardless of velocity for:
    • Vascular injury; compartment syndrome; intra-articular bullet/fragment (lead synovitis and systemic lead toxicity — remove it).
    • Gross contamination; a transabdominal trajectory (bowel contamination); significant soft-tissue loss; neurologic compromise needing exploration.
  • Nerve injuries with low-velocity wounds are usually neurapraxias from the temporary cavity and mostly recover — observe with EMG at 6 weeks unless transection is known.
  • Documentation: describe wounds factually; do not attempt to designate entrance vs exit; preserve clothing and projectiles and record the chain of custody.

Trauma in pregnancy

Resuscitating two patients.

3 of 10 sittings2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • The fundamental principle: resuscitate the mother — the best treatment for the fetus is optimal maternal resuscitation. Maternal hypotension and hypoxia are the greatest fetal threats.
  • Physiologic changes that mislead you:
    • Plasma volume rises 40–50% (physiologic anaemia); heart rate rises 10–20 bpm; blood pressure falls in the second trimester.
    • She can lose 30–35% of blood volume before becoming hypotensive — the fetus is compromised long before the mother looks unstable (uterine vasoconstriction preserves maternal pressure at fetal expense).
    • Tachycardia and fetal distress are the early signs.
  • After ~20 weeks, tilt the patient 15–30° to the LEFT (or manually displace the uterus) to relieve aortocaval compression — supine positioning can drop cardiac output by 30%.
  • Imaging is NOT contraindicated — the most important point:
    • The threshold for deterministic fetal effects is 50 mGy, and no single diagnostic study approaches it.
    • Extremity radiograph <0.01 mGy; chest radiograph negligible; CT abdomen/pelvis ~25 mGy (still below threshold).
    • Necessary imaging should never be withheld.
  • Teratogenic risk by timing: greatest at 2–15 weeks (organogenesis); before implantation it is all-or-none; after 15 weeks the main risk is a small increase in childhood malignancy.
  • Obstetric involvement is mandatory:
    • Continuous cardiotocography for at least 4–6 hours after significant trauma (longer with contractions/abnormalities).
    • Assess for placental abruption — the commonest cause of fetal death after blunt trauma (contractions, bleeding, tender uterus, fetal distress), uterine rupture, preterm labour.
    • Kleihauer-Betke testing and Rh immunoglobulin for the Rh-negative mother.
  • Medications:
    • Avoid tetracyclines (teeth/bone), fluoroquinolones (cartilage), and relatively aminoglycosides (oto/nephrotoxicity).
    • Cephalosporins and penicillins are safe. Avoid NSAIDs in the third trimester (premature ductus closure). LMWH is safe; warfarin is teratogenic.
  • Surgery: do not delay necessary orthopaedic surgery. The second trimester is safest for elective procedures; urgent surgery proceeds at any stage with left lateral tilt, obstetric and anaesthetic input, and fetal monitoring. Perimortem caesarean within 4 minutes of maternal arrest after 23–24 weeks.

Morel-Lavallée lesion & soft-tissue degloving

The closed internal degloving injury.

2 of 10 sittings2023, 2024F

  • What it is: a closed internal degloving injury — shear separates subcutaneous fat and dermis from the deep fascia, disrupting perforating vessels and lymphatics and creating a cavity of haemolymph, blood, fat and necrotic debris, lined by a fibrous capsule once chronic.
  • Location:
    • Greater trochanter / proximal lateral thigh is the commonest (the classic answer).
    • Then flank/lumbodorsal, gluteal, knee (common in American football), scapular.
    • Strongly associated with pelvic and acetabular fractures (~10–30%).
  • Clinical features:
    • Fluctuance, soft-tissue swelling with skin hypermobility, contour deformity, ecchymosis.
    • A characteristic area of decreased cutaneous sensation over the lesion; overlying skin may be devitalised.
    • Frequently missed initially — a third or more are diagnosed late.
  • Imaging: MRI is the study of choice (Mellado types 1–6 by signal and capsule); ultrasound guides aspiration.
  • The critical point is infection risk:
    • Culture-positive in up to a third of cases.
    • Operating through or near an unrecognised lesion markedly raises deep infection after pelvic/acetabular fixation — identify and address it before or at the time of definitive fixation.
  • Management:
    • Small acute → compression dressings and observation; percutaneous aspiration if persistent.
    • Larger/persistent → percutaneous drainage with débridement and irrigation (brush/curette the fibrous lining), compression and a drain; or sclerodesis (doxycycline, talc, fibrin glue) for recurrence.
    • Chronic encapsulated → open débridement with capsule excision.
    • Do not simply aspirate and ignore — recurrence is high and the capsule prevents obliteration. Culture at every drainage; stage fixation if infected.
  • Once a capsule forms (beyond ~3 weeks) the lesion does not resolve spontaneously and can persist for months to years.

Segmental bone defect: Masquelet & bone transport

The induced membrane technique and alternatives for critical-sized defects.

3 of 10 sittings2017, 2020, 2025

  • Masquelet (induced membrane) is a two-stage technique:
    • Stage 1 — radical débridement of necrotic/infected bone and soft tissue, a PMMA cement spacer in the defect, stable fixation (usually external), and soft-tissue coverage.
    • Stage 2 at 6–8 weeks — carefully remove the spacer without damaging the membrane and fill the cavity with cancellous autograft.
  • The induced membrane is the biological engine:
    • A richly vascularised, synovium-like pseudo-membrane secreting VEGF, TGF-β1 and BMP-2.
    • Acts as a barrier preventing soft-tissue ingrowth and graft resorption, and provides a vascular bed.
    • Bioactivity peaks at ~4–8 weeks then declines — hence the timing of stage 2.
  • Antibiotic-loaded cement is used when infection is present or suspected; the spacer must fill the defect completely and overlap the bone ends.
  • Graft volume is the practical challenge:
    • The reamer-irrigator-aspirator (RIA) is the best single source — 40–90 mL, with growth-factor content comparable to iliac crest and less donor-site pain.
    • This is the answer when ~50 mL of graft is needed; bilateral iliac crest yields substantially less.
  • Strengths and limitations:
    • Works for very large defects (published series up to 25 cm), needs no microsurgical expertise, and tolerates infection better than a vascularised graft.
    • Requires two operations, a long time to union, graft consolidates as trabecular bone initially, and it does not correct limb-length discrepancy.
  • Alternatives for critical-sized defects:
    • Distraction osteogenesis / bone transport (Ilizarov) — restores bone and length with true cortical regenerate, but long frame time, docking-site nonunion and pin-site infection.
    • Vascularised free fibular transfer — living cortical bone that hypertrophies, ideal for defects >6 cm in the young, but needs microsurgery and has donor-site morbidity.
  • Prerequisites for any reconstruction: eradication of infection, a healthy vascularised soft-tissue envelope, stable fixation, and an optimised host (nutrition, smoking cessation, glycaemic control, vitamin D).

Femoral neck stress fracture

The high-risk stress fracture that must not be missed.

1 of 10 sittings2020

  • A high-risk stress fracture — missed or mismanaged it can progress to a complete displaced fracture with catastrophic AVN and nonunion in a young patient. Keep a high index of suspicion for any young, active patient with insidious groin or anterior thigh pain that worsens with activity.
  • Presentation and imaging:
    • Gradual groin pain worse with weightbearing and at the extremes of rotation; antalgic gait; positive log-roll.
    • Plain radiographs are normal in the early stages.
    • MRI is the investigation of choice (marrow oedema, low-signal fracture line); bone scan is sensitive but less specific.
  • The critical distinction — tension vs compression side:
    • Superolateral (tension) side is UNSTABLE — distraction forces, high risk of displacement → prophylactic percutaneous cannulated screw fixation regardless of size.
    • Inferomedial (compression) side is relatively stable — small lesions can be treated with strict protected weightbearing.
  • Within the compression-side group, fix if:
    • The lesion involves >50% of the neck width; the line progresses on serial imaging; there is an associated effusion; or symptoms fail to settle.
    • A ~35% inferomedial lesion with an effusion is the grey zone — strict non-weightbearing with close serial imaging and a low threshold to fix.
  • Any displaced femoral neck stress fracture is a surgical emergency — urgent anatomic reduction and fixation; AVN risk 20–40%.

    • Female athlete triad / RED-S — low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction, low BMD — the key association; it must be addressed or the fracture recurs.
    • Check vitamin D and calcium intake; consider DEXA and endocrine assessment; screen sensitively for eating disorders.
    • Also: rapid increase in training volume, change in surface/footwear, coxa vara, neck geometry, smoking, corticosteroids.
  • Return to activity is gradual over 3–6 months, guided by symptom resolution and imaging, with correction of training and metabolic contributors.

Forearm shaft fracture & Galeazzi

The forearm as a joint, and the Galeazzi fracture-dislocation.

3 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2019 (x2), 2021

  • Treat the adult forearm as a JOINT, not two long bones:
    • Rotation depends on precise restoration of radial bow, length and rotational alignment.
    • So displaced both-bone fractures need anatomic ORIF with 3.5 mm compression plating — union >95%; non-operative treatment loses rotation.
  • Restoring the radial bow is the key technical determinant of rotation — match the maximum bow and its location to the contralateral side; loss of bow correlates directly with loss of supination/pronation and grip strength.
  • Galeazzi = distal-third radius fracture + DRUJ disruption:
    • The "fracture of necessity" — non-operative treatment reliably fails in adults.
    • ORIF the radius first, then assess the DRUJ.
    • DRUJ instability is more likely with a fracture within ~7.5 cm of the distal articular surface.
    • Radiographic signs: ulnar styloid base fracture, DRUJ widening on AP, ulnar dislocation on a true lateral, radial shortening >5 mm.
  • Algorithm after ORIF of the radius:
    • Stable → no further treatment.
    • Reducible but unstable → immobilise in supination in a long-arm cast ~6 weeks (or pin the ulna to the radius).
    • Irreducible → open the joint — the block is usually an entrapped ECU tendon or the TFCC, repaired to the fovea.
  • Monteggia (the mirror injury) = proximal ulna fracture with radial head dislocation — anatomic reduction of the ulna reduces the radial head.
  • Isolated ulnar shaft ("nightstick") fracture:
    • Non-operative in a functional brace if distal two-thirds, angulated <10°, displaced <50% of shaft width.
    • More proximal, angulated or displaced — or any suspicion of a Monteggia variant — needs ORIF.
  • Complications of forearm ORIF:
    • Synostosis — higher with a single incision for both bones, high energy, head injury, graft in the interosseous space, delayed surgery → use two separate incisions.
    • Refracture after plate removal — why routine removal is discouraged; protect the arm for months if removed.
    • Nerve injury (PIN with the Thompson/dorsal approach); compartment syndrome.
  • Essex-Lopresti — radial head fracture + interosseous membrane disruption + DRUJ injury (longitudinal instability). Never excise a radial head without examining the wrist.

Geriatric fracture care & orthogeriatrics

The system of care that determines outcome in the elderly hip fracture patient.

1 of 10 sittings2023

  • A hip fracture is a medical emergency with a surgical component:
    • One-year mortality ~20–30%; only about half of survivors return to their pre-fracture function.
    • The system of care matters as much as the operation.
  • AAOS CPG (2021) — the strong recommendations:
    • Surgery within 24–48 hours improves outcomes (mortality, pain, delirium, length of stay).
    • Regional and general anaesthesia are equivalent for mortality (REGAIN, RAGA).
    • A restrictive transfusion threshold (~80 g/L, or symptoms) is as safe as liberal (FOCUS).
    • Multimodal analgesia including a regional nerve block.
  • Orthogeriatric co-management is the single most impactful system intervention — shared care with a geriatrician reduces delirium, medical complications and length of stay (and mortality in several studies), and improves osteoporosis treatment on discharge.
  • Delirium occurs in 20–50% and predicts mortality, institutionalisation and functional decline:
    • Prevention beats treatment — early surgery, adequate analgesia, early mobilisation, hydration, oxygenation, sensory aids.
    • Untreated pain causes delirium — opioid sparing is desirable but under-treated pain is worse.
    • Avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics.
  • Do not delay surgery to "optimise" a patient who cannot be meaningfully optimised:
    • Correct genuinely reversible problems (severe anaemia, electrolyte derangement, uncontrolled heart failure, new arrhythmia).
    • A chronic comorbidity is not a reason to delay; nor is clopidogrel.
  • Weightbearing as tolerated is the standard after fixation or arthroplasty — restricted weightbearing is poorly complied with, causes deconditioning, and does not improve outcomes.
  • Secondary fracture prevention is the most commonly omitted element:
    • Every fragility fracture needs calcium and vitamin D, BMD assessment, fracture-risk assessment and pharmacologic therapy.
    • Fracture liaison services close this gap and are cost-effective — without one, fewer than 20% of patients are treated.
  • Also address: falls assessment (medication review, vision, home hazards, gait/balance), nutrition, and advance care planning — a hip fracture is often a sentinel event marking a change in trajectory.

Mangled extremity & limb salvage

Scoring systems, the LEAP findings, and how to make the decision.

2 of 10 sittings2019 (x2), 2024S

  • MESS (Mangled Extremity Severity Score) has four components:
    • Skeletal/soft-tissue injury severity; limb ischaemia (doubled if >6 hours); shock; patient age.
    • A score ≥7 was originally reported to predict amputation.
  • The critical modern point: scoring systems have poor positive predictive value and must NOT decide amputation:
    • LEAP specifically validated this — MESS, PSI, LSI, NISSSA and HFS-97 all failed to reliably predict the need for amputation or eventual function.
    • They retain some value in their negative predictive value (a low score suggests salvage is reasonable).
  • The LEAP finding that reframed the field:
    • At 2 and 7 years, functional outcomes (SIP) were EQUIVALENT between reconstruction and amputation.
    • Outcomes were predicted by psychosocial factors — self-efficacy, education, income, social support, smoking — not by the treatment chosen.
    • Both groups did poorly overall, with roughly half not returning to work.
  • Insensate plantar skin is NOT an absolute indication for amputation — a major LEAP finding:
    • More than half regained sensation by 2 years, and outcomes matched the insensate amputation group.
    • The historical teaching that an insensate plantar foot mandates amputation is wrong.
  • Genuine indications favouring primary amputation:
    • Unreconstructible vascular injury with prolonged warm ischaemia (>6–8 hours) and a non-viable limb.
    • Massive unreconstructible bone and soft-tissue loss.
    • A physiologically unstable patient in whom salvage would be life-threatening (life over limb); severe associated injuries.
  • Factors favouring salvage: young patient, upper limb injury (always attempt salvage — even a poorly functioning sensate limb is valuable), preserved sensation, viable vascular supply, reconstructible bone and soft tissue.
  • Counsel on the whole trajectory: salvage means multiple operations, prolonged non-weightbearing, high complication and reoperation rates, and a real chance of eventual amputation anyway — and late amputation after failed salvage does worse than primary amputation.
  • Process: decide with two experienced surgeons where possible, document carefully, and — when the patient cannot consent and the limb is not immediately life-threatening — defer using temporary shunting and external fixation to buy time.

Nonunion: Diagnosis & management

Classification, the diamond concept, and the systematic work-up.

2 of 10 sittings2019, 2023

  • Definition: a fracture that has failed to unite with no radiographic progression over three consecutive months, generally at 6–9 months (the FDA definition is 9 months with 3 months of no progression — a guide, not a rule).
  • Classification by biology (Weber-Čech):
    • Hypertrophic ("elephant foot"/"horse hoof") — abundant callus, good biology but inadequate mechanical stability → improve stability (compression plating, exchange nailing) WITHOUT bone graft.
    • Atrophic — no callus, poor vascularity/biology → débride fibrous tissue and sclerotic bone ends, bone graft, AND stabilise.
  • The "diamond concept" for assessing every nonunion: (1) osteogenic cells, (2) an osteoconductive scaffold, (3) osteoinductive growth factors, (4) mechanical stability — plus vascularity and the host.
  • Systematic work-up — the checklist:
    • (a) Exclude infection — history, examination, ESR/CRP/WBC, and intra-operative cultures and histology at revision (a proportion of "aseptic" nonunions grow organisms).
    • (b) Mechanics — is the fixation stable? Is there a gap? Is there malalignment?
    • (c) Biology — vascularity, soft-tissue envelope, bone loss.
    • (d) Host — smoking (the single biggest modifiable factor), diabetes, nutrition, vitamin D, NSAIDs, endocrine disease.
  • Treatment principle: fix what is deficient.
    • Hypertrophic with good alignment → compression (plate or exchange nail).
    • Atrophic → débride to bleeding bone, autologous cancellous graft (iliac crest or RIA), stable fixation.
    • Large segmental defect → Masquelet, bone transport or vascularised graft.
    • Infected nonunion → eradicate infection first (débridement, antibiotic-loaded cement, staged reconstruction).
  • Exchange nailing is highly effective for a hypertrophic diaphyseal tibial or femoral nonunion with an intact soft-tissue envelope — reaming grafts locally and allows a larger, stiffer nail. Less effective in atrophic nonunions, with a gap or bone loss, and in the distal tibia.
  • Adjuncts:
    • BMP-2/BMP-7 (osteoinductive, expensive, off-label concerns); bone marrow aspirate concentrate; teriparatide (encouraging in atypical femoral fractures).
    • LIPUS — the TRUST trial (Canadian-led, BMJ 2016) found NO benefit in tibial fracture healing, substantially weakening the case for routine use.
  • Correct malalignment at revision — a nonunion in varus that is simply grafted and re-plated in varus will fail again.

Psychosocial outcomes & recovery after injury

Why psychosocial factors dominate long-term outcome after major trauma.

2 of 10 sittings2019, 2024F

  • The central LEAP finding: after severe lower extremity trauma, outcomes at 2 and 7 years were determined far more by psychosocial factors than by injury characteristics or by salvage vs amputation.
    • Dominant predictors: low self-efficacy, low educational attainment, poverty/low income, lack of insurance, smoking, poor social support, and involvement in litigation/compensation.
  • Depression and PTSD are extremely common:
    • ~20–50% screen positive in the first year after major orthopaedic trauma.
    • Strongly associated with persistent pain, poorer function, delayed return to work and lower satisfaction — independent of injury severity.
  • Injuries with the highest rate of psychological distress:
    • High-energy lower extremity injuries — tibial pilon, calcaneus, open tibia, severe foot injuries.
    • Injuries needing prolonged non-weightbearing or multiple operations.
    • Calcaneal and pilon fractures are classically associated with prolonged disability, chronic pain, failure to return to work and depression.
  • Pain catastrophising and low self-efficacy are the strongest modifiable psychological predictors of persistent pain and disability — more predictive than objective injury severity. Screen with PHQ-9, PCL-5, the Pain Catastrophizing Scale.
  • Persistent opioid use after orthopaedic surgery:
    • The strongest predictor is pre-operative opioid use, then mental health comorbidity (depression, anxiety, PTSD), substance use disorder, and a larger initial prescription.
    • Injury severity and pain scores are weaker predictors than psychosocial history — an important reframing.
  • Practical implications:
    • Screen for depression, PTSD and substance use; set realistic expectations early and repeatedly.
    • Treat pain multimodally with opioid stewardship; involve social work, psychology and vocational rehabilitation.
    • Address smoking, nutrition and social support as part of the fracture care plan — early psychological intervention improves outcomes.
  • Counsel honestly about the expected 1–2 year recovery trajectory and the likelihood of some permanent limitation — this improves satisfaction more than optimistic reassurance that is later contradicted.
