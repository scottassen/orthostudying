Talus fracture & avascular necrosis

The vascular supply, the Hawkins classification and sign, and what actually drives AVN.

5 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022 (x3), 2023, 2024S, 2025 (x2)

  • Blood supply is the whole story — the talus has no muscular attachments and ~60% cartilage cover, so perfusion enters through one narrow non-articular corridor:
    • Artery of the tarsal canal (from the posterior tibial artery) — the dominant supply to the talar body.
    • Deltoid branch of the posterior tibial artery — the critical remaining supply after a displaced neck fracture; never strip the deltoid ligament medially, even during a medial malleolar osteotomy.
    • Artery of the sinus tarsi (peroneal + dorsalis pedis) — anastomoses with the tarsal canal and feeds the inferior neck.
    • Dorsalis pedis — feeds the head and neck dorsally.
  • Hawkins classification — predicts AVN by the displacement at injury (see figure):
    • Type I non-displaced → AVN ~0–15%.
    • Type II subtalar subluxation/dislocation → ~20–50%.
    • Type III subtalar and tibiotalar dislocation → ~50–100%.
    • Type IV III plus talonavicular dislocation → ~100%.
    • The determinant of AVN is the energy/displacement at the moment of injury — not the time to surgery.
  • Timing — reduce urgently, fix electively:
    • Urgent ORIF does not reduce AVN — the vascular insult happens at impact (Vallier overturned the emergent-fixation dogma).
    • What is urgent: reduction of any dislocation to relieve skin/neurovascular pressure.
    • Definitive fixation can wait for soft-tissue readiness (wrinkle sign).
  • The Hawkins sign is a good prognostic sign:
    • A subchondral lucency in the talar dome on the AP ankle film at 6–8 weeks.
    • It requires intact perfusion to form, so its presence indicates viability; its absence suggests AVN.
    • MRI is definitive — use titanium implants so it can be obtained.
  • Surgical principles:
    • Dual anteromedial + anterolateral approaches to judge and prevent the classic varus/dorsal malreduction (comminution is dorsomedial).
    • Medial malleolar osteotomy for body exposure — protect the deltoid.
    • Fixation: posterior-to-anterior lag screws are biomechanically superior; mini-fragment plates prevent shortening in comminuted necks.
    • Canale view (max equinus, 15° pronation, tube 75° cephalad) profiles the neck for varus.
  • Related patterns worth knowing:
    • Lateral process ("snowboarder's") fracture — the most commonly missed talus fracture.
    • Posterior process / os trigonum — posterior ankle impingement.
    • Subtalar dislocation: medial (~85%, inversion) is blocked by the EDB/talonavicular capsule; the less common lateral dislocation is blocked by the posterior tibialis tendon (worse prognosis) — reduce urgently, then CT.
  • Complications:
    • Post-traumatic subtalar arthritis — the most common long-term problem (more frequent than AVN overall).
    • Varus malunion (from missed dorsomedial comminution).
    • Skin necrosis/infection in open injuries (~15–25% are open).

Ankle arthroscopy & impingement

Portal safety, the nerve at risk, and the anterior/posterior impingement syndromes.

5 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022, 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025

  • Anteromedial portal — just medial to tibialis anterior at the joint line; structure at risk is the saphenous nerve and greater saphenous vein. It is made first, under direct vision.
  • Anterolateral portal — just lateral to peroneus tertius; structure at risk is the superficial peroneal nerve (intermediate dorsal cutaneous branch), identified pre-op by plantarflexing and inverting the foot, and placed by transillumination/needle localisation. The superficial peroneal nerve is the most commonly injured structure in ankle arthroscopy overall.
  • Accessory posterolateral portal — lateral to the Achilles at the fibular tip; risk is the sural nerve and small saphenous vein.
  • Posterior (prone) two-portal hindfoot arthroscopy (van Dijk) accesses the os trigonum, FHL and subtalar joint; the structure at greatest risk is the tibial nerve/posterior neurovascular bundle — stay lateral to the FHL, the medial safety landmark.
  • Posterior ankle impingement — pain in forced plantarflexion in a dancer/footballer, from an os trigonum or prominent Stieda process, often with FHL tenosynovitis (the FHL runs between the posteromedial and posterolateral talar tubercles). Treat with rest/injection, then posterior arthroscopic excision + FHL release.
  • Anterior ankle impingement — anterior pain with dorsiflexion from anterior tibial/talar osteophytes ("footballer's ankle"), soft-tissue/synovial impingement, and anterolateral (Bassett ligament/meniscoid) impingement after recurrent sprains. Gastrocsoleus contracture/equinus is NOT a cause — it limits dorsiflexion but does not create the bony lesion (the classic "which is not a cause" answer).
  • Traction improves posterior access but raises neurologic risk; most anterior work is done in dorsiflexion without traction. Non-invasive distraction is preferred over pin/invasive distraction.
  • Overall arthroscopy complication rate is ~9–10%, dominated by neurologic (SPN) injury; the rate falls with the dorsiflexion/no-traction technique.

Calcaneus fracture: Approaches & outcomes

Sanders, the extensile-lateral vs sinus-tarsi debate, and the Canadian operative-vs-nonoperative evidence.

4 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2025 (x2)

  • Sanders classification counts fracture lines through the posterior facet on a coronal CT: I non-displaced; II two-part; III three-part; IV four-part/comminuted. The number of posterior-facet fragments is the strongest predictor of outcome and of later subtalar fusion.
  • Radiographic angles: Böhler's angle (normal 20–40°; decreases with depression) and the critical angle of Gissane (normal ~120–145°; increases). Add Broden's view (posterior facet), Harris axial (width/varus/length) and the Saltzman hindfoot-alignment view. The superomedial "constant" (sustentacular) fragment stays attached to the deltoid/talus and anchors reduction. Essex-Lopresti splits tongue-type vs joint-depression.
  • Goals of ORIF: restore posterior-facet congruity, height (Böhler), length and width (decompress the lateral wall to relieve peroneal impingement), and correct hindfoot varus. Articular reduction of the posterior facet is the primary goal.
  • Buckley's multicentre Canadian RCT found no overall difference operative vs non-operative for displaced intra-articular fractures, but identified subgroups who benefit from surgery: women, younger patients, not on workers' compensation, Böhler 0–14°, light workload, simple patterns; non-operatively-treated patients were ~5.5× more likely to need late subtalar fusion.
  • Approach trade-off (the tested "consequence"): the extensile lateral approach gives the best articular visualisation but a wound-complication rate of 10–30% (smokers, diabetics, open injuries, early surgery); the sinus-tarsi (limited) approach markedly lowers wound problems and allows earlier surgery at the cost of less articular visualisation and less correction of severe comminution/lateral-wall widening. Functional outcomes are comparable for Sanders II–III.
  • Timing: wait for the wrinkle sign (~10–21 days) before an extensile approach; never operate through blistered/swollen skin. Sinus-tarsi allows earlier surgery.
  • Most common problem after non-operative treatment = post-traumatic subtalar arthritis needing fusion, plus loss of heel height (anterior ankle impingement), heel widening (peroneal impingement) and varus. In-situ fusion of a malunion is harder and worse than fusion after anatomic ORIF; a distraction bone-block fusion may be needed to restore height.
  • Anterior process fractures >25% of the calcaneocuboid joint and displaced tuberosity ("beak", Achilles pull, skin at risk) are operative; tongue-type fractures can threaten posterior skin and warrant urgent reduction.

Hallux valgus: Assessment & osteotomy selection

Radiographic parameters, choosing the osteotomy by IMA, and the complications of correction.

6 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S, 2025

  • Weight-bearing AP parameters: HVA normal <15°; IMA normal <9°; DMAA normal <10°; plus MTP congruency and sesamoid position. Severity: mild HVA <20°/IMA <11°; moderate HVA 20–40°/IMA 11–16°; severe HVA >40°/IMA >16°.
  • Procedure selection by IMA: distal osteotomy (chevron/Austin) for mild–moderate (IMA <13–15°); proximal or diaphyseal osteotomy (proximal crescentic/chevron, scarf, Ludloff) for larger IMA; Lapidus (first TMT fusion) for first-ray hypermobility, TMT arthritis or recurrence; first-MTP arthrodesis for severe deformity with arthritis, inflammatory arthritis or a neuromuscular foot. A distal soft-tissue (modified McBride) procedure is added to bony correction.
  • The modified McBride now spares the fibular sesamoid (excision predisposes to hallux varus): lateral release + medial eminence resection (2–3 mm medial to the sagittal sulcus) + medial capsular plication.
  • An increased DMAA (congruent joint) requires a biplanar/redirectional osteotomy (e.g. medial closing-wedge or double osteotomy); correcting IMA/HVA alone through a congruent joint creates incongruence and recurrence.
  • Troughing is the classic complication of the scarf osteotomy — the dorsal fragment sinks into the plantar fragment, losing correction and elevating/shortening the first ray. Chevron risks AVN of the head with an aggressive lateral release; proximal osteotomies risk dorsal malunion/transfer metatarsalgia.
  • Iatrogenic hallux varus is most often caused by over-aggressive lateral release with fibular sesamoid excision/over-correction of the IMA and over-resection of the medial eminence (violating the sagittal sulcus) with over-tight medial plication.
  • Risk factors: female sex, a positive family history (strongest), first-ray hypermobility, pes planus, ligamentous laxity, inflammatory/neuromuscular disease and constrictive footwear. Hallux rigidus is not a risk factor.
  • Recurrence correlates with under-correction of the IMA, an uncorrected DMAA/incongruent joint and failure to address first-ray hypermobility; residual sesamoid subluxation is a strong predictor.
  • Juvenile/adolescent HV: high familial incidence, frequently an increased DMAA with a congruent joint, associated pes planus/metatarsus adductus, and a recurrence rate up to 50% after surgery in the skeletally immature — delay surgery to maturity and operate for pain, not appearance.

Ankle fracture: Malleolar fixation & weightbearing

Classification, the mortise measurements, operative indications, and the early-weightbearing evidence.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Danis–Weber (fibula vs syndesmosis): A below, B at, C above. Lauge–Hansen pairs foot position + talar force: supination-adduction (Weber A, vertical medial malleolus + plafond impaction), supination-external-rotation (Weber B, the most common), pronation-abduction and pronation-external-rotation (Weber C).
  • Normal mortise: medial clear space ≤4 mm (and ≤ superior clear space), tibiofibular clear space <6 mm, tibiofibular overlap >6 mm (AP) / >1 mm (mortise), talocrural angle 83°±4°. A medial clear space >4–5 mm on a stress/gravity view signals deltoid incompetence (bimalleolar-equivalent).
  • Operative indications: displaced bi-/tri-malleolar fractures; a displaced lateral malleolus with an incompetent deltoid; displaced medial malleolus; syndesmotic disruption; posterior malleolus fragment >25% (or any size producing instability).
  • The isolated Weber B/SER lateral malleolus is the recurring judgement call: if the medial side is stable on a weight-bearing or external-rotation stress radiograph, treat non-operatively; medial tenderness alone over-predicts instability.
  • Posterior malleolus: fixing it (when it captures the PITFL) restores syndesmotic stability and buttresses the plafond — increasingly favoured over a trans-syndesmotic screw for the unstable posterior fragment.
  • Early weight-bearing is safe in stable fixation: Canadian and European trials show WBAT in a boot from ~2 weeks improves early function without raising complication or failure rates in well-fixed unstable ankle fractures — a shift away from 6 weeks non-weight-bearing.
  • Diabetics/neuropaths are the exception — prolonged protected weight-bearing, supplementary fixation and vigilance for Charcot; they have far higher wound and hardware-failure rates.
  • Fibular length and rotation (restore the "dime/talocrural" relationship) are the technical keys; a short/malrotated fibula is the commonest malreduction and drives post-traumatic arthritis.

Syndesmotic injury: Diagnosis, reduction & fixation

The ligaments, how to detect and reduce the syndesmosis, and screw vs suture-button.

4 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024F

  • Four ligaments: AITFL, PITFL (superficial + deep/transverse), interosseous ligament, plus the bony incisura. Contribution to stability: AITFL ~35%, deep PITFL ~33%, interosseous ~22%, superficial PITFL ~9% — so the PITFL complex (~42%) contributes most, and the posterior malleolus (its attachment) matters.
  • Mechanism = external rotation/hyperdorsiflexion. Suspect it in Weber C, high SER patterns, and the Maisonneuve (proximal fibula) injury — always image the proximal fibula and look for a widened medial clear space.
  • The tibiofibular clear space is the most reliable radiographic measure (least affected by rotation). Overlap and clear-space thresholds are less consistent.
  • Intra-operative tests: the hook/Cotton test (lateral pull; >2 mm translation is positive) and the external-rotation stress test; direct arthroscopic or open visualisation is the most accurate.
  • Malreduction is the enemy and is common: up to ~50% are malreduced on post-operative CT with clamp/screw techniques, and malreduction is the strongest predictor of poor outcome. Open/direct reduction and a post-op CT (or intra-op 3D) beat "feel." Avoid over-compression and place the clamp along the trans-malleolar axis.
  • Restore fibular length/rotation and fix a capturing posterior malleolus first — both dramatically improve syndesmotic reduction and stability.
  • Suture-button (dynamic) vs screw: randomized and comparative data (including Canadian work) show suture buttons give lower malreduction, fewer implant failures/removals and better early function, with equivalent or better outcomes; rigid screws remain reasonable, and routine removal is no longer mandated.

Ankle arthritis: Fusion vs total ankle arthroplasty

Aetiology, fusion position, TAA indications/contraindications, and the Canadian outcome data.

4 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2024F, 2025

  • ~70% of ankle arthritis is post-traumatic (vs mostly primary OA at the hip/knee) — often younger patients with malalignment, so correct deformity first.
  • Ankle fusion position: neutral dorsiflexion, ~5° hindfoot valgus, ~5–10° external rotation, with the talus slightly posteriorly translated. Malposition (equinus, varus) is poorly tolerated and drives midfoot/knee compensation.
  • The price of fusion is adjacent-joint arthritis — the subtalar joint (most common), then talonavicular — progressing over 5–10 years; this is the main argument for arthroplasty in the right patient.
  • TAA contraindications: active/prior deep infection, insensate foot, severe uncorrectable deformity, Charcot, talar osteonecrosis (avascular talus), poor soft tissues, neuromuscular disease and severe osteopenia. Younger, high-demand/heavy-labour patients favour fusion.
  • The most common intra-operative complication of TAA is medial malleolar fracture; aseptic loosening/subsidence is the leading cause of revision. Suspect loosening with >5°/5 mm component migration on serial films; work up pain with ESR/CRP, weight-bearing films, aspiration and CT for osteolysis.
  • Deformity around the ankle is corrected with a supramalleolar osteotomy when there is asymmetric varus/valgus OA with ≥50% joint preserved — it re-centres the talus and offloads the worn side (adjunct to, or delay of, fusion/TAA).
  • Ankle distraction arthroplasty (hinged external fixator) is a joint-preserving option for a young patient with a congruent joint and >20° of motion; pin-tract infection is the commonest complication and results are variable.
  • Modern comparative and Canadian prospective data (Daniels/CanadIAn cohorts) show TAA achieves function comparable to fusion with better gait and lower adjacent-joint arthritis, at the cost of higher reoperation for implant issues — shared decision-making by age, demand, deformity and bone quality.

Diabetic foot: Ulceration, Charcot & infection

The neuropathic triad, healing predictors, Charcot staging, and distinguishing Charcot from infection.

4 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2024F, 2025

  • Triad: sensory neuropathy (loss of protective sensation — a 5.07/10-g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament defines risk), autonomic neuropathy (dry, fissured skin) and ischaemia/PVD; deformity concentrates plantar pressure and drives ulceration.
  • Predictors of poor ulcer healing: ABI <0.45, transcutaneous oxygen <30 mmHg, albumin <3.0 g/dL, total lymphocyte count <1,500/mm³; glycaemic control (HbA1c) and smoking also matter. In calcified vessels the ABI is falsely elevated — use toe pressures/TcPO₂.
  • Foot ulceration is the single strongest risk factor for eventual amputation. Wagner grades 0 (at-risk) → 1 superficial → 2 to tendon/capsule → 3 deep abscess/osteomyelitis → 4 forefoot gangrene → 5 whole-foot gangrene.
  • A positive probe-to-bone test plus a plain-film or MRI signal strongly supports osteomyelitis; bone biopsy is the gold standard. An ESR >70 mm/hr raises the likelihood of underlying osteomyelitis.
  • Neuropathic (Charcot) arthropathy is most commonly diabetic. Acute Charcot is a hot, swollen foot with bounding pulses, whose erythema resolves on elevation (cellulitis does not) — the key clinical discriminator.
  • Eichenholtz stages: 0 (clinical, normal x-ray) → 1 fragmentation → 2 coalescence → 3 reconstruction. Brodsky anatomic pattern: type 1 tarsometatarsal (most common, "rocker-bottom") → 2 subtalar/Chopart → 3 ankle.
  • No single test separates Charcot from osteomyelitis; a combined bone scan + labelled-WBC scan (or WBC/marrow) is most useful (sensitivity ~90–100%, specificity ~80%); MRI helps but marrow oedema overlaps.
  • Management: acute Charcot → total-contact casting and offloading until quiescent; ulcers → debridement, offloading (TCC), culture-directed antibiotics and vascular assessment; reconstruction/exostectomy for recurrent ulceration over a bony prominence; amputation when limb-threatening or unsalvageable.

Lisfranc & midfoot injury

The keystone anatomy, why injuries are missed, and ORIF vs primary arthrodesis.

3 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2023, 2024F (x2)

  • Up to 20% of Lisfranc injuries are missed initially; a missed or malreduced injury → painful post-traumatic midfoot arthritis and arch collapse. Low-energy sprains in athletes are as easily missed as high-energy crush injuries.
  • The recessed base of the second metatarsal seated in the cuneiform mortise is the "keystone"; the Lisfranc ligament runs from the medial (first) cuneiform to the second metatarsal base (plantar band strongest) — there is no intermetatarsal ligament between MT1 and MT2, the weak link.
  • Radiographic signs: the "fleck sign" (avulsion in the first web space), loss of alignment of the second-MT medial border with the middle cuneiform, and first–second MT diastasis. Get weight-bearing/stress views or CT — non-weight-bearing films miss subtle instability; MRI shows the ligament.
  • Non-displaced, stable injuries (negative weight-bearing/stress views) → non-operative (boot, protected weight-bearing) with close follow-up.
  • Displaced/unstable injuries → anatomic reduction and stable fixation; the technical priority is anatomic reduction (the strongest outcome predictor), whether by transarticular screws, dorsal bridge plates or fusion.
  • Primary arthrodesis vs ORIF: for purely ligamentous Lisfranc injuries, primary arthrodesis (Ly & Coetzee RCT) gives better outcomes and fewer reoperations/hardware removals; bony (fracture-pattern) injuries do well with anatomic ORIF. Comminuted or delayed injuries favour fusion.

Achilles tendon rupture & tendinopathy

The Canadian functional-rehab evidence, chronic reconstruction, and tendinopathy.

4 of 7 sittings2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • Diagnosis: palpable gap, weak plantarflexion, a positive Thompson (Simmonds) squeeze test and an abnormal resting ankle angle (Matles test, prone knee flexion). Chronic lateral ankle instability & Broström repair

Ligament anatomy, anatomic repair vs reconstruction, and correcting hindfoot varus.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2023, 2024S, 2025

  • The ATFL is the weakest lateral ligament and the primary restraint to inversion in plantarflexion; the CFL restrains inversion in dorsiflexion and crosses both the ankle and subtalar joints. Most sprains injure the ATFL first, then the CFL.
  • Most acute sprains are managed non-operatively with functional rehabilitation and peroneal/proprioceptive training, which outperforms immobilisation and prevents most chronic instability.
  • First-line surgery for chronic instability = anatomic repair (Broström) with the Gould modification (imbricate the ATFL±CFL and reinforce with the inferior extensor retinaculum) — low-cost, low-complication, preserves subtalar motion.
  • Anatomic reconstruction with tendon graft (auto/allograft) is preferred over repair when there is poor-quality remnant tissue, failed prior repair, high BMI, generalized ligamentous laxity, or long-standing instability.
  • Non-anatomic tenodeses (Watson-Jones, Evans, Chrisman-Snook) are largely historical — they sacrifice peroneus brevis and stiffen the subtalar joint, causing late arthrosis.
  • Always identify and correct hindfoot varus — the commonest cause of failure. Use the Coleman block test: if varus corrects on the block, it is forefoot-driven/flexible → dorsiflexion first-metatarsal osteotomy; if it does not correct, it is fixed → lateralizing (Dwyer) calcaneal osteotomy.
  • Address associated pathology found at surgery/arthroscopy: peroneal tears, anterolateral impingement, loose bodies and osteochondral lesions of the talus.

Deltoid ligament injury

The two layers, its role in ankle-fracture stability, and when to repair.

3 of 7 sittings2023, 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • Two layers. The superficial deltoid (tibionavicular, tibiospring, tibiocalcaneal, superficial posterior tibiotalar) crosses ankle and subtalar joints and resists hindfoot eversion/abduction; the deep deltoid (anterior and posterior tibiotalar) is the primary restraint to lateral talar translation and external rotation and the key ankle stabiliser.
  • In ankle fractures the deltoid is the "medial malleolus equivalent." A Weber B/SER lateral malleolus with a competent deltoid is stable; deltoid incompetence makes it bimalleolar-equivalent and unstable.
  • A medial clear space >4–5 mm (and greater than the superior clear space) on a gravity/external-rotation stress or weight-bearing radiograph indicates deltoid incompetence — medial tenderness/ecchymosis alone over-predicts instability and should not drive the decision.
  • For the unstable rotational ankle fracture, anatomic fixation of the fibula (restoring length and rotation) usually restores the mortise; the deltoid heals without repair once the lateral column is reduced — routine deltoid repair is not required.
  • Persistent medial clear-space widening after fibular fixation means soft-tissue interposition (entrapped deltoid) blocking reduction → open the medial side, clear the interposed tissue, and repair the deltoid.
  • Selective acute deltoid repair is favoured in specific settings — supination-external-rotation with medial instability plus syndesmotic injury, some pronation injuries, and to protect a syndesmotic reduction — and deltoid reconstruction is part of managing valgus ankle deformity/end-stage flatfoot with lateral talar tilt.

Cavovarus foot & Charcot-Marie-Tooth

The cavovarus foot, the Coleman block test and CMT.

3 of 7 sittings2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Charcot-Marie-Tooth (hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy) is the commonest inherited neuropathy; type 1A (autosomal dominant, PMP22 duplication on chromosome 17) is the most frequent. The characteristic muscle imbalance: weak tibialis anterior with a relatively strong peroneus longus (plantarflexing the first ray) and weak peroneus brevis with a strong tibialis posterior (driving hindfoot varus and forefoot adduction), plus intrinsic weakness producing clawtoes.
  • The result is a cavovarus foot: plantarflexed first ray, forefoot pronation (valgus) relative to the hindfoot, hindfoot varus, clawtoes and a high arch, with recurrent ankle sprains and lateral column overload (fifth metatarsal stress fracture/base fracture, peroneal tears).
  • Radiographic findings of cavovarus: increased Meary angle (talus–first metatarsal, apex dorsal), increased calcaneal pitch, a 'bullet hole' sinus tarsi on the lateral, a positive Hibbs angle, and the 'double density'/overlapping talus and calcaneus on the AP. A decreased calcaneal pitch is a flatfoot finding, not cavovarus — the standard distractor.
  • Coleman block test is the key clinical discriminator: standing on a 1-inch block with the first ray hanging free, if the hindfoot varus corrects, the deformity is forefoot-driven (flexible) and is treated by addressing the plantarflexed first ray (dorsiflexion osteotomy of the first metatarsal) ± soft-tissue procedures. If it does not correct, the hindfoot is rigid and requires a lateralising/valgus-producing (Dwyer) calcaneal osteotomy, or arthrodesis in severe rigid deformity.
  • Comprehensive surgical correction typically combines: plantar fascia release, first metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy, calcaneal osteotomy, peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer (removes the deforming plantarflexion force on the first ray and augments eversion), posterior tibial tendon transfer to the dorsum for foot drop, Jones procedure for clawtoes, and lateral ligament reconstruction.
  • Bilateral, symmetric cavovarus in an adolescent should prompt a neurologic work-up (CMT, Friedreich ataxia, spinal dysraphism, tethered cord) — a unilateral cavovarus foot is a spinal cord lesion until proven otherwise (MRI of the neuraxis).

Peroneal tendon pathology

Tears, subluxation and the anatomic risk factors.

3 of 7 sittings2022, 2024F, 2025

  • Peroneus brevis lies anterior and deep to peroneus longus at the level of the fibula and inserts on the base of the fifth metatarsal; peroneus longus passes under the cuboid to insert on the plantar base of the first metatarsal and medial cuneiform (it plantarflexes the first ray — important in cavovarus). Peroneus brevis is the more commonly torn because it is compressed between the longus and the fibula.
  • Anatomic risk factors for peroneal tendon tears: a shallow or convex retromalleolar (fibular) groove, a low-lying peroneus brevis muscle belly, the presence of a peroneus quartus (accessory muscle, ~10–20%), a hypertrophied peroneal tubercle, hindfoot varus/cavovarus alignment, and lateral ligament insufficiency/chronic instability. Pes planus/hindfoot valgus is not a risk factor for brevis tears — it is the classic 'all except' distractor (though it can cause peroneal spasm/impingement in a different pattern).
  • Peroneal tendon subluxation/dislocation results from injury to the superior peroneal retinaculum, classically a forced dorsiflexion with sudden reflex contraction (skiing). Look for a 'fleck sign' — an avulsion of the posterolateral fibular rim. Acute injuries in athletes and chronic subluxation are treated surgically with SPR repair, with fibular groove deepening where the groove is shallow or convex.
  • Distinguish from a lateral ankle sprain: peroneal pathology causes pain and swelling posterior to the fibula (rather than anterior over the ATFL), pain with resisted eversion, and a snapping sensation. Dynamic ultrasound is excellent for demonstrating subluxation.
  • Treatment of tears: débridement and tubularisation if <50% of the tendon is involved; tenodesis to the adjacent intact tendon if >50%; and allograft/tendon transfer if both tendons are unusable. Always correct hindfoot varus with a lateralising calcaneal osteotomy at the same time or the repair will fail.
  • Chronic pain over the lateral hindfoot after a calcaneal fracture is frequently peroneal tendon impingement/subluxation from lateral wall blowout — treated with lateral wall exostectomy and tendon débridement.

Fifth metatarsal & Jones fracture

Zone anatomy, the Jones fracture and fixation pitfalls.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2024S

  • Zone 1 (tuberosity/avulsion) — an avulsion at the base from the lateral band of the plantar fascia and the peroneus brevis insertion, from an inversion injury. It heals reliably with symptomatic treatment (hard-soled shoe, weightbearing as tolerated). Zone 2 (Jones fracture) — at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction extending into the fourth–fifth intermetatarsal articulation, in a watershed vascular zone with a high rate of delayed union/nonunion. Zone 3 — proximal diaphyseal stress fracture, with the highest nonunion rate.
  • Fixation of a Jones fracture: use the largest-diameter partially threaded intramedullary screw the canal will accept (usually 4.5–6.5 mm) — under-sizing is the commonest technical error and predicts failure. Do not over-tap or use an excessively long screw: a screw that is too long straightens the lateral bow of the metatarsal and distracts/gaps the fracture at the lateral cortex, and can penetrate the diaphysis. The screw should be short enough to remain within the proximal metaphysis/diaphysis.
  • The starting point is 'high and inside' — at the very proximal tip of the base, slightly medial and dorsal, aligned with the axis of the metatarsal. Avoid the sural nerve and the peroneus brevis insertion.
  • Indications for acute fixation: the competitive athlete (faster union and return to sport), a displaced fracture, an established delayed union/nonunion, and the recreational athlete who prefers to avoid 8–12 weeks of non-weightbearing. Non-operative treatment is 6–8 weeks non-weightbearing in a cast with a union rate of roughly 75–85%.
  • Always assess hindfoot alignment: a cavovarus foot causes lateral column overload and is a major risk factor for a proximal fifth metatarsal stress fracture, nonunion and refracture. A Jones nonunion with correctable hindfoot varus on the Coleman block test is treated with screw fixation plus bone grafting AND correction of the varus (first metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy if forefoot-driven, or a lateralising calcaneal osteotomy if hindfoot-driven).
  • Also evaluate for vitamin D deficiency and metabolic bone disease in a stress fracture, and for relative energy deficiency in female athletes.

1 of 7 sittings2025

Gastrocnemius contracture & equinus

The Silfverskiöld test and the consequences of equinus.

1 of 7 sittings2021

  • Equinus is defined as <10° (some authors <5°) of ankle dorsiflexion with the knee extended and the hindfoot held in neutral/slight inversion (locking the midfoot so the motion measured is true ankle dorsiflexion, not midfoot sag).
  • Silfverskiöld test: measure dorsiflexion with the knee extended and then with the knee flexed to 90°. If dorsiflexion improves markedly with the knee flexed, the contracture is isolated to the gastrocnemius (which crosses the knee) → treat with a gastrocnemius recession (Strayer, Baumann or Vulpius). If dorsiflexion remains limited with the knee flexed, the soleus is also involved → tendo-Achilles lengthening is required.
  • Associations/consequences of a gastrocsoleus contracture: plantar fasciitis, metatarsalgia and forefoot overload, midfoot (Charcot/Lisfranc) collapse, diabetic forefoot ulceration, Achilles tendinopathy, progressive collapsing foot deformity, and anterior ankle pain — and it is a key contributor to recurrence after almost any foot reconstruction if not addressed. Posterior ankle impingement is not caused by equinus (it is caused by an os trigonum/Stieda process).
  • In cerebral palsy with equinus and toe walking, the same test guides treatment: an isolated gastrocnemius contracture (dorsiflexion −10° with the knee extended improving to 0° with the knee flexed) is treated with a gastrocnemius recession, whereas involvement of both heads requires a tendo-Achilles lengthening. Over-lengthening the Achilles causes a calcaneal (crouch) gait, which is far worse and largely irreversible — the cardinal error in CP surgery.
  • Tendo-Achilles lengthening reduces forefoot plantar pressures and is an evidence-based adjunct in the treatment and prevention of recurrent diabetic forefoot ulceration.
  • Non-operative treatment (dedicated gastrocnemius stretching with the knee extended, night splints, serial casting) should be attempted first in most non-neurologic patients.

Hallux rigidus & first MTP arthritis

Grading and choosing between cheilectomy and fusion.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • Hallux rigidus is degenerative arthritis of the first MTP joint with characteristic dorsal osteophyte formation and progressive loss of dorsiflexion. Classic examination: dorsal joint-line tenderness with pain at the extremes of dorsiflexion but a relatively pain-free mid-range, and a negative or mildly positive grind test in early disease.
  • Coughlin & Shurnas grading: 0 — stiffness only; 1 — mild dorsal osteophyte, dorsiflexion 40–60°; 2 — dorsiflexion 30–40° with mild-moderate joint narrowing and pain only at terminal dorsiflexion; 3 — dorsiflexion <30°, substantial narrowing/osteophyte, with pain at terminal range but NOT mid-range; 4 — as grade 3 but with pain throughout the mid-range of motion.
  • Cheilectomy (dorsal osteophyte and 25–30% of the dorsal metatarsal head resection) is appropriate for grades 1–3 — where pain is at the extremes of motion and the plantar/mid-range articular cartilage is preserved. Mid-range pain (grade 4) means the whole joint surface is involved and cheilectomy will fail — this is the key discriminator.
  • First MTP arthrodesis is the gold-standard operation for grade 3–4 disease with mid-range pain: it is durable and reliably relieves pain. Fusion position: 10–15° of dorsiflexion relative to the ground (about 20–25° to the first metatarsal shaft), 10–15° of valgus and neutral rotation — the pulp of the toe should just clear the ground and allow footwear.
  • The stem describing a middle-aged patient with dorsal AND plantar (sesamoid) tenderness, 5° of dorsiflexion and 10° of plantarflexion who has failed injections and orthotics has grade 4 disease with mid-range/plantar pain → first MTP arthrodesis, not cheilectomy.
  • Alternatives: interpositional arthroplasty, synthetic cartilage implant (Cartiva — comparable to fusion in the pivotal trial but with mixed longer-term data), Moberg dorsiflexion osteotomy of the proximal phalanx (adjunct to cheilectomy in the young), and hemiarthroplasty (historically poor results).

Metatarsalgia, lesser toe deformity & Weil osteotomy

Treating transfer metatarsalgia and the Weil osteotomy's characteristic complication.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • Metatarsalgia results from abnormal load distribution across the lesser metatarsal heads — from a long or plantarflexed metatarsal, first-ray insufficiency (after hallux valgus surgery or in hallux rigidus), equinus contracture, claw/hammer toe with plantar plate attenuation, or a plantar fat pad atrophy.
  • The Weil osteotomy is an intra-articular, oblique, distal metatarsal shortening osteotomy that translates the metatarsal head proximally to offload it. The most common complication of a Weil osteotomy is 'floating toe' — the toe fails to touch the ground (loss of MTP plantarflexion/toe purchase), reported in up to 15–50% of cases.
  • The mechanism of floating toe is plantar translation of the metatarsal head axis relative to the intrinsic muscles: the interossei, whose line of pull now passes dorsal to the new (proximally translated) centre of rotation, become extensors rather than flexors of the MTP joint. Modifying the osteotomy by removing a dorsal slice of bone to prevent plantar displacement of the head reduces the incidence. Post-operative stiffness and transfer metatarsalgia to the adjacent ray are the other common problems.
  • Always address the plantar plate — attenuation/tear causes MTP instability with a positive drawer (Lachman) test of the MTP joint. Modern practice frequently repairs the plantar plate at the time of the Weil osteotomy.
  • Consider gastrocnemius recession where equinus contributes to forefoot overload — this is increasingly recognised as an important adjunct.
  • Non-operative treatment (metatarsal pads/bars placed proximal to the metatarsal head, stiff-soled rocker shoes, callus care and activity modification) should be exhausted first, and is often sufficient.

Plantar fasciitis & heel pain

Risk factors, natural history and treatment.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • Risk factors for plantar fasciitis: obesity/increased BMI (the strongest in the non-athletic population), limited ankle dorsiflexion/gastrocnemius-soleus contracture (the strongest modifiable factor), prolonged weightbearing occupations, pes planus and pes cavus, sudden increases in running volume, and inappropriate footwear. A calcaneal (plantar) heel spur is a radiographic association, not a cause — spurs occur in ~15–20% of asymptomatic people and lie within the flexor digitorum brevis origin, not the fascia; spur excision is not required.
  • Classic history: pain with the first steps in the morning or after rest, improving with a few minutes of walking, and worsening again at the end of the day. Tenderness at the medial calcaneal tuberosity, worse with passive dorsiflexion of the toes (windlass mechanism).
  • The natural history is favourable: >90% resolve within 10–12 months with non-operative treatment. First-line treatment is plantar fascia-specific stretching (Digiovanni protocol — dorsiflexing the toes) plus gastrocnemius/Achilles stretching, which outperforms generic Achilles stretching alone; add heel cups/orthoses, activity modification, weight loss and night splints.
  • Second-line: corticosteroid injection (short-term relief only, with a small risk of plantar fascia rupture and fat-pad atrophy), extracorporeal shockwave therapy, PRP (evidence mixed). Surgery — partial (medial one third) plantar fascia release ± gastrocnemius recession — is reserved for symptoms lasting >9–12 months. Complete fascia release causes lateral column overload and arch collapse.
  • Isolated gastrocnemius recession has emerging evidence as an effective operation for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis with an equinus contracture.
  • Differential diagnosis: Baxter's neuritis (entrapment of the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve — pain more medial and proximal, no morning-start pattern, occasional ADM atrophy), calcaneal stress fracture (positive squeeze test, night pain, MRI), fat pad atrophy, tarsal tunnel syndrome, and S1 radiculopathy.

Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction & adult flatfoot

Progressive collapsing foot deformity: staging and treatment.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • Johnson & Strom staging (modified by Myerson), now increasingly described as progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD): Stage I — tenosynovitis with a normally aligned foot, intact single-leg heel rise; Stage II — flexible deformity with hindfoot valgus, forefoot abduction ('too many toes' sign) and inability to perform a single-leg heel rise; Stage III — rigid hindfoot valgus with subtalar arthritis; Stage IV — valgus talar tilt at the ankle from deltoid ligament failure.
  • The posterior tibial tendon is the dynamic stabiliser of the medial longitudinal arch and the primary invertor; its failure allows the spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament — the key static stabiliser — to attenuate. Both must be considered.
  • Treatment: Stage I — immobilisation/orthosis, NSAIDs, tenosynovectomy if refractory. Stage II — joint-preserving reconstruction: FDL transfer to the navicular + medialising calcaneal osteotomy ± lateral column lengthening (for forefoot abduction) ± Cotton osteotomy or first TMT fusion (for forefoot varus) ± gastrocnemius recession ± spring ligament repair. Stage III — hindfoot (triple or double) arthrodesis. Stage IV — deltoid reconstruction with hindfoot correction, or tibiotalocalcaneal fusion/TAA in selected cases.
  • Always assess and correct equinus — a gastrocnemius contracture is present in the majority and drives recurrence if untreated (Silfverskiöld test to distinguish isolated gastrocnemius from gastrocsoleus contracture).
  • A young athlete with a symptomatic posterior tibial tendon insertional avulsion/accessory navicular but normal, symmetric arches and full dorsiflexion does not have PCFD — treatment is excision of the accessory navicular with advancement/reattachment of the posterior tibial tendon (modified Kidner procedure), not a flatfoot reconstruction.
  • The too-many-toes sign (viewed from behind, more lateral toes visible because of forefoot abduction) and the single-limb heel rise (failure to invert the hindfoot) are the two key examination findings.

Puncture wound of the foot & Pseudomonas osteomyelitis

The classic nail-through-a-shoe injury.

1 of 7 sittings2024F

  • A puncture wound through the sole of a running/athletic shoe that develops infection is classically caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which colonises the moist foam rubber of the shoe insole and is inoculated directly into the deep tissues, including bone and cartilage. Staphylococcus aureus remains the commonest organism in puncture wounds overall, but the shoe-associated deep infection/osteomyelitis is Pseudomonas until proven otherwise.
  • Presentation: increasing pain and swelling 3–7 days after the puncture (in contrast to simple cellulitis, which appears within 1–2 days), with purulent drainage and often a benign initial appearance.
  • Management: surgical débridement/exploration of the puncture tract with removal of the foreign body (shoe/sock material), plus antipseudomonal antibiotic coverage — ciprofloxacin (in adults) or an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime). Tetracycline, cephalexin (Keflex) and clindamycin do not cover Pseudomonas and are the standard distractors.
  • Imaging: radiographs for retained foreign body and later bony change; MRI is the study of choice for osteomyelitis and abscess. Deep puncture wounds over the metatarsal heads and calcaneus are the most likely to produce osteomyelitis or septic arthritis.
  • Tetanus prophylaxis must be addressed in every puncture wound. Prophylactic antibiotics for a simple, clean, superficial puncture are not routinely indicated in an immunocompetent patient; the diabetic and immunocompromised patient warrants a lower threshold.
  • Note the fluoroquinolone caveat in children (cartilage toxicity concerns) — ceftazidime or another antipseudomonal beta-lactam is generally preferred in the paediatric population, though ciprofloxacin is used when needed.

Supramalleolar osteotomy & distal tibial malunion

Realigning the distal tibia to offload asymmetric ankle arthritis.

1 of 7 sittings2023

  • A supramalleolar osteotomy (SMO) realigns the distal tibial articular surface to redistribute load in asymmetric (varus or valgus) ankle arthritis — most commonly after a malunited pilon, distal tibial or ankle fracture, or in primary varus ankle osteoarthritis.
  • The ideal candidate has: asymmetric/eccentric ankle arthritis with a preserved (congruent) portion of the joint to offload onto, a mobile ankle with a reasonable arc of motion, a correctable deformity, and adequate vascularity and soft tissue. It is a joint-preserving alternative to fusion or arthroplasty in a younger, active patient.
  • Contraindications/poor candidates: concentric/global (end-stage) ankle arthritis (there is nowhere to shift the load to), severe stiffness, neuropathy/Charcot, active infection, and significant talar tilt with instability that cannot be corrected.
  • Planning must define the centre of rotation of angulation (CORA) and correct in all planes — coronal (varus/valgus), sagittal (recurvatum/procurvatum) and rotation. Medial opening-wedge osteotomy for varus (adds length, may need graft, risks wound problems over the thin medial skin) and lateral opening or medial closing wedge for valgus. A concomitant fibular osteotomy is often required for larger corrections.
  • Adjunctive procedures are frequently needed: calcaneal osteotomy for a compensatory hindfoot deformity, lateral ligament reconstruction, and débridement/microfracture of an osteochondral lesion.
  • Outcomes are good in properly selected patients, with pain relief and delay/avoidance of fusion; results deteriorate with more advanced arthritis, and conversion to fusion or arthroplasty later remains possible.

Turf toe & first MTP injuries

The plantar plate injury of the great toe.

1 of 7 sittings2024F

  • Turf toe is a hyperextension injury of the first MTP joint that disrupts the plantar plate/capsuloligamentous complex, classically on artificial turf with flexible footwear. Grade I — stretch/sprain; Grade II — partial tear with moderate pain, ecchymosis and restricted motion; Grade III — complete tear of the plantar plate, with marked swelling, ecchymosis, inability to push off, and often sesamoid retraction/diastasis.
  • Radiographic sign of a complete (grade III) injury: proximal migration of the sesamoids on a lateral or axial view (comparison with the contralateral foot), or an increased sesamoid–joint distance; a bipartite sesamoid may show diastasis. MRI is the definitive study and shows the plantar plate disruption.
  • Grade III injuries in a collegiate/professional athlete are the group considered for surgical repair — direct repair of the plantar plate ± sesamoid fixation or excision of a fragmented sesamoid. Grade I and II injuries are treated non-operatively with taping, a stiff/rigid-soled shoe or carbon-fibre insert, and progressive return to play (grade I ~days, grade II ~2 weeks, grade III 8–10 weeks or surgery).
  • Loss of push-off power and persistent pain are the hallmarks of an inadequately treated turf toe, and chronic injury leads to hallux valgus/varus deformity, cock-up deformity, sesamoid problems and first MTP arthritis. This is a career-affecting injury in football and is a leading cause of missed games.
  • Sesamoid excision should be avoided where possible (removing both sesamoids causes a cock-up deformity; removing the tibial sesamoid predisposes to hallux valgus, and the fibular sesamoid to hallux varus).
  • Distinguish from a plantar plate/sesamoid stress injury and from hallux rigidus (dorsal pain, dorsal osteophyte, limited dorsiflexion with a grind test).
