Surgical approaches: Hip & pelvis

Internervous intervals, structures at risk and indications for the standard hip/pelvis approaches.

7 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Smith-Petersen (anterior) — and the direct anterior approach derived from it.
    • Internervous interval: SARTORIUS and RECTUS FEMORIS (FEMORAL nerve) versus TENSOR FASCIA LATA (SUPERIOR GLUTEAL nerve). Deeper, the interval is between rectus femoris (femoral) and gluteus medius (superior gluteal).
    • The superficial structure at risk is the LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS NERVE, which crosses roughly 2 cm medial and distal to the ASIS — injury or neuropraxia is the commonest complication of the approach, and it is avoided by developing the interval lateral to the sartorius fascia, not through it.
    • The deep structure at risk is the ASCENDING BRANCH OF THE LATERAL FEMORAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY, which crosses the interval and must be ligated.
    • Gives excellent access to the anterior column, the anterior wall and the hip capsule, and is the approach for Pipkin femoral head fractures (particularly Pipkin I and II, where the fragment is anteroinferior), for the anterior approach THA, and for paediatric open reduction of DDH. It gives poor access to the posterior column and to the femoral shaft.
  • Kocher-Langenbeck (posterior).
    • Not a true internervous approach — it splits gluteus maximus (inferior gluteal nerve proximally, but the split is between fibres supplied by the same nerve).
    • The SCIATIC NERVE is the structure at risk. Protect it by positioning with the knee FLEXED and the hip EXTENDED throughout, and avoid prolonged or forceful retraction; the peroneal division is the more vulnerable.
    • The SUPERIOR GLUTEAL NEUROVASCULAR BUNDLE emerges from the greater sciatic notch just above piriformis and limits how far proximally the exposure can be extended — tearing the superior gluteal artery in the notch causes brisk bleeding that retracts into the pelvis.
    • Preserve QUADRATUS FEMORIS and OBTURATOR EXTERNUS — the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery runs deep to them, and dividing quadratus femoris devascularises the femoral head.
    • Indications: posterior wall and posterior column acetabular fractures, transverse and T-type patterns, and posterior fracture-dislocations. Posterior wall is by far the commonest acetabular fracture pattern, and the stability of the hip after fixation depends on the size of the wall fragment and the presence of marginal impaction, which must be disimpacted and bone-grafted.
  • Ilioinguinal (Letournel) — the three windows.
    • LATERAL window — lateral to iliopsoas: exposes the internal iliac fossa and the SI joint; the LFCN is in this window.
    • MIDDLE window — between iliopsoas/femoral nerve and the femoral vessels: exposes the pelvic brim, quadrilateral surface and superior pubic ramus.
    • MEDIAL window — medial to the femoral vessels (and the spermatic cord or round ligament): exposes the symphysis and the retropubic space.
    • Indications: anterior column and anterior column-posterior hemitransverse fractures, and the anterior half of both-column fractures. An associated both-column (ABC) fracture is most often approached anteriorly (ilioinguinal or intrapelvic), because the anterior column carries the majority of the displacement and the posterior column usually reduces indirectly.
    • The external iliac vessels and the femoral nerve are the structures at risk; lymphocele and femoral nerve palsy are the characteristic complications.
  • Stoppa / anterior intrapelvic (AIP) approach, and the corona mortis.
    • A midline (or Pfannenstiel) incision through the linea alba, dividing the rectus abdominis insertion, into the space of Retzius, working from inside the pelvis outward.
    • Exposes the quadrilateral surface, the pelvic brim, the obturator neurovascular bundle and the medial wall superbly — it has largely replaced the middle and medial windows of the ilioinguinal approach, and is combined with a lateral window when the iliac fossa is needed.
    • The CORONA MORTIS must be identified and ligated: a vascular anastomosis between the OBTURATOR and the EXTERNAL ILIAC or INFERIOR EPIGASTRIC vessels, crossing the superior pubic ramus roughly 4–6 cm lateral to the symphysis. It is present in a majority of hemipelves (commonly quoted at 60–80%, venous more often than arterial), and an unrecognised avulsion retracts into the pelvis and bleeds catastrophically.
    • The obturator nerve and vessels exit through the obturator canal in the SUPEROLATERAL quadrant of the obturator foramen — the safe quadrant for a screw or a clamp is the inferomedial one.
  • Surgical hip dislocation (Ganz) and the blood supply that dictates it.
    • A trochanteric flip (digastric) osteotomy leaving gluteus medius on the proximal fragment and vastus lateralis on the distal fragment, with a Z- or T-shaped ANTERIOR capsulotomy and anterior dislocation of the head.
    • The structure that must be protected is the DEEP BRANCH OF THE MEDIAL FEMORAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY. It runs POSTERIOR to obturator externus and ANTERIOR to the tendons of superior gemellus, obturator internus and inferior gemellus, then pierces the capsule at the level of the superior gemellus.
    • Therefore: OBTURATOR EXTERNUS MUST BE PRESERVED (it is the protective sling), and the short external rotators may be released only lateral to their insertions, leaving a cuff. Piriformis may be divided; quadratus femoris must not be.
    • Performed correctly, the reported rate of osteonecrosis is essentially zero, which is the point of the technique — it allows 360° access to the head and acetabulum for FAI, femoral head fractures, OCD and tumour.
  • Direct lateral (Hardinge) and posterior (Southern/Moore) approaches for arthroplasty.
    • DIRECT LATERAL — splits gluteus medius and vastus lateralis in continuity with the anterior capsule. Not internervous. The SUPERIOR GLUTEAL NERVE is the structure injured, and Trendelenburg gait / abductor weakness is the characteristic complication; the nerve runs transversely roughly 3–5 cm proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter, so the split must not extend beyond that. Lower dislocation rate than the posterior approach.
    • POSTERIOR — splits gluteus maximus and detaches the short external rotators; the sciatic nerve is at risk. Preserves the abductors entirely (no limp), at the cost of a higher dislocation rate, which is substantially reduced by formal posterior capsular and short-external-rotator repair.
    • Watson-Jones (anterolateral) — between tensor fascia lata (superior gluteal) and gluteus medius (superior gluteal): not internervous, and the superior gluteal nerve is again the structure at risk.
  • Extended approaches and quick anatomy checks.
    • Extended iliofemoral (Letournel) gives simultaneous access to both columns but has the highest rate of heterotopic ossification and abductor damage; it is now rarely used.
    • Landmark relationships: within the greater sciatic notch, from superior to inferior, superior gluteal neurovascular bundle → piriformis → inferior gluteal vessels and nerve, sciatic nerve, internal pudendal vessels and pudendal nerve (the pudendal bundle re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen).
    • The sciatic nerve exits BELOW piriformis in about 85% of people; the common variant is the peroneal division passing through or above the muscle.
    • The safe zone for extra-articular screws from the intrapelvic side is defined by staying on the quadrilateral surface and confirming with an obturator-oblique outlet view; intra-articular penetration is the complication to exclude fluoroscopically.

Brachial plexus & peripheral nerve anatomy of the upper limb

Plexus organisation, named branch levels and clinically important trifurcations.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2021 (x2), 2024S, 2024F (x2)

  • The architecture: Roots, Trunks, Divisions, Cords, Branches.
    • ROOTS — ventral rami of C5–T1, emerging between anterior and middle scalene.
    • TRUNKS — UPPER (C5–C6), MIDDLE (C7), LOWER (C8–T1), in the posterior triangle over the first rib.
    • DIVISIONS — each trunk splits into an anterior and a posterior division behind the clavicle. The divisions are the part behind the clavicle — a favourite recall point.
    • CORDS — named for their relation to the AXILLARY ARTERY: LATERAL (anterior divisions of upper and middle trunks), POSTERIOR (all three posterior divisions), MEDIAL (anterior division of the lower trunk alone).
    • BRANCHES — the five terminal nerves.
  • Branches that come off before the cords — and why they localise a lesion.
    • From the ROOTS: DORSAL SCAPULAR (C5, to rhomboids and levator scapulae) and LONG THORACIC (C5, C6, C7, to serratus anterior). The long thoracic nerve pierces the middle scalene; injury gives MEDIAL scapular winging.
    • From the UPPER TRUNK: SUPRASCAPULAR nerve (C5–C6) and the nerve to SUBCLAVIUS.
    • Preserved rhomboid and serratus function localises a lesion DISTAL to the roots; their loss implies a very proximal (pre-ganglionic / root avulsion) injury.
    • Other localising signs of a PRE-ganglionic (root avulsion) injury: HORNER'S SYNDROME (T1 sympathetic), an elevated hemidiaphragm (phrenic, C3–C5), winging, pseudomeningoceles on CT myelogram or MRI, and PRESERVED sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) in an anaesthetic limb — the dorsal root ganglion is intact and distal to the lesion, so the sensory axon does not degenerate. Preserved SNAPs with absent sensation is the pathognomonic electrodiagnostic finding.
    • A pre-ganglionic avulsion cannot be repaired directly — it requires nerve transfer (neurotisation), whereas a post-ganglionic rupture can be grafted.
  • Cord branches and the five terminal nerves.
    • LATERAL cord — lateral pectoral nerve; terminal: MUSCULOCUTANEOUS and the lateral root of the MEDIAN.
    • POSTERIOR cord — Upper subscapular, thoracoDorsal, Lower subscapular ("ULnar-D" mnemonic: Upper subscapular, thoracoDorsal, Lower subscapular); terminal: AXILLARY and RADIAL.
    • MEDIAL cord — medial pectoral, medial brachial cutaneous, medial antebrachial cutaneous; terminal: ULNAR and the medial root of the MEDIAN.
    • The MUSCULOCUTANEOUS nerve pierces CORACOBRACHIALIS on average 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid — the "safe zone" limit for medial retraction in an anterior shoulder approach; it terminates as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.
    • The AXILLARY nerve passes through the quadrangular space (teres minor above, teres major below, long head of triceps medially, humeral shaft laterally) with the posterior circumflex humeral artery, then wraps the humerus roughly 5–7 cm distal to the acromion (about a hand's breadth) — the limit of a deltoid-splitting approach.
    • The RADIAL nerve passes through the triangular interval (teres major above, long head of triceps medially, humerus laterally) with the profunda brachii, runs in the spiral groove roughly 14 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle and 20 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle, and pierces the lateral intermuscular septum about 10 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle.
  • Injury patterns worth recognising.
    • ERB-DUCHENNE (C5–C6, upper trunk) — loss of abduction, external rotation and elbow flexion; the arm is adducted, internally rotated and pronated: the "waiter's tip" position. Caused by a forced increase in the head-shoulder angle.
    • KLUMPKE (C8–T1, lower trunk) — intrinsic hand paralysis and a claw hand, often with Horner's syndrome; caused by forced abduction/traction on an elevated arm. Worse prognosis than Erb's.
    • THORACIC OUTLET SYNDROME — most often NEUROGENIC and involving the LOWER TRUNK (C8–T1), from a cervical rib or fibrous band; symptoms in the ulnar forearm and hand with intrinsic wasting.
    • PARSONAGE-TURNER (neuralgic amyotrophy) — severe shoulder pain preceding a patchy motor palsy, classically the suprascapular, long thoracic or anterior interosseous nerve; non-operative, and most recover.
    • Scapular winging: MEDIAL winging = long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior), worse on forward flexion / wall push-up. LATERAL winging = spinal accessory nerve (trapezius), worse on abduction, with a drooped shoulder. This distinction is the one most often reversed in an option list.
  • Nerve transfers: the modern reconstructive vocabulary.
    • OBERLIN transfer — a fascicle of the ULNAR nerve (to FCU) transferred to the musculocutaneous branch to biceps, restoring elbow flexion in C5–C6 avulsion. The double Oberlin adds a median fascicle to the brachialis branch.
    • Spinal accessory to suprascapular for shoulder abduction and external rotation; triceps branch of the radial nerve to the axillary nerve for deltoid.
    • Intercostal nerves and the contralateral C7 are donors when there are no ipsilateral options.
    • Timing matters: a nerve transfer must reach the motor endplate within roughly 12–18 months of injury before irreversible motor endplate degeneration; a sharp/open injury is repaired acutely, a closed traction injury is observed for 3–6 months with serial examination and EMG.
    • Priority of reconstruction: elbow flexion first, then shoulder stability and abduction, then hand sensation, then wrist/finger function.
  • Peripheral relationships worth memorising for approaches.
    • Deltopectoral interval — between deltoid (AXILLARY) and pectoralis major (MEDIAL and LATERAL PECTORAL): a true internervous interval; the cephalic vein marks it and is taken laterally with the deltoid.
    • The axillary nerve is the structure at risk with the inferior capsule in shoulder instability surgery and with an inferior glenoid anchor.
    • The suprascapular nerve passes under the transverse scapular ligament through the suprascapular notch (the nerve goes UNDER, the artery goes OVER), then around the spinoglenoid notch; it is tethered by a large retracted supraspinatus tear and at risk with medialised glenoid work and glenoid baseplate screws.
    • The MEDIAN nerve crosses from LATERAL to MEDIAL relative to the brachial artery in the mid-arm; in the antecubital fossa the order from lateral to medial is Tendon (biceps), Artery (brachial), Nerve (median).

Surgical approaches: Elbow & forearm

Choosing the correct interval for radius, coronoid and distal humerus exposure.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2024S (x2), 2024F, 2025

  • Henry (anterior/volar) approach to the radius — the one for the proximal third.
    • Internervous interval: BRACHIORADIALIS (RADIAL nerve) versus PRONATOR TERES (MEDIAN nerve) proximally, and brachioradialis versus FCR (MEDIAN) distally. It is a true internervous interval along the whole length, which is why it is extensile from the biceps tuberosity to the wrist.
    • The RADIAL ARTERY lies deep to brachioradialis and is retracted MEDIALLY (ulnarly) with the flexor mass; the superficial radial nerve runs on the undersurface of brachioradialis and is retracted laterally with it.
    • To expose the proximal radius safely: SUPINATE the forearm. Supination carries the PIN laterally and posteriorly, and brings the insertion of supinator on the anterior radius into view so it can be released subperiosteally from its radial insertion, keeping the dissection on bone and the nerve within the muscle.
    • This is the approach for a proximal-third radial shaft fracture (including a comminuted gunshot injury) and for the anterior radial head or neck when the LCL is intact.
    • Distally the approach requires taking pronator quadratus off the radius; this is the standard volar approach to the distal radius.
  • Thompson (dorsal) approach — the middle and distal third.
    • Internervous interval: ECRB (RADIAL nerve) versus EDC (PIN).
    • The PIN is directly in the field, within the supinator, and MUST be formally identified before the supinator is divided. This is the approach's defining hazard.
    • PRONATE the forearm to bring the PIN's exit point medially away from the dissection, or identify the nerve proximally between brachioradialis and brachialis and follow it distally.
    • Best for the middle and distal thirds of the radius, and for dorsal plating; it is not the approach of choice for the proximal third precisely because of the PIN.
  • Kocher and Kaplan — the lateral elbow.
    • KOCHER: ANCONEUS (RADIAL nerve) versus ECU (PIN) — the more posterior of the two intervals, and the workhorse for the radial head and capitellum.
    • KAPLAN: ECRB versus EDC — more anterior, giving better access to the anterior radial head but sitting closer to the PIN.
    • In both, keep the capsular incision ANTERIOR to the MIDLINE (equator) of the radial head — extending posteriorly detaches the LUCL from the supinator crest and creates iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability.
    • Keep the forearm PRONATED for any work on the radial neck; the PIN sits an average of 3–4 cm distal to the radiocapitellar joint in pronation and moves closer with supination. Do not place retractors more than about 3 cm distal to the joint.
  • Getting to the coronoid.
    • A small TIP fragment (O'Driscoll type 1) is reached laterally through the defect created by the radial head fracture, or after temporarily removing the head.
    • A large ANTEROMEDIAL FACET or BASAL fragment requires a MEDIAL approach — the Hotchkiss "over-the-top" (elevating the flexor-pronator mass off the medial epicondyle) or the Taylor-Scham FCU-splitting interval, both with the ULNAR NERVE identified and protected first.
    • Attempting to buttress an anteromedial facet fragment from the lateral side is the classic error — there is no way to place a medial buttress plate through a lateral window.
  • Posterior humerus, and finding the radial nerve.
    • A triceps-splitting or paratricipital (Alonso-Llames) posterior approach gives the widest exposure of the distal two-thirds of the humerus; the modified posterior (Gerwin) extensile approach mobilises the radial nerve and allows exposure of nearly the whole shaft.
    • The radial nerve in the spiral groove lies roughly 14 cm proximal to the LATERAL epicondyle and 20 cm proximal to the MEDIAL epicondyle, and pierces the lateral intermuscular septum about 10 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle.
    • The posterolateral (Henry) approach to the distal humerus and the anterolateral approach both find the nerve between brachialis and brachioradialis.
    • A radial nerve palsy after a humeral shaft fracture is usually a neuropraxia and is observed; a palsy that appears after manipulation or fixation, or an open injury, is explored.
  • Olecranon osteotomy for the distal humerus.
    • Apex-DISTAL CHEVRON osteotomy at the BARE AREA of the trochlear (sigmoid) notch, roughly 2–3 cm from the tip — a deliberately non-articular strip.
    • Pre-drill and pre-tap the fixation BEFORE cutting, and finish the cut with an osteotome so the cancellous surfaces interdigitate on repair.
    • Yields approximately 57% of the distal humeral articular surface, versus ~46% for a triceps-reflecting and ~35% for a triceps-splitting approach.
    • Do not use it if conversion to total elbow arthroplasty is a possibility; use a paratricipital exposure instead.
  • Other intervals to keep straight.
    • Boyd — a posterolateral approach elevating anconeus and supinator subperiosteally off the ulna; historically used for Monteggia lesions, but its subperiosteal ulnar exposure is associated with radioulnar synostosis.
    • Anterior (Fiolle-Delmas) approach to the elbow for the brachial artery and median nerve; the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve emerges lateral to the biceps tendon and is at risk.
    • Distal biceps repair: the two-incision (muscle-splitting Boyd-Anderson modification) approach reaches the posteroulnar footprint of the tuberosity but risks synostosis; the single anterior incision risks the LABCN (commonest complication) and the PIN.
    • Between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris lies Osborne's ligament — the roof of the cubital tunnel and the commonest site of ulnar nerve compression.

Surgical approaches: Cervical spine

The Smith-Robinson anterior approach and its structures at risk.

3 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2024F

  • The anterior (Smith-Robinson) approach: the plane and what sits on either side.
    • A transverse (or longitudinal) incision along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid; the plane runs MEDIAL to sternocleidomastoid and the CAROTID SHEATH, and LATERAL to the strap muscles, trachea and oesophagus.
    • The CAROTID SHEATH — containing the CAROTID ARTERY, the INTERNAL JUGULAR VEIN and the VAGUS NERVE — is retracted LATERALLY. The vagus lies WITHIN the sheath, between and posterior to the artery and vein.
    • The trachea, oesophagus, thyroid and recurrent laryngeal nerve are retracted MEDIALLY.
    • Deep to this, the LONGUS COLLI is elevated subperiosteally off the vertebral bodies; keeping the dissection medial to the medial border of longus colli protects the SYMPATHETIC CHAIN and the VERTEBRAL ARTERY.
    • The vertebral artery ascends in the transverse foramina, entering at C6 in the great majority (a variant entry at C7 or C5 exists), and is at risk with lateral dissection, with excessively lateral vertebral body resection, and with transarticular or pedicle screws.
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve: the left-versus-right argument.
    • It runs in the TRACHEO-OESOPHAGEAL GROOVE. On the LEFT it loops under the AORTIC ARCH and ascends in the groove with a long, constant, predictable course. On the RIGHT it loops around the SUBCLAVIAN ARTERY and takes a more oblique and variable course, crossing the operative field at the lower cervical levels.
    • This anatomy is the traditional argument for a LEFT-sided approach at the lower cervical levels, although comparative clinical series have not consistently shown a difference in palsy rates by side — the anatomical asymmetry is the examinable fact, the clinical superiority is contested.
    • Injury produces HOARSENESS and vocal cord paresis; the practical prevention measures are gentle, intermittent retraction, keeping the retractor blade under the longus colli, and deflating and re-inflating the endotracheal tube cuff after the retractor is placed.
    • Persistent hoarseness beyond a few weeks warrants ENT laryngoscopy. Bilateral injury (a risk of revision anterior surgery from the opposite side) can cause airway obstruction — which is why a revision approach is generally made from the SAME side as the index surgery, after documenting cord function pre-operatively.
  • The other named nerves at risk, by level.
    • HYPOGLOSSAL NERVE (CN XII) — at C2–C3, in the high anterior exposure. Injury gives tongue deviation TOWARD the side of the lesion and dysarthria.
    • SUPERIOR LARYNGEAL NERVE, external branch (to cricothyroid) — at C3–C4, running with the superior thyroid artery. Injury gives loss of high-pitch phonation and vocal fatigue — the professional-voice-user complication.
    • SYMPATHETIC CHAIN — on the anterolateral surface of longus colli, most superficial and most at risk at C6 (the stellate ganglion sits at C7–T1). Injury gives HORNER'S SYNDROME: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis.
    • THORACIC DUCT — on the LEFT at the cervicothoracic junction; injury gives a chylous leak.
    • OESOPHAGUS — perforation is rare but among the most feared complications; it may present late with fever, dysphagia, sepsis or a persistent draining wound, and needs urgent contrast study and repair.
  • Dysphagia and airway: the commonest problems in practice.
    • Dysphagia is the commonest post-operative complaint after anterior cervical surgery, affecting a large minority early and settling in most by 6–12 months.
    • Risk factors: longer retraction time, multi-level surgery, more cephalad levels, higher-profile anterior plates, revision surgery, female sex and prevertebral soft-tissue swelling.
    • Airway compromise from prevertebral haematoma or swelling is the emergency — it typically declares in the first 24–72 hours, is more likely after multi-level or long procedures, and is managed by opening the wound at the bedside before attempting intubation.
  • Posterior cervical exposure.
    • A midline approach through the avascular ligamentum nuchae, with subperiosteal elevation of the paraspinal muscles — staying strictly midline minimises bleeding.
    • Preserve the C2 (semispinalis cervicis) muscular attachments where possible; stripping them contributes to post-laminectomy kyphosis and axial neck pain.
    • Lateral mass screws: the Magerl or Roy-Camille trajectories aim to avoid the nerve root (inferolateral) and the vertebral artery (medial and anterior). C7 has a small lateral mass and is often instrumented with a pedicle screw instead.
    • The C5 nerve root is the one that palsies after posterior decompression (reported in roughly 5% of laminoplasty/laminectomy cases) — deltoid and biceps weakness appearing days after surgery, attributed to root tethering as the cord drifts posteriorly; most recover.
    • C1–C2 (Harms/Goel) fixation: the vertebral artery in the groove on the superior surface of the C1 posterior arch and the C2 nerve root and its venous plexus are the structures at risk; a pre-operative CT angiogram is used to check for a high-riding vertebral artery, which precludes a C2 pars/pedicle screw.
  • Anterior versus posterior: choosing the side of the problem.
    • Anterior for 1–3 level disease with anterior (discogenic/osteophytic) compression, kyphosis, or the need to restore lordosis — the anterior approach can correct alignment.
    • Posterior for multi-level (3 or more) disease, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, congenital stenosis, and where lordosis is already preserved — the cord must be able to drift posteriorly, so a fixed kyphotic deformity is a contraindication to posterior-only decompression.
    • The classic teaching point: a posterior decompression relies on posterior drift of the cord, so it fails in a kyphotic spine.

Anterolateral vs anteromedial exposure of the pilon and the limits of each.

  • Anterolateral approach to the distal tibia.

  • Anteromedial approach, and the skin bridge rule.
    • Gives the best access to the medial and central articular surface and to the medial malleolus, but the subcutaneous anteromedial tibia has a thin, poorly vascularised soft-tissue envelope.
    • The classic error is combining an anteromedial tibial incision with a direct lateral fibular incision, leaving a narrow strip of skin between them.

Posterior and posterolateral exposure of the knee and tibial plateau.

The INFRAPATELLAR BRANCH OF THE SAPHENOUS NERVE crosses the midline transversely and is divided by any anterior midline incision, producing the near-universal numb patch lateral to the scar, which must be part of the consent conversation.

    • Positioning prone or floppy-lateral; the interval is between the MEDIAL HEAD OF GASTROCNEMIUS (retracted LATERALLY, which protects the POPLITEAL NEUROVASCULAR BUNDLE) and the semimembranosus / pes anserinus.

    • Direct posterior (S-shaped / Trickey) approach allows PCL tibial avulsion fixation and access to the popliteal fossa; the MEDIAL SURAL CUTANEOUS NERVE, the short saphenous vein and the popliteal vessels are at risk, and the interval passes between the two heads of gastrocnemius.

Gracilis and semitendinosus lie BETWEEN layers I and II, which is where they are harvested.

    • POSTEROMEDIAL portal — safe zone bounded by the medial femoral condyle and the posteromedial tibia; the SAPHENOUS NERVE and vein are at risk.
    • POSTEROLATERAL portal — made anterior to the biceps femoris and posterior to the iliotibial band, with the knee FLEXED 90° to move the peroneal nerve posteriorly; the COMMON PERONEAL NERVE is at risk.

Surgical approaches: Thoracolumbar spine

Anterior/retroperitoneal exposure and the thoracolumbar junction.

  • Anterior retroperitoneal lumbar approach: the order of structures.
    • From MEDIAL to LATERAL at the L4–5 level: great vessels (aorta / IVC, or the common iliac vessels) → SYMPATHETIC CHAIN → PSOAS → URETER → GENITOFEMORAL NERVE.
    • The GENITOFEMORAL NERVE lies ON THE ANTEROMEDIAL SURFACE OF PSOAS — the specific structure identified there, and the answer when asked what sits on the anteromedial psoas.
    • The URETER crosses OVER the common iliac vessels at the pelvic brim and is swept ANTERIORLY with the peritoneum; if it is not seen, it is usually still attached to the peritoneum and safe.
    • The SYMPATHETIC CHAIN runs in the groove between the psoas and the vertebral bodies; unilateral injury gives a warm, dry, "vasodilated" ipsilateral leg that patients frequently interpret as the opposite leg being cold.
    • The approach is retroperitoneal, and the plane is developed by sweeping the peritoneal sac and its contents medially — a transperitoneal approach is rarely needed and adds ileus and adhesive obstruction.
  • The specific hazards of the anterior lumbar approach.
    • RETROGRADE EJACULATION from injury to the SUPERIOR HYPOGASTRIC PLEXUS, which lies over the L5–S1 disc and the sacral promontory. Prevention: approach L5–S1 from the LEFT in men, use BLUNT dissection only, and avoid MONOPOLAR cautery (use bipolar).     • Access surgeon involvement (vascular or general surgery) is standard practice for the anterior approach in many Canadian centres.
    • LATERAL TRANSPSOAS (LLIF/XLIF) approaches traverse the LUMBAR PLEXUS within the psoas. The plexus migrates ANTERIORLY as you descend, so L4–5 carries the highest risk and L5–S1 is generally inaccessible because of the iliac crest and the plexus. The GENITOFEMORAL NERVE is the one most commonly injured (anterior thigh/groin numbness), and transient hip flexor weakness and anterior thigh pain occur in a large minority. Real-time triggered EMG neuromonitoring is mandatory.
  • Thoracic and thoracolumbar approaches.
    • Thoracotomy alone for T4–T10; a thoracoabdominal (transthoracic-retroperitoneal) approach for T11–L2, which requires taking down the diaphragm at its PERIPHERAL attachment, leaving a 1–2 cm cuff for repair.
    • Resect the rib one or two levels ABOVE the target vertebra (the rib angles downward), and the resected rib serves as autograft.
    • Approach from the LEFT for thoracic and thoracolumbar levels: the AORTA is thick-walled and tolerates mobilisation, whereas the thin-walled VENA CAVA does not, and the LIVER is avoided. The THORACIC DUCT is on the RIGHT in the lower thorax before crossing to the left at about T5.

The ARTERY OF ADAMKIEWICZ (great radicular artery) usually arises on the LEFT between T8 and L1; sacrificing multiple left segmentals in that zone risks anterior spinal cord ischaemia.

    • The midline posterior approach is subperiosteal through the avascular midline raphe; the PARASPINAL (Wiltse) approach uses the internervous-in-practice plane between MULTIFIDUS and LONGISSIMUS and gives direct access to the transverse processes, the pars, and far-lateral disc herniations with less muscle stripping and blood loss.
    • The costotransversectomy and lateral extracavitary approaches extend posterior exposure to the anterior column without a thoracotomy; the segmental nerve root and the pleura are at risk.
    • In the thoracic spine a nerve root may be ligated to gain anterior access; in the lumbar spine, NEVER — and even in the thoracic spine, roots at T1, T2 and the Adamkiewicz level should be preserved.
  • Pedicle and iliac screw anatomy.
    • Pedicle diameter is SMALLEST at T4–T6 and largest at L5; the medial angulation is greatest in the upper thoracic spine and decreases caudally, then increases again in the lower lumbar spine.
    • The medial pedicle wall is 2–3 times thicker than the lateral wall, so a breach tends to occur laterally — but a MEDIAL breach is the dangerous one (dura and cord/root), while a lateral breach in the thoracic spine risks the pleura and the segmental vessels.
    • Entry points: lumbar at the junction of the transverse process midline and the lateral border of the superior articular facet ("pars-transverse" or the mamillary process); thoracic just inferior to the rim of the superior facet and lateral to the midline of the facet, varying by level.
    • S2-alar-iliac (S2AI) screws are lower-profile than classic iliac screws and cross the SI joint; the trajectory passes above the sciatic notch, which is the structure to avoid.
    • Anterior structures at risk from an over-long lumbar pedicle screw: the aorta and iliac vessels on the left, the IVC on the right; screws breaching anteriorly at L4–5 have caused fatal vascular injury.

Biceps musculotendinous anatomy & rotation

The 90° rotation of the distal biceps and its clinical consequences.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2023

  • The 90° rotation of the musculotendinous unit.
    • The biceps musculotendinous unit undergoes an approximately 90° EXTERNAL (lateral, supination-ward) ROTATION as it travels from the muscle belly to the radial tuberosity.
    • The consequence: the SHORT HEAD fibres come to lie DISTAL on the radial tuberosity and the LONG HEAD fibres PROXIMAL, and the surface that faces anteriorly at the musculotendinous junction faces laterally at the insertion.
    • The tendon inserts on the ULNAR (medial) and POSTERIOR aspect of the radial tuberosity, as a ribbon roughly 21 × 7 mm — not on the anterior surface where an anterior approach naturally lands.
  • Functional consequences of the two heads' geometry.
    • The LONG HEAD, inserting more proximally and therefore further from the axis of forearm rotation, contributes more to SUPINATION.
    • The SHORT HEAD, inserting more distally with a longer moment arm about the elbow, is the more effective ELBOW FLEXOR.
    • This is why supination strength (and especially supination ENDURANCE) is the deficit that persists after an unrepaired or non-anatomically repaired distal biceps rupture, whereas flexion is partly compensated by brachialis and brachioradialis.
    • The biceps is the most powerful supinator with the elbow FLEXED to 90°; with the elbow extended, supinator takes over and the deficit is less obvious on examination.
  • Why the footprint matters in repair.
    • Anatomic repair must restore the POSTERIOR-ULNAR footprint to recover the supination moment arm; a repair placed too anteriorly on the tuberosity shortens that moment arm and loses supination torque even when the tendon heals.
    • This is the mechanical argument in favour of the two-incision (muscle-splitting) technique, which reaches the posteroulnar footprint directly.
    • Whichever technique is used, the forearm must be fully PRONATED to bring the tuberosity anterolaterally into view and to move the PIN away from the field.
  • The lacertus fibrosus, and diagnosis.
    • The lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis) sweeps medially from the myotendinous junction into the antebrachial fascia over the flexor-pronator mass.
    • An INTACT lacertus prevents proximal retraction, so the arm contour looks close to normal and there is no reverse Popeye — the classic reason a complete rupture is missed.
    • The HOOK TEST (O'Driscoll) is the most accurate physical examination test: with the elbow flexed 90° and actively supinated, the examiner hooks an index finger under the LATERAL edge of the tendon. 100% sensitivity and specificity in the original series, outperforming MRI. Hooking from the medial side can catch the lacertus and give a false negative.
    • MRI in the FABS position (Flexed elbow, ABducted shoulder, forearm Supinated) lays the tendon out along its length when the examination is equivocal.
  • Proximal biceps anatomy, for contrast.
    • The LONG HEAD originates from the supraglenoid tubercle and the superior labrum (with substantial variation in labral versus tubercle contribution) and runs through the rotator interval; the SHORT HEAD originates from the coracoid, conjoined with coracobrachialis.
    • The long head is stabilised in the bicipital groove by the biceps pulley: the superior glenohumeral ligament, the coracohumeral ligament, and the subscapularis and supraspinatus tendon slips. Medial subluxation of the long head implies a subscapularis or pulley lesion.
    • The MUSCULOCUTANEOUS nerve enters coracobrachialis on average 5–8 cm distal to the coracoid — the safe zone for medial retraction in the deltopectoral approach.

Foot & ankle neuroanatomy (plantar nerves, dorsomedial cutaneous)

  • The tibial nerve and its plantar branches.
    • The tibial nerve enters the TARSAL TUNNEL deep to the flexor retinaculum (laciniate ligament) and divides into the MEDIAL and LATERAL PLANTAR NERVES, usually within the tunnel.

    • The MEDIAL CALCANEAL BRANCH supplies the heel pad and frequently arises PROXIMAL to the flexor retinaculum, which is why heel sensation is often spared in tarsal tunnel syndrome.
  • Baxter's nerve — the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve.
    • It innervates ABDUCTOR DIGITI MINIMI, and also supplies quadratus plantae, flexor digitorum brevis and the long plantar ligament (which is why it can produce heel pain without any motor complaint).
    • Entrapment occurs between the deep fascia of ABDUCTOR HALLUCIS and the medial edge of QUADRATUS PLANTAE.
    • Clinically: chronic medial/plantar heel pain that, unlike plantar fasciitis, is NOT worst with the first steps in the morning and does not have classic night pain; maximal tenderness is more proximal and medial than the plantar fascia origin. Isolated denervation atrophy of abductor digiti minimi on MRI is the confirmatory finding.
    • It accounts for a meaningful minority of "recalcitrant plantar fasciitis" and is released along with the fascia when suspected.
  • Dorsal cutaneous nerves and bunion surgery.
    • The SUPERFICIAL PERONEAL NERVE divides into the MEDIAL DORSAL CUTANEOUS and INTERMEDIATE DORSAL CUTANEOUS branches.
    • The DORSOMEDIAL CUTANEOUS NERVE TO THE HALLUX is a branch of the MEDIAL DORSAL CUTANEOUS branch of the SUPERFICIAL PERONEAL NERVE — not of the deep peroneal or saphenous nerve.
    • It is directly at risk in the dorsomedial approach for hallux valgus and bunion surgery, and injury produces a painful neuroma over the medial hallux and a numb medial toe — a well-recognised and repeatedly examined complication.
    • Prevention: keep the incision truly midaxial-medial rather than dorsal, raise full-thickness flaps, and identify and retract the nerve rather than dissecting through it.
    • The DEEP PERONEAL NERVE supplies the FIRST DORSAL WEB SPACE and extensor digitorum brevis; it is at risk with the dorsal first web space approach and with anterior ankle arthroscopy portals placed too centrally.
    • The SAPHENOUS NERVE reaches only as far distally as the medial midfoot — it is the nerve at risk with the anteromedial ankle arthroscopy portal and with medial malleolar fixation, not with hallux surgery.
  • The sural nerve.
    • Formed from the medial sural cutaneous (tibial) and the peroneal communicating (common peroneal) branches, it runs with the short saphenous vein.
    • It crosses the lateral border of the Achilles roughly 10 cm proximal to the insertion, and passes about 1–1.5 cm posterior and distal to the tip of the LATERAL MALLEOLUS.
    • The nerve most commonly injured in percutaneous Achilles repair, in the lateral extensile calcaneal approach, in the posterolateral ankle arthroscopy portal, and in fifth metatarsal base fixation.
    • It is also the standard donor for a nerve graft, giving roughly 30–40 cm of graft.
  • Ankle arthroscopy portals — which nerve for which portal.
    • ANTEROMEDIAL (made FIRST), just medial to tibialis anterior: the GREATER SAPHENOUS VEIN and SAPHENOUS NERVE.
    • ANTEROLATERAL (made SECOND, under transillumination), lateral to peroneus tertius: the SUPERFICIAL PERONEAL NERVE, INTERMEDIATE DORSAL CUTANEOUS branch — and this is the nerve most commonly injured in ankle arthroscopy overall.
    • An ANTEROCENTRAL portal is avoided — the deep peroneal nerve and anterior tibial artery lie directly under it.
    • POSTEROLATERAL: the SURAL NERVE. POSTEROMEDIAL (rarely used): the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle, with the FHL tendon as the medial safety landmark in posterior hindfoot arthroscopy.
    • When the question names the first portal it is asking about the anteromedial portal and the saphenous structures; when it asks which nerve is most commonly injured, it is the superficial peroneal. Both are asked, and they have different answers.
  • Two more nerves worth knowing.
    • Morton's neuroma is a perineural fibrosis of a COMMON DIGITAL NERVE, most often in the THIRD web space, where the medial and lateral plantar nerve branches join and the nerve is thickest and least mobile, tethered under the deep transverse intermetatarsal ligament.
    • Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome is compression of the DEEP PERONEAL NERVE under the inferior extensor retinaculum, giving first web space numbness and EDB wasting.

Gait cycle

Phases, percentages and muscle activity.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2024S

  • The division of the cycle.
    • STANCE = ~60%, SWING = ~40% of one gait cycle (heel strike to ipsilateral heel strike).
    • DOUBLE-LIMB SUPPORT occupies ~20% of the cycle, in TWO periods of ~10% each, at the beginning and end of stance.
    • Double-limb support DISAPPEARS in running and is replaced by a FLOAT (double-float) phase — this is the defining difference between walking and running, not speed. As walking speed increases, the proportion spent in double support falls.
    • STANCE sub-phases: initial contact / heel strike (0%), loading response, midstance, terminal stance / heel off, pre-swing / toe off (~60%). SWING sub-phases: initial, mid and terminal swing.
  • Muscle activity, phase by phase.
    • TIBIALIS ANTERIOR fires ECCENTRICALLY at and just after heel strike to control the descent of the forefoot (preventing FOOT SLAP), and again concentrically in swing for ground clearance. Loss gives a steppage gait and foot slap.
    • QUADRICEPS act ECCENTRICALLY in loading response to control knee flexion; quadriceps weakness produces a back-knee (genu recurvatum) or hand-on-thigh gait.
    • GASTROCSOLEUS is active in TERMINAL STANCE for push-off (concentric plantarflexion), having acted eccentrically through midstance to control forward tibial progression.
    • HIP ABDUCTORS (gluteus medius) fire in SINGLE-LIMB STANCE to prevent contralateral pelvic drop. Weakness gives a Trendelenburg gait: the pelvis drops on the CONTRALATERAL (unsupported) side, and the trunk lurches TOWARD the affected side to move the centre of gravity over the weak hip.
    • Hamstrings decelerate the limb in terminal swing (eccentric) and extend the hip in early stance.
  • The numbers.
    • Cadence ~100–115 steps/min; walking velocity ~80 m/min (about 1.3 m/s); step length ~40 cm and stride length ~80 cm.
    • The CENTRE OF GRAVITY is LOWEST at DOUBLE SUPPORT and HIGHEST at MIDSTANCE, with a total vertical displacement of about 5 cm and a similar lateral displacement.
    • Energy efficiency depends on minimising displacement of the centre of gravity; the classic determinants of gait (pelvic rotation, pelvic tilt, knee flexion in stance, foot and knee mechanisms, and lateral pelvic displacement) all serve that end.
    • Oxygen consumption rises with proximal amputation: roughly +10–20% for a transtibial, +50–70% for a transfemoral, and above 100% for a hip disarticulation, with self-selected walking speed falling correspondingly.
  • Foot and subtalar mechanics through the cycle.
    • The subtalar joint PRONATES (everts) through loading response, which UNLOCKS the transverse tarsal (Chopart) joints by making the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid axes PARALLEL — a flexible foot that absorbs shock and accommodates terrain.
    • The subtalar joint SUPINATES (inverts) in terminal stance, which LOCKS the transverse tarsal joints by making those axes DIVERGENT — converting the midfoot into a rigid lever for push-off.
    • The WINDLASS MECHANISM reinforces this: MTP dorsiflexion in terminal stance tensions the plantar fascia, elevating the arch and inverting the hindfoot.
    • This is why a fixed hindfoot valgus (as in stage III flatfoot) prevents the midfoot from locking, and why a subtalar or triple arthrodesis in the wrong position destroys push-off.
  • Pathological gaits worth recognising by name.
    • ANTALGIC — shortened stance phase on the painful limb; the most common abnormal gait.
    • TRENDELENBURG — abductor insufficiency (superior gluteal nerve injury, hip abductor tear, THA with reduced offset). Compensated (Duchenne) Trendelenburg lurches the trunk over the affected hip.
    • STEPPAGE / DROP FOOT — exaggerated hip and knee flexion to clear a plantarflexed foot; common peroneal or L5 lesion.
    • VAULTING and CIRCUMDUCTION — compensations for a functionally long limb (stiff knee, poor prosthetic clearance).
    • CROUCH gait in cerebral palsy — excessive hip and knee flexion with ankle dorsiflexion, classically worsened by an isolated Achilles lengthening.
    • SHORT-LEG gait — ipsilateral pelvic drop and toe-walking; noticeable clinically at a discrepancy above about 2 cm.

Shoulder girdle & pectoralis major anatomy

Insertional anatomy relevant to repair and to TSA.

2 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S

  • Pectoralis major: two heads, and a tendon that twists 180°.
    • CLAVICULAR head — from the medial half of the clavicle. STERNOCOSTAL head — from the sternum, the upper six costal cartilages and the external oblique aponeurosis.
    • The tendon inserts on the LATERAL LIP OF THE BICIPITAL GROOVE as a BILAMINAR (two-layered) tendon.
    • The CLAVICULAR head inserts ANTERIORLY and more DISTALLY; the STERNAL head fibres TWIST to insert POSTERIORLY and more PROXIMALLY — the characteristic 180° twist. Restoring that layered relationship is the aim of an anatomic repair.
    • Innervation: LATERAL PECTORAL nerve (C5–C7) to the clavicular head; MEDIAL PECTORAL nerve (C8–T1) to the sternocostal head. Note that the medial pectoral nerve pierces pectoralis MINOR to reach pectoralis major.
    • Blood supply is chiefly the PECTORAL BRANCH OF THE THORACOACROMIAL ARTERY (from the second part of the axillary artery) — not the intercostal perforators, which supply it only segmentally and medially.
  • Rupture and repair.
    • Occurs at the humeral insertion or the musculotendinous junction, most often on ECCENTRIC loading during a bench press, and typically in men in their 20s–40s; anabolic steroid use is a risk factor.
    • The STERNAL head tears first, so the clinical picture is loss of the axillary fold contour with a preserved clavicular head, ecchymosis in the arm and chest, and weakness of adduction and internal rotation.
    • Acute anatomic repair (within 6 weeks) gives significantly better strength and outcome than delayed repair or non-operative treatment in the active patient; chronic tears may need allograft reconstruction.
    • Non-operative treatment is reasonable for intramuscular tears, low-demand patients and elderly patients — the strength deficit is roughly 25–40% in adduction.
  • The pectoralis major landmark in shoulder arthroplasty.
    • The distance from the SUPERIOR BORDER of the PECTORALIS MAJOR insertion to the TOP OF THE HUMERAL HEAD averages about 5.6 cm.
    • This is used to set humeral head height in arthroplasty for a fracture or for a proximal humerus with no reliable landmark — it is more reproducible than the calcar or the tuberosities in a comminuted fracture.
    • Related landmark: the tuberosity should sit roughly 5–8 mm BELOW the top of the prosthetic head; setting the implant too proud is the commonest error and causes tuberosity failure and stiffness.
  • Shoulder girdle anatomy that gets tested alongside it.
    • The CORACOID is the "lighthouse of the shoulder": medial to it lie the neurovascular structures, so all safe dissection stays lateral. Attached to it are pectoralis minor (medial), the conjoined tendon of coracobrachialis and the short head of biceps (lateral/inferior), and the coracoacromial, coracoclavicular and coracohumeral ligaments.
    • Coracoclavicular ligaments: the CONOID is MEDIAL and POSTERIOR (about 4.5 cm from the distal clavicle) and resists SUPERIOR translation; the TRAPEZOID is LATERAL and ANTERIOR (about 3 cm) and resists AXIAL compression / posterior translation.
    • The ACROMIOCLAVICULAR ligaments (particularly the superior and posterior) are the primary restraint to HORIZONTAL (anteroposterior) translation, while the coracoclavicular ligaments restrain VERTICAL translation — the basis of the Rockwood classification and of why type III–V injuries need both addressed.
    • The SUPRASCAPULAR NERVE goes UNDER the transverse scapular ligament while the SUPRASCAPULAR ARTERY goes OVER it ("the army goes over the bridge, the navy goes under").
    • Scapular winging: MEDIAL = long thoracic nerve / serratus anterior (worse with forward flexion and a wall push-up); LATERAL = spinal accessory nerve / trapezius (worse with abduction, with a drooping shoulder).
    • Latissimus dorsi and teres major both insert on the humerus near the bicipital groove — latissimus on the FLOOR (medial lip) of the groove, teres major on the MEDIAL LIP/crest of the lesser tuberosity, with the "lady between two majors" mnemonic placing latissimus anterior to teres major at the inferior border of the axilla.
  • Humeral head blood supply — the modern answer.
    • Classic teaching (Gerber) made the ANTEROLATERAL ASCENDING BRANCH of the ANTERIOR HUMERAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY, continuing as the ARCUATE ARTERY of Laing, the dominant supply to the humeral head.
    • Contemporary quantitative perfusion studies (Hettrich, Duparc and others) show the POSTERIOR HUMERAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY supplies the MAJORITY of the humeral head — commonly quoted at about 64%, with the anterior circumflex contributing roughly a third.
    • This explains why humeral head osteonecrosis is uncommon even after anterior circumflex disruption in a proximal humerus fracture, and why the posterior periosteal soft tissues must be respected.
    • The Hertel criteria predict ischaemia after proximal humerus fracture: a short calcar segment (<8 mm), a disrupted medial hinge, and a basic anatomic-neck fracture pattern — the medial calcar periosteal sleeve is what keeps the head perfused.

Spinal cord & nerve root anatomy

Tract anatomy and how it maps to incomplete cord syndromes.

1 of 7 sittings2020 (x2)

  • The three tracts that matter, and where each decussates.
    • DORSAL (POSTERIOR) COLUMNS — IPSILATERAL proprioception, vibration and light touch; decussate in the MEDULLA. Fasciculus gracilis (medial, lower limb) and cuneatus (lateral, upper limb).
    • LATERAL SPINOTHALAMIC TRACT — CONTRALATERAL pain and temperature; decussates WITHIN 1–2 LEVELS of entry, in the anterior white commissure. This near-immediate crossing is why a hemisection gives contralateral pain loss.
    • LATERAL CORTICOSPINAL TRACT — IPSILATERAL motor; decussates in the MEDULLA (the pyramidal decussation).
    • Two of the three cross in the medulla; only the spinothalamic crosses in the cord — that single fact reconstructs every cord syndrome.
  • Somatotopy, and the syndromes it explains.
    • In the LATERAL CORTICOSPINAL TRACT, CERVICAL / UPPER-LIMB fibres are the most MEDIAL and SACRAL fibres the most LATERAL. In the spinothalamic tract the arrangement is the reverse (sacral most lateral and superficial).
    • CENTRAL CORD SYNDROME — the commonest incomplete injury; classically an older patient with cervical spondylosis after a hyperextension injury. UPPER limbs affected more than lower, distal more than proximal, with variable sacral sparing. Best prognosis of the incomplete syndromes, though hand function often recovers last and least.
    • ANTERIOR CORD SYNDROME — loss of motor, pain and temperature with PRESERVED dorsal columns (proprioception and vibration); from anterior spinal artery infarction or a flexion-compression injury. The WORST prognosis of the incomplete syndromes (roughly 10–20% recovery).
    • BROWN-SÉQUARD — hemisection: IPSILATERAL motor and dorsal column loss, CONTRALATERAL pain and temperature loss. The BEST prognosis — most regain ambulation.
    • POSTERIOR CORD SYNDROME — rare; isolated loss of proprioception and vibration, with a wide-based sensory ataxic gait.
    • CONUS MEDULLARIS versus CAUDA EQUINA: the conus lesion is more symmetrical, with early and severe bowel/bladder involvement, saddle anaesthesia and mixed upper and lower motor neuron signs; the cauda equina lesion is asymmetrical, radicular, painful, and purely LOWER motor neuron.
  • Blood supply of the cord.
    • ONE anterior spinal artery supplies the ANTERIOR TWO-THIRDS (corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts, anterior horns).
    • TWO posterior spinal arteries supply the POSTERIOR ONE-THIRD (dorsal columns) — the paired supply is why posterior cord syndrome is rare.
    • The ARTERY OF ADAMKIEWICZ (great anterior radiculomedullary artery) is the dominant thoracolumbar feeder; it arises on the LEFT in about 80% and between T9 and L2 in most people.
    • The watershed zone is the MID-THORACIC cord (around T4–T9), where radicular supply is sparsest — the region most vulnerable to hypotension during deformity correction, and the reason mean arterial pressure is maintained above about 85 mmHg after an acute cord injury and during corrective surgery.
  • Cord level versus vertebral level, and roots.
    • The cord ends at the CONUS MEDULLARIS at about L1–L2 in adults (lower in children, reaching the adult level by about 2 years); below that is the CAUDA EQUINA, which is lower motor neuron and therefore has better recovery potential and no spasticity.
    • In the CERVICAL spine, the nerve root exits ABOVE its correspondingly numbered pedicle down to C7; the C8 root exits between C7 and T1, and from T1 downward the root exits BELOW its numbered pedicle.
    • Consequently a paracentral cervical disc herniation compresses the EXITING root of that level (C5–6 disc → C6 root), whereas in the lumbar spine a paracentral herniation compresses the TRAVERSING root (L4–5 disc → L5 root) and a FAR-LATERAL herniation compresses the EXITING root (L4–5 disc → L4 root).
    • Root landmarks: C5 deltoid and biceps (biceps reflex); C6 wrist extension and the thumb (brachioradialis reflex); C7 triceps, wrist flexion and finger extension (triceps reflex); C8 finger flexion; T1 interossei. L4 tibialis anterior and the medial leg (patellar reflex); L5 EHL and the first web space (no reflex); S1 gastrocsoleus and the lateral foot (Achilles reflex).
  • Classification, spinal shock and prognosis.
    • ASIA A complete (no motor or sensory function in S4–S5); B sensory incomplete; C motor incomplete with more than half the key muscles below the level grading <3; D motor incomplete with most key muscles ≥3; E normal.
    • SACRAL SPARING — voluntary anal contraction, perianal sensation, or deep anal pressure — is what defines an INCOMPLETE injury, and must be tested in every case.
    • The ZONE OF PARTIAL PRESERVATION applies ONLY to a complete (ASIA A) injury, and describes the dermatomes and myotomes below the neurological level that retain some function.
    • RETURN OF THE BULBOCAVERNOSUS REFLEX marks the END OF SPINAL SHOCK, typically within 24–48 hours. An accurate ASIA classification and prognosis cannot be given until spinal shock has resolved — a "complete" injury declared during spinal shock is not a valid prognosis.
    • Distinguish SPINAL shock (loss of reflexes, a transient physiological state) from NEUROGENIC shock (hypotension with BRADYCARDIA from loss of sympathetic outflow in injuries above T6) — neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of exclusion after haemorrhage has been ruled out, and is treated with vasopressors rather than volume alone.

Vascular anatomy: Medial femoral circumflex & femoral head blood supply

The blood supply of the femoral head and how to protect it.

2 of 7 sittings2023, 2025

  • The dominant supply: the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery.
    • The DEEP BRANCH OF THE MEDIAL FEMORAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY (MFCA) is the dominant supply to the ADULT femoral head, terminating as the SUPERIOR RETINACULAR (LATERAL EPIPHYSEAL) VESSELS, which supply the weight-bearing superolateral portion of the head.
    • Course: it arises from the PROFUNDA FEMORIS (occasionally directly from the femoral artery), passes between PECTINEUS and ILIOPSOAS, then runs along the inferior border of OBTURATOR EXTERNUS and up the posterior femoral neck.
    • The critical relationship: the deep branch passes POSTERIOR (deep) to the OBTURATOR EXTERNUS tendon and ANTERIOR (superficial) to the tendons of SUPERIOR GEMELLUS, OBTURATOR INTERNUS and INFERIOR GEMELLUS, before piercing the capsule at the superoposterior femoral neck at the level of the superior gemellus.
    • Therefore OBTURATOR EXTERNUS acts as a protective sling and MUST BE PRESERVED during surgical hip dislocation; the short external rotators lying superficial to the artery may be released. Dividing QUADRATUS FEMORIS devascularises the head and is the specific error to avoid in a posterior approach.
    • The terminal retinacular vessels run in the retinacula of Weitbrecht along the femoral neck, and are INTRACAPSULAR but EXTRAOSSEOUS — which is why an intracapsular fracture with displacement tears them and an extracapsular (intertrochanteric) fracture does not.
  • The minor contributors.
    • LATERAL FEMORAL CIRCUMFLEX ARTERY — supplies the ANTERIOR and INFERIOR retinacular vessels and a modest territory; its ascending branch is the vessel ligated in the Smith-Petersen interval.
    • ARTERY OF THE LIGAMENTUM TERES (foveal artery), a branch of the OBTURATOR artery — supplies a small area around the fovea. It is significant in young children and contributes little (roughly 10–20% at most, and often nothing) in adults, and cannot be relied upon to save a head.
    • The inferior gluteal artery contributes a variable anastomotic branch running with the piriformis tendon.
    • Ascending cervical branches form an extracapsular arterial ring at the base of the neck, from which the retinacular vessels ascend.
  • What is preserved in a displaced femoral neck fracture.
    • A severely displaced intracapsular femoral neck fracture disrupts the SUPERIOR RETINACULAR vessels — the dominant supply — which is why osteonecrosis rates after fixation of a displaced (Garden III–IV) fracture reach 20–35%, versus a few per cent for undisplaced fractures.
    • The supply most likely to be PRESERVED is the ARTERY OF THE LIGAMENTUM TERES (the foveal / medial epiphyseal vessels), because it enters through the fovea rather than crossing the fracture — but the territory it supplies is small, and its preservation does not prevent osteonecrosis.
    • Consequences that follow: anatomic reduction and stable fixation are urgent in the young patient (varus and retroversion malreduction is the strongest modifiable predictor of both non-union and osteonecrosis), and capsulotomy or aspiration to decompress the intracapsular haematoma has a biological rationale though the clinical evidence is mixed.
    • In the elderly, arthroplasty rather than fixation is the answer for a displaced fracture, precisely because the blood supply cannot be relied upon — and total hip arthroplasty outperforms hemiarthroplasty for function in the active, cognitively intact patient (HEALTH and Canadian-led trial evidence), at the cost of a higher dislocation rate.
  • The paediatric hip and SCFE.
    • In children, the PHYSIS IS A BARRIER to vessels crossing from the metaphysis, so after about age 4 the LATERAL EPIPHYSEAL (superior retinacular) vessels are effectively the SOLE supply to the capital epiphysis, and the foveal contribution becomes significant again only later in childhood.
    • This is the anatomical basis for Perthes disease, and for the vulnerability of the head in unstable SCFE, hip dislocation and femoral neck fracture in children.
    • UNSTABLE SCFE carries an osteonecrosis rate of roughly 20–50%, versus close to zero for a stable slip — the single most important prognostic distinction (Loder), defined by whether the child can bear weight even with crutches.
    • The modified Dunn procedure (via a surgical hip dislocation with preservation of the MFCA) allows anatomic realignment of a severe slip; it is technically demanding and the reported osteonecrosis rate varies widely between centres.
  • The Ganz surgical dislocation, restated as a vascular operation.
    • Trochanteric flip (digastric) osteotomy, anterior Z-capsulotomy, anterior dislocation — deliberately designed so that every step stays superficial to the deep branch of the MFCA.
    • Do not release obturator externus. Do not divide quadratus femoris. Release piriformis and the gemelli only lateral to their insertions if needed.
    • Correctly performed, the reported osteonecrosis rate is essentially zero — which is what makes it the exposure of choice for femoral head fractures, FAI with complex deformity, OCD of the head, and intra-articular tumour.

Adductor canal & thigh anatomy

Contents of the adductor (Hunter's) canal.

1 of 7 sittings2020

  • Boundaries of the adductor (Hunter's, subsartorial) canal.
    • It runs from the APEX OF THE FEMORAL TRIANGLE (where sartorius crosses adductor longus) to the ADDUCTOR HIATUS in adductor magnus, occupying the middle third of the thigh.
    • ANTEROLATERAL wall — VASTUS MEDIALIS.
    • POSTEROMEDIAL floor — ADDUCTOR LONGUS proximally and ADDUCTOR MAGNUS distally.
    • ROOF — the SARTORIUS and the subsartorial (vastoadductor) fascia stretched between vastus medialis and the adductors.
  • Contents — and, more usefully, what is NOT in it.
    • SUPERFICIAL FEMORAL ARTERY, FEMORAL VEIN, SAPHENOUS NERVE, and the NERVE TO VASTUS MEDIALIS.
    • The SCIATIC NERVE IS NOT IN THE ADDUCTOR CANAL — it lies in the posterior compartment, deep to the hamstrings and posterior to adductor magnus. It is therefore the structure LEAST at risk from an anteromedial mid-thigh penetrating injury, and this is the recurring answer.
    • The PROFUNDA FEMORIS and its perforating branches lie POSTERIOR to adductor longus, outside the canal, passing through adductor magnus — they are the vessels responsible for occult thigh haemorrhage in a femoral shaft fracture.
    • The femoral artery becomes the POPLITEAL ARTERY as it passes through the ADDUCTOR HIATUS; the saphenous nerve continues superficially without passing through the hiatus, emerging between sartorius and gracilis.
    • The saphenous nerve is purely SENSORY (medial leg and medial foot to the medial malleolus) — the terminal branch of the posterior division of the femoral nerve.
  • Clinical relevance: the adductor canal block.
    • An adductor canal block anaesthetises the saphenous nerve and, variably, the nerve to vastus medialis and articular branches to the anteromedial knee — it is predominantly SENSORY.
    • It therefore SPARES QUADRICEPS STRENGTH compared with a femoral nerve block, reducing the risk of falls while giving comparable analgesia after TKA — the basis of modern ERAS pathways.
    • It is usually combined with a posterior capsular infiltration (IPACK block) or periarticular local anaesthetic infiltration to cover the posterior knee, which the adductor canal block does not reach.
  • The femoral triangle, just proximal to the canal.
    • Boundaries: INGUINAL LIGAMENT superiorly, SARTORIUS laterally, ADDUCTOR LONGUS medially; the floor is iliopsoas and pectineus.
    • Contents from LATERAL to MEDIAL — "NAVEL": Nerve (femoral), Artery, Vein, Empty space (femoral canal), Lymphatics. The femoral NERVE lies OUTSIDE the femoral sheath; the artery, vein and canal lie within it.
    • The FEMORAL CANAL, medial to the vein, is the site of a femoral hernia and contains Cloquet's node.
    • The femoral nerve divides into anterior and posterior divisions about 4 cm distal to the inguinal ligament; the posterior division gives the saphenous nerve and the branch to rectus femoris.
  • Compartments of the thigh, for completeness.
    • ANTERIOR — quadriceps, sartorius, iliopsoas; FEMORAL nerve.
    • MEDIAL — adductor longus, brevis and magnus, gracilis, obturator externus; OBTURATOR nerve (with the hamstring part of adductor magnus supplied by the TIBIAL division of the sciatic — the classic dual innervation).
    • POSTERIOR — biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus; TIBIAL division of the SCIATIC nerve, except the SHORT HEAD OF BICEPS FEMORIS, which is supplied by the PERONEAL division — the standard "which hamstring is the odd one out" point.
    • Thigh compartment syndrome is uncommon because the compartments are large and compliant; when it occurs it is usually the anterior compartment, after a femoral shaft fracture, a crush injury, or external compression, and is released through a single lateral incision (with a separate medial incision if the adductor compartment is involved).

Hip musculature & secondary abductors

Primary and secondary abductors of the hip.

1 of 7 sittings2024S

  • The primary abductors — all superior gluteal nerve.
    • GLUTEUS MEDIUS — the principal abductor, providing roughly 60% of abduction torque; its posterior fibres also externally rotate and its anterior fibres internally rotate.
    • GLUTEUS MINIMUS — abduction and, importantly, a dynamic stabiliser that compresses the head into the acetabulum.
    • TENSOR FASCIA LATA — abducts and internally rotates through the iliotibial band.
    • All three are innervated by the SUPERIOR GLUTEAL NERVE (L4, L5, S1), which is why a single nerve injury abolishes abduction.
  • The secondary abductors — and the muscles that are not.
    • PIRIFORMIS, the SUPERIOR (upper) PORTION OF GLUTEUS MAXIMUS, SARTORIUS and RECTUS FEMORIS contribute to abduction in certain hip positions.
    • Note that piriformis is an external rotator in extension but becomes an ABDUCTOR (and internal rotator) with the hip FLEXED beyond about 60°, because its line of pull changes relative to the joint centre — the classic reason it appears in a secondary-abductor list.
    • NOT abductors: OBTURATOR EXTERNUS, ADDUCTOR MAGNUS, PECTINEUS, GRACILIS and the ADDUCTOR group — these are adductors, and one of them is the odd one out in any "which is not a secondary abductor" list.
    • The obturator internus and the gemelli are external rotators rather than abductors, though obturator internus contributes a small abduction moment in flexion.
  • Superior gluteal nerve injury.
    • Causes: an over-aggressive direct lateral (Hardinge) or anterolateral split, proximal retraction in the greater sciatic notch during a Kocher-Langenbeck approach, and iatrogenic injury during acetabular fracture surgery.
    • The nerve runs transversely between gluteus medius and minimus, roughly 3–5 cm PROXIMAL TO THE TIP OF THE GREATER TROCHANTER — the split must not extend beyond that.
    • The result is a TRENDELENBURG GAIT: the pelvis drops on the CONTRALATERAL (unsupported) side during single-limb stance, and the patient compensates by lurching the trunk TOWARD the affected side.
    • Unlike a muscle detachment, a nerve injury cannot be repaired — the deficit is often permanent, which is the argument for approaches that preserve the abductors entirely.
  • Offset, and why it is the abductor's mechanical advantage.
    • Femoral offset is the perpendicular distance from the centre of rotation of the femoral head to the long axis of the femur — and it is the ABDUCTOR MOMENT ARM.
    • Restoring or increasing offset LENGTHENS the abductor moment arm, so less abductor force is needed to balance the pelvis. That in turn REDUCES the joint reaction force, reduces polyethylene wear, improves abductor efficiency and reduces the risk of impingement and instability.
    • Failing to restore offset ("under-offsetting") is a common cause of a limp, abductor fatigue, trochanteric pain and instability after THA, and it cannot be fixed by lengthening the leg — lengthening tightens the abductors but does not restore the moment arm and creates a leg-length discrepancy instead.
    • Medialising the cup (with a corresponding increase in femoral offset) reduces the body-weight moment arm and further reduces joint reaction force; the two are considered together as "global offset".
    • In a single-leg stance free-body diagram, the abductor force is roughly 2–3 times body weight and the joint reaction force 2.5–4 times body weight, because the body-weight moment arm is about 2–3 times the abductor moment arm. Using a cane in the CONTRALATERAL hand reduces the joint reaction force by up to about 50%.
  • Abductor tendon tears — the "rotator cuff of the hip".
    • Gluteus medius and minimus tears at their greater trochanteric footprint are a common and underdiagnosed cause of greater trochanteric pain syndrome, and they mimic trochanteric bursitis.
    • Clinical clues: a positive Trendelenburg sign, weakness on the 30-second single-leg stance test, and a positive resisted external derotation test, with pain and tenderness over the trochanter.
    • MRI shows tendon signal change and fatty atrophy of gluteus minimus and the anterior medius; the degree of fatty infiltration predicts repairability, exactly as in the shoulder.
    • Management ranges from physiotherapy and injection to open or endoscopic repair, with gluteus maximus or vastus lateralis transfer for irreparable chronic tears.

Sesamoid anatomy & biomechanics

The hallux sesamoid complex.

1 of 7 sittings2020

  • What they are and where they sit.
    • Two sesamoids lie within the tendons of FLEXOR HALLUCIS BREVIS (a medial/tibial head and a lateral/fibular head), embedded in the PLANTAR PLATE of the first MTP joint.
    • The MEDIAL (TIBIAL) sesamoid is LARGER, more DISTALLY placed, and bears MORE WEIGHT — and is correspondingly more often fractured, more often the site of sesamoiditis, and more often affected by osteonecrosis.
    • The FHL TENDON RUNS IN THE GROOVE (crista) BETWEEN THEM, on the plantar surface of the first metatarsal head — the crista is the ridge that is eroded in long-standing hallux valgus, allowing the sesamoids to subluxate laterally.
    • The complex is stabilised by the medial and lateral sesamoidal (metatarsosesamoid) ligaments, the intersesamoid ligament, the plantar plate, the abductor hallucis (medial sesamoid) and the adductor hallucis (lateral sesamoid).
  • Function.
    • They INCREASE THE MOMENT ARM of flexor hallucis brevis by displacing the tendon away from the joint centre — a pulley effect that augments plantarflexion power at the MTP joint.
    • They absorb and distribute weightbearing load beneath the first metatarsal head and protect the FHL tendon from direct compression.
    • Load: they carry up to about 50% of body weight in stance and up to 3× body weight during push-off.
    • They also elevate the first metatarsal head off the ground, contributing to the tripod of the forefoot.
  • Sesamoid excursion and why it matters.
    • During gait the sesamoids translate DISTALLY with the metatarsal head as the MTP joint DORSIFLEXES, gliding along the plantar surface.
    • Loss of that excursion — from hallux rigidus, post-traumatic or post-surgical scarring, or arthrofibrosis — directly LIMITS MTP DORSIFLEXION, and is a common reason a cheilectomy fails to restore motion.
    • Restoring sesamoid glide (release of the sesamoid-metatarsal adhesions) is a specific step in revision hallux rigidus surgery.
  • Bipartite sesamoid: the great mimic.
    • A bipartite MEDIAL (tibial) sesamoid occurs in roughly 10–30% of people and is bilateral in a majority — bipartite lateral sesamoids are rare.
    • Distinguishing it from a fracture: a bipartite sesamoid has SMOOTH, SCLEROTIC, ROUNDED margins and the combined fragments are LARGER than a normal sesamoid; a fracture has SHARP, IRREGULAR, NON-CONGRUENT edges and the fragments reassemble into a normal-sized sesamoid.
    • An AXIAL (tangential) sesamoid view and comparison radiographs of the contralateral foot are the standard workup; MRI or bone scan resolves the doubt, and MRI is also the test for sesamoid osteonecrosis.
  • Pathology: turf toe, sesamoiditis, fracture and osteonecrosis.
    • TURF TOE is a hyperextension injury to the plantar plate and sesamoid complex, classically on an unyielding surface in a flexible shoe.
    • PROXIMAL MIGRATION of the sesamoids on a lateral radiograph (or failure of the sesamoids to translate distally on a dorsiflexion stress lateral) indicates a COMPLETE PLANTAR PLATE DISRUPTION — and, with a diastasis of a bipartite sesamoid or a fracture, is an indication for operative repair.
    • Grade I (stretch) is treated with taping and a stiff-soled shoe or turf-toe plate; grade II (partial tear) with a walking boot; grade III (complete tear, with sesamoid migration, a diastasis, or an associated articular injury) with repair.
    • Sesamoid excision is a last resort. Excising the TIBIAL sesamoid risks HALLUX VALGUS; excising the FIBULAR sesamoid risks HALLUX VARUS; excising BOTH produces a COCK-UP (clawed) HALLUX from loss of the FHB insertion and must be avoided.
    • Alternatives that preserve the complex: partial (shaving) excision, bone grafting of a non-union, or a plantar-flexing (dorsiflexion) first metatarsal osteotomy to offload the sesamoids.

Spring ligament complex

Components and role in the medial longitudinal arch.

1 of 7 sittings2020

  • The three components of the complex.
    • SUPEROMEDIAL CALCANEONAVICULAR LIGAMENT — the largest and most important. It runs from the sustentaculum tali to the superomedial navicular, forming a fibrocartilaginous SLING beneath and medial to the TALAR HEAD, and it blends with the tibiocalcaneal portion of the superficial deltoid.
    • MEDIOPLANTAR OBLIQUE calcaneonavicular ligament.
    • INFEROPLANTAR LONGITUDINAL calcaneonavicular ligament.
    • Together they span the gap between the anterior and middle facets of the calcaneus and the navicular, completing the socket of the "acetabulum pedis" for the talar head, along with the talonavicular capsule and the anterior/middle subtalar facets.
  • Function: the STATIC restraint under the talar head.
    • It supports the head of the talus and maintains the MEDIAL LONGITUDINAL ARCH.
    • It is the STATIC restraint to PLANTAR-MEDIAL subluxation of the talar head; the POSTERIOR TIBIAL TENDON is the principal DYNAMIC restraint. That pairing is the single most examinable point about the ligament.
    • The plantar fascia and the long and short plantar ligaments are the other static arch supports, working as a truss with the windlass mechanism.
    • Because the superomedial component contains fibrocartilage where the talar head rests on it, it behaves partly like an articular surface and heals poorly once attenuated.
  • Why it matters in progressive collapsing foot deformity.
    • Chronic posterior tibial tendon dysfunction transfers load to the static restraints, and the spring ligament ATTENUATES — the deformity then becomes a bony and ligamentous problem rather than a tendon problem.
    • The flatfoot deformity does NOT fully correct with tendon reconstruction (FDL transfer) alone once the spring ligament has failed — spring ligament repair or reconstruction, together with a bony realignment (medialising calcaneal osteotomy, lateral column lengthening, or Cotton osteotomy), is required.
    • It is the structure whose failure allows the talar head to become palpable and visible medially — the "peek-a-boo" or medial talar head prominence — and it drives the talonavicular uncoverage measured on the weightbearing AP.
    • Contemporary understanding places spring ligament and deltoid failure at the centre of PCFD, which is why the classification has moved away from the tendon-centred Johnson-Strom staging toward a deformity-based system (flexible/rigid, with hindfoot valgus, midfoot abduction, forefoot varus, medial column instability and ankle valgus each assessed separately).
  • The deltoid connection.
    • The SUPEROMEDIAL spring ligament is CONTIGUOUS with the TIBIOCALCANEAL portion of the SUPERFICIAL DELTOID — they form a continuous medial soft-tissue sleeve.
    • This anatomical continuity is why DELTOID INSUFFICIENCY AND SPRING LIGAMENT FAILURE OFTEN COEXIST in advanced valgus hindfoot deformity, and why valgus talar tilt within the ankle mortise (stage IV) is the endpoint of an untreated flatfoot.
    • Practically: in a stage IV deformity, correcting the hindfoot without addressing the deltoid leaves the ankle valgus, and a total ankle arthroplasty in that setting fails by edge loading.
  • Assessment and reconstruction.
    • MRI is the imaging test of choice for the spring ligament, though it under-calls partial attenuation; weightbearing radiographs and weightbearing CT quantify the resulting deformity (talonavicular coverage angle, Meary's angle, calcaneal pitch, hindfoot moment arm).
    • Intra-operative assessment — the ligament is inspected through the medial approach used for the FDL transfer, and laxity is confirmed by the ease of plantar-medial translation of the talar head.
    • Reconstruction options: direct imbrication/repair, augmentation with the FDL or peroneus longus, an internal brace, or a free tendon graft from the sustentaculum to the navicular; all are done in combination with bony correction, never in isolation.

Frequently tested positional relationships in the arm and forearm.

1 of 7 sittings2022

    • The RADIAL nerve enters the arm through the TRIANGULAR INTERVAL (teres major above, long head of triceps medially, humerus laterally), runs POSTERIORLY in the SPIRAL GROOVE roughly 14 cm proximal to the LATERAL epicondyle and 20 cm proximal to the MEDIAL epicondyle, then pierces the LATERAL INTERMUSCULAR SEPTUM about 10 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle to lie anteriorly between brachialis and brachioradialis.

  • The distal forearm, radial to ulnar.
    • RADIAL ARTERY → FCR → PALMARIS LONGUS → MEDIAN NERVE (deep and just ULNAR to palmaris longus) → FDS → ULNAR ARTERY → ULNAR NERVE.

    • Contents: 9 TENDONS (4 FDS, 4 FDP, 1 FPL) and the MEDIAN NERVE — the ulnar nerve and artery are OUTSIDE, in Guyon's canal.
    • The MEDIAN NERVE is the MOST SUPERFICIAL and most RADIAL structure in the tunnel, lying immediately deep to the transverse carpal ligament — which is why it is the structure injured by an incompletely controlled blade.
    • The RECURRENT MOTOR BRANCH classically arises EXTRALIGAMENTOUSLY and RADIALLY (about 50%), but there are well-described subligamentous (~30%) and TRANSLIGAMENTOUS (~20%) variants — the transligamentous branch pierces the flexor retinaculum and is the one divided during a release, so keep the release at the ULNAR edge of the ligament.
    • Within the tunnel the FDS tendons to the middle and ring fingers lie most superficial (volar), with the index and small FDS deeper — relevant to the distribution of pressure on the nerve.
  • Anatomical snuffbox and the dorsal wrist.
    • Boundaries: EPB and APL (first dorsal compartment) radially, EPL (third compartment) ulnarly; the floor is the SCAPHOID and trapezium, and the RADIAL ARTERY crosses the floor.
    • The SUPERFICIAL RADIAL NERVE branches cross the snuffbox subcutaneously — at risk with a dorsal scaphoid approach, a first dorsal compartment (de Quervain) release, and a percutaneous scaphoid screw.
    • The EPL turns around LISTER'S TUBERCLE, which is why it ruptures after a minimally displaced distal radius fracture (attritional and ischaemic) and after dorsal plating with a prominent screw.
    • The six dorsal extensor compartments: (1) APL/EPB, (2) ECRL/ECRB, (3) EPL, (4) EDC/EIP, (5) EDM, (6) ECU.

    • The ANTERIOR INTEROSSEOUS NERVE is a PURE MOTOR branch of the median nerve — FPL, FDP to the index and middle, and pronator quadratus. Weakness of the "OK sign" with NO sensory loss.
    • The PIN is a PURE MOTOR branch of the radial nerve — finger drop with wrist extension preserved but in RADIAL DEVIATION, and NO sensory loss (ECRL is supplied by the radial nerve proper, ECU by the PIN).
    • The brachial artery bifurcates into the radial and ulnar arteries at the level of the radial neck; the ulnar artery gives the common interosseous, which divides into anterior and posterior interosseous arteries.
    • The DEEP palmar arch is formed mainly by the RADIAL artery and lies PROXIMAL; the SUPERFICIAL arch is formed mainly by the ULNAR artery and lies DISTAL — the superficial arch lies at the level of the fully extended thumb (Kaplan's cardinal line).
    • Allen's test before radial artery harvest or cannulation confirms an intact ulnar contribution; the arches are incomplete in a substantial minority.
