Bone cement (PMMA): Properties, technique & complications

PMMA material behaviour, modern cementing technique, antibiotic elution and the mechanism of cemented stem failure.

6 of 7 sittings2021, 2022 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2024S (x3), 2024F, 2025 (x2)

  • PMMA is a grout, not a glue — the single conceptual point behind most cement questions:
    • It fixes by mechanical interdigitation into cancellous bone and implant surface features. There is no chemical adhesion to bone or to metal.
    • It is strongest in compression, much weaker in tension and weakest in shear — so a cemented construct must be designed to load the mantle in compression.
    • Young's modulus (~2–3 GPa) sits between cortical bone (~15–20 GPa) and metal (110–200 GPa), so the mantle acts as a load-transfer buffer/shock absorber.
    • It is a brittle material — it fails at or near its yield point with little plastic deformation, and it is highly notch- and void-sensitive.
  • What is actually in the packet:
    • Powder — pre-polymerised PMMA beads, benzoyl peroxide (initiator), and a radio-opacifier: barium sulphate or zirconium dioxide.
    • Liquid — methyl methacrylate monomer, N,N-dimethyl-p-toluidine (accelerator) and hydroquinone (stabiliser, prevents premature polymerisation in the vial).
    • Radio-opacifier is a third-body wear source if cement debris reaches the articulation, and slightly weakens the cement.
  • Polymerisation is a free-radical, exothermic reaction passing through four phases:
    • Mixing → doughing → working → setting. Total working time is roughly 8–10 minutes at room temperature.
    • Peak exotherm in vitro is ~80–110°C; in vivo the interface temperature is far lower (~40–70°C) because blood flow, the implant and bone act as heat sinks. Thermal necrosis is real but is not the main mode of failure.
    • Cooling the monomer lengthens working time; a warm theatre or warm components shorten it — a practical point when a mantle sets before the stem is fully seated.
  • Modern (third/fourth generation) cementing technique — each step exists to improve interdigitation or reduce porosity:
    • Pulsatile lavage of the canal to clear marrow fat and blood — the single most important step for interdigitation.
    • Distal canal plug to allow pressurisation and control mantle length.
    • Retrograde filling with a cement gun (never finger-packing) to avoid laminations and air.
    • Sustained pressurisation during and after filling.
    • Vacuum mixing or centrifugation to reduce porosity — porosity is the dominant determinant of fatigue strength.
    • Stem centraliser to guarantee a uniform mantle. Fourth generation adds proximal and distal centralisation.
    • Target a uniform mantle ≥2 mm; thin (<2 mm) or deficient mantles, voids and blood laminations are the classic radiographic failure predictors.
  • Barrack classification of cement mantle quality (femoral, on the immediate post-op radiograph):
    • A — complete "white-out", no cement–bone interface visible.
    • B — slight radiolucency at the cement–bone interface.
    • C — radiolucency in 50–99% of the interface, or a mantle defect.
    • D — radiolucency in 100% of the interface, or no cement distal to the stem tip. C and D predict early loosening.
  • How a cemented stem actually fails — know the sequence:
    • Debonding at the stem–cement interface (the weakest link in a polished-taper or a roughened stem alike).
    • → cement mantle fracture → particulate cement debris → osteolysis.
    • → subsidence into varus with a distal cement pedestal and progressive radiolucency (Gruen zones 1 and 7 first).
    • Two competing philosophies: composite-beam (roughened/precoated stem, bonded, fails badly once debonded) versus taper-slip / force-closed (polished collarless double-taper, designed to subside a controlled amount and load the mantle in compression). Roughening a taper-slip stem is a known cause of catastrophic failure.
  • Antibiotic-loaded cement — elution is a surface phenomenon:
    • Release is biphasic: a large early burst over the first 24–48 h, then a low-level exponential tail; the great majority of the drug is never released.
    • Low dose (≤1 g per 40 g packet, factory-mixed) = prophylaxis. High dose (≥2–4 g per 40 g, hand-mixed) = treatment / spacers only — it substantially reduces mechanical strength.
    • Antibiotics must be heat-stable, powder form and water-soluble: vancomycin, tobramycin, gentamicin. Avoid liquid formulations (they act as a plasticiser).
    • Elution is increased by higher porosity and larger surface area — so vacuum mixing, which improves strength, reduces elution. That trade-off is the exam point.
    • Systemic toxicity (nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity) is reported with very high-dose spacers, particularly with renal impairment.
  • Bone cement implantation syndrome (BCIS) — a named, gradeable event, not just "a drop in pressure":
    • Hypotension, hypoxaemia, arrhythmia, loss of consciousness or cardiac arrest at pressurisation, prosthesis insertion, joint reduction or tourniquet deflation.
    • Mechanism is embolisation of marrow fat, air and cement raising pulmonary vascular resistance, plus a humoral/anaphylactoid component — not simple monomer toxicity.
    • Highest risk: cemented hemiarthroplasty for a hip fracture in a frail elderly patient with cardiopulmonary disease, pathologic fracture or a wide, osteoporotic canal.
    • Mitigation: warn anaesthesia before pressurising, correct hypovolaemia, thorough lavage and suction/venting of the canal, and avoid over-pressurising a fragile femur.
    • This is not a reason to abandon cement in the elderly — national registry and randomised data show cemented hemiarthroplasty has fewer periprosthetic fractures and better early function than uncemented (WHiTE 5; NICE guidance).
  • Cementless is not simply "the modern option" — the distinction that gets tested:
    • Cementless fixation needs biologic ingrowth: pore size 100–400 µm, porosity 40–50%, gaps <50 µm (ideally <150 µm) and micromotion <20–40 µm; over ~150 µm of micromotion produces fibrous ingrowth instead.
    • Cement gives immediate fixation and full weight-bearing regardless of bone quality — which is why it remains the answer for the osteoporotic femur, pathologic bone and after irradiation.

Biomechanics: Plate fixation principles (locking, LC-DCP, buttress, pre-tension)

What each plate function does mechanically, why locked constructs behave differently, and the classic construct errors.

6 of 7 sittings2021 (x2), 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2024F (x2), 2025 (x2)

  • Learn the five plate FUNCTIONS, not the plate names — the same plate can perform several:
    • Compression — absolute stability across a transverse/short-oblique fracture; eccentric screw placement in a DCP hole drives the bone toward the fracture (~1 mm per hole).
    • Neutralisation (protection) — a lag screw provides the compression, the plate protects it from bending/torsion. Put the lag screw in first.
    • Buttress / antiglide — resists axial and shear collapse at a metaphyseal or oblique fracture by converting shear into compression.
    • Bridging — relative stability spanning a comminuted zone; healing is by callus, and the comminuted segment is left undisturbed.
    • Tension band — a plate on the tension side of an eccentrically loaded bone converts tension into compression at the far cortex (e.g. lateral femur, dorsal olecranon).
  • Locked plates are internal fixators — a fundamentally different mechanism:
    • The screw head threads lock into the plate, so the construct is a fixed-angle device and does not depend on plate–bone friction.
    • Advantages: preserves periosteal blood supply (the plate need not be pressed onto bone), resists loss of reduction in osteoporotic or metaphyseal bone, and does not require precise contouring.
    • Locking a plate does not reduce the fracture — the reduction must be achieved before the locking screws go in. This is the commonest exam distractor.
    • You cannot use a locking screw to lag, and you cannot mix a locking screw before a non-locking screw has pulled the plate down if you want compression — place non-locking screws first.
    • Disadvantage: a very stiff construct over a short working length suppresses callus — the classic locked distal femur nonunion.
  • LC-DCP vs DCP — what the "limited contact" undercut actually buys:
    • Reduced plate–periosteum contact area → better cortical perfusion and less cortical porosis under the plate.
    • More even distribution of stiffness along the plate — the undercuts remove the stress risers at the screw holes, so the plate bends evenly rather than at a hole.
    • Symmetric, trapezoidal hole geometry allows compression in either direction and greater screw angulation (up to ~40° longitudinally).
    • It does not make the plate stronger, and it does not eliminate the need for good technique.
  • Pre-bending (pre-tensioning) a compression plate — the geometry point:
    • A perfectly straight plate on a transverse fracture compresses the near (plate-side) cortex and gaps the far cortex.
    • Slightly over-bending the plate at the fracture (apex away from bone) means that as the screws are tightened the far cortex closes and compresses too.
    • Confirming far-cortex compression is the goal of absolute-stability plating; a gapped far cortex is a fatigue failure waiting to happen.
  • Buttress / antiglide detail — commonly tested in ankle and tibial plateau work:
    • The plate must sit on the apex/vertex of the oblique fracture line and be fixed proximal to it, so the distal fragment cannot slide up the incline.
    • An under-contoured plate acts as an antiglide; an over-contoured one does not.
    • Classic applications: posterolateral antiglide plate for a Weber B fibula (fewer symptomatic implants and no screws in the joint, but a risk of peroneal tendon irritation), and medial buttress for a split-depression plateau.
  • Far cortical locking / dynamic locking screws:
    • Engineered to reduce near-cortex stiffness so the construct deflects more symmetrically at both cortices.
    • The problem they solve: a laterally applied locked plate produces asymmetric motion — the far (medial) cortex moves and the near cortex does not — which produces asymmetric or absent callus and nonunion.
    • Other ways to soften a locked construct: longer plate, fewer and more spread-out screws, omit screws adjacent to the fracture, titanium instead of steel.
  • Material choice — titanium vs stainless steel:
    • Modulus: stainless steel ~200 GPa; titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4V) ~110 GPa; cortical bone ~15–20 GPa. A titanium plate of identical geometry is roughly half as stiff.
    • Titanium: better biocompatibility and osseointegration, fewer MRI artefacts, but notch-sensitive and prone to cold welding / seizing of locking heads (a well-known removal problem).
    • Stainless steel: stronger, more ductile, easier to contour and re-contour, cheaper — but more artefact and greater stress shielding.
    • Plate stiffness scales with thickness cubed (area moment of inertia ≈ bh³/12), so a small increase in thickness dominates any material choice.
  • Screw pull-out and construct rules of thumb:
    • Pull-out strength depends on outer (thread) diameter, thread depth and bone quality, not on the core diameter. Core diameter governs the screw's bending/shear strength.
    • Bicortical purchase and larger outer diameter improve pull-out; in cancellous bone a wide-pitch, deep-thread screw is used.
    • Aim for ≥6 cortices (3 bicortical screws) either side of a diaphyseal fracture in healthy bone, more in osteoporotic bone.
    • Plate span ratio >2–3 in comminuted fractures and >8–10 in simple ones; plate screw density <0.5 (fill fewer than half the holes) for a bridging construct.
  • Absolute vs relative stability determines the healing pathway — and mixing them is the commonest conceptual error:
    • Absolute stability (interfragmentary compression, strain <2%) → primary/direct bone healing through cutting cones, no visible callus. Any callus at a compression construct means it has failed.
    • Relative stability (bridging, strain ~2–10%) → secondary healing with abundant callus.
    • A construct that is too stiff to make callus but not stiff enough to be absolutely stable is the nonunion zone.

Perioperative medicine: Analgesia, opioids & ERAS

Multimodal analgesia, opioid stewardship, enhanced-recovery elements, and the medication-management questions (DMARDs, GLP-1 agonists, penicillin labels).

4 of 7 sittings2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S (x4), 2024F (x3)

  • Multimodal analgesia is the standard — several drugs at different points in the pain pathway, opioid last:
    • Acetaminophen scheduled (not PRN) + an NSAID/COX-2 unless contraindicated: together they cut opioid consumption by roughly 30–50%.
    • Regional: peripheral block or periarticular local infiltration analgesia (LIA).
    • Adjuncts: dexamethasone (analgesic + antiemetic, one or two doses), gabapentinoids (weak evidence, sedation in the elderly — largely fallen out of routine ERAS protocols), ketamine for opioid-tolerant patients.
    • Short-course, small-quantity opioid for breakthrough only.
  • Blocks in arthroplasty — the motor-sparing principle:
    • LIA is at least equivalent to femoral nerve block in TKA without quadriceps weakness, and therefore without the associated fall risk.
    • Adductor canal block has largely replaced femoral block: comparable analgesia with preserved quadriceps strength.
    • IPACK (interspace between popliteal artery and capsule of the knee) adds posterior capsular coverage without motor block.
    • Spinal (neuraxial) anaesthesia for hip and knee arthroplasty is associated with less blood loss, less delirium, fewer respiratory complications and lower early mortality in large observational series — though the randomised REGAIN trial found no difference in ambulation or 60-day mortality after hip fracture surgery.
  • Opioid stewardship — the numbers that get tested:
    • Pre-operative opioid use is the single strongest predictor of persistent post-operative opioid use, ahead of pain severity, procedure and comorbidity.
    • Other risks: mental-health comorbidity (depression, anxiety, catastrophising), substance-use disorder, benzodiazepine co-prescription, and a larger initial prescription size or duration.
    • Prescription size, not pain, drives consumption — patients consume a fixed fraction of what they are given, and roughly 60–70% of prescribed tablets go unused, creating a diversion pool.
    • The risk of persistent use climbs steeply after about 5 days of continuous opioid supply.
    • Canadian guidance (2017 Canadian Opioid Guideline) recommends against initiating opioids for chronic non-cancer pain in patients with active substance use or a mental-health disorder, and suggests a ceiling below 90 mg morphine equivalents/day.
  • ERAS in arthroplasty — the components with real evidence:
    • Pre-op: education/expectation setting, optimisation (anaemia, HbA1c, smoking, BMI, dental), carbohydrate loading and avoidance of prolonged fasting (clear fluids to 2 h, solids to 6 h).
    • Intra-op: neuraxial anaesthesia, TXA, LIA, normothermia, no routine drains, no routine urinary catheter, judicious fluid.
    • Post-op: multimodal analgesia, mobilisation the day of surgery, early oral intake, structured discharge criteria.
    • Net effect: shorter length of stay and fewer complications without an increase in readmission, enabling outpatient/same-day arthroplasty in selected patients.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide):
    • They delay gastric emptying, so a patient may have retained solid gastric contents despite standard fasting → aspiration risk.
    • Current anaesthesia guidance: hold weekly agents for 1 week and daily agents for 1 day before elective surgery; if not held, consider gastric ultrasound, a longer clear-fluid fast, and rapid-sequence induction.
    • Do not simply cancel the case reflexively — risk-stratify and modify the airway plan.
  • Antirheumatic drugs around surgery (ACR/AAHKS guideline) — a frequently tested list:
    • Continue conventional synthetic DMARDs through surgery: methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, leflunomide. Stopping methotrexate increases flares without reducing infection.
    • Hold biologics (TNF inhibitors, rituximab, tocilizumab, abatacept, ustekinumab) and schedule surgery at the end of the dosing cycle — e.g. weekly agents operate in week 2, and infliximab q8-weekly operates in week 9.
    • Hold JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib) for 3 days before surgery.
    • Restart biologics at ~14 days post-operatively, once the wound shows no drainage, swelling or erythema and there is no systemic infection.
    • Continue the daily glucocorticoid at the usual dose (do not give supraphysiologic "stress-dose" steroid for routine arthroplasty in a patient on ≤16 mg/day prednisone); taper chronic steroid to the lowest feasible dose pre-operatively.
    • For SLE, continue severe-disease drugs; for non-severe SLE hold mycophenolate/azathioprine/cyclosporine.
  • Reported penicillin allergy — delabelling is the correct answer:
    • Around 10% of patients carry a penicillin-allergy label but >90% of them tolerate penicillin on formal testing; most labels reflect childhood rash, GI upset or a viral exanthem.
    • Substituting vancomycin or clindamycin is associated with higher surgical site infection rates (roughly 50% higher in the large Blumenthal cohort), plus more C. difficile and AKI.
    • Cefazolin cross-reactivity with penicillin is <1% — it shares no R1 side chain with penicillin. Cefazolin is safe in almost all patients with a non-severe penicillin label.
    • Reserve vancomycin for a documented IgE-mediated reaction (anaphylaxis, angioedema, urticaria within an hour) or a severe delayed reaction (SJS/TEN, DRESS) — and even then, refer for allergy assessment rather than perpetuating the label.
  • Other perioperative optimisation targets:
    • HbA1c <7.5–8% and a peri-operative glucose <10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL); fructosamine <292 µmol/L is a better short-term marker.
    • Smoking cessation ≥4 weeks pre-operatively reduces wound complications; nicotine impairs osteoblast function and fracture healing.
    • Preoperative anaemia is the strongest modifiable predictor of transfusion — treat iron deficiency rather than transfusing peri-operatively.
    • Malnutrition: albumin <35 g/L, total lymphocyte count <1500/mm³, transferrin <200 mg/dL predict wound complications and infection.
    • Frailty and cognitive screening predict delirium; delirium prevention bundles (orientation, sleep, early mobility, avoiding anticholinergics) matter more than any single drug.

Surgical site infection prevention & antibiotic prophylaxis

What is proven, what is not, and the specific prophylaxis numbers (agent, timing, dose, redosing, duration).

5 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2024S, 2024F (x3), 2025

  • The prophylaxis prescription — memorise the numbers:
    • Agent: a first-generation cephalosporin, cefazolin, for essentially all orthopaedic surgery.
    • Timing: within 60 minutes of incision (120 minutes for vancomycin and fluoroquinolones, which need a slow infusion), and before tourniquet inflation.
    • Dose (weight-based): 2 g, or 3 g if >120 kg; paediatric 30 mg/kg.
    • Redosing: at approximately two half-lives — cefazolin every 4 hours intra-operatively — or after blood loss exceeding ~1500 mL.
    • Duration: ≤24 hours post-operatively (many guidelines now say a single pre-operative dose is sufficient). Continuing antibiotics until drains are removed is wrong and increases resistance and C. difficile.
  • Measures with good supporting evidence:
    • Chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation, superior to povidone–iodine for clean surgery.
    • Hair removal with clippers, or not at all — razors increase SSI by creating microabrasions.
    • Maintaining normothermia and adequate tissue oxygenation.
    • Peri-operative glycaemic control (glucose <10 mmol/L) in diabetics and non-diabetics.
    • Nasal S. aureus screening and decolonisation with mupirocin ± chlorhexidine body wash — reduces staphylococcal SSI; universal decolonisation is a reasonable alternative to screen-and-treat.
    • Pre-operative chlorhexidine bathing/wipes the night before and morning of surgery.
  • Measures that are NOT supported (classic "which is least useful" answers):
    • Prolonged post-operative antibiotics or antibiotics until drain removal.
    • Iodine-impregnated incise drapes as a stand-alone measure.
    • Routine substitution of vancomycin for cefazolin because of an unverified penicillin label — this raises SSI risk.
    • Laminar flow: registry and observational data show no benefit, and possibly harm, in hip and knee arthroplasty — a reversal of long-standing teaching and a favourite exam item.
    • Routine pre-operative shaving, and shaving on the ward the night before, are the worst options.
  • Intrawound vancomycin powder:
    • Widely used in spine (especially instrumented and deformity) and increasingly in trauma (the VANCO trial in tibial plateau/pilon fractures).
    • Consistently reduces gram-positive infection but is associated with a shift toward gram-negative and polymicrobial infections.
    • Reported concerns: seroma, potential impairment of osteoblast function/fusion at high concentrations, and rare systemic absorption.
    • The usual exam answer: "reduces gram-positive infection but selects for gram-negative organisms."
  • Dental antibiotic prophylaxis after joint replacement:
    • The current AAOS/ADA position is that routine prophylaxis is not required for most patients with a prosthetic joint before dental procedures.
    • It may be considered in the immunocompromised, poorly controlled diabetics, those with a prior prosthetic joint infection, and possibly within the first 1–2 years — as shared decision-making with the dentist.
    • Good dental health matters more than prophylaxis; active dental infection should be treated before elective arthroplasty.
  • Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes — the organism with its own rules:
    • A slow-growing, anaerobic, gram-positive rod that lives in the sebaceous glands of the dermis of the upper chest, shoulder and back — so it is not eliminated by surface skin preparation.
    • The dominant pathogen in shoulder arthroplasty infection, and far more common in men (greater sebaceous gland density and hair follicle density).
    • Presents indolently: pain and stiffness, often with normal CRP/ESR and a normal-looking wound — the classic "aseptic" loosening that grows an organism.
    • Cultures must be held 13–14 days; a single positive culture may be a contaminant, so multiple tissue samples are taken.
    • Benzoyl peroxide topically pre-operatively reduces the load; chlorhexidine alone does not. It is susceptible to cefazolin/penicillin and vancomycin, so standard prophylaxis is appropriate.
  • Patient-level risk factors to optimise before an elective case:
    • BMI >40, HbA1c >7.5–8%, active smoking, malnutrition (albumin <35 g/L), active S. aureus colonisation, uncontrolled inflammatory arthropathy, IV drug use, and untreated dental/skin/urinary infection.
    • Asymptomatic bacteriuria does NOT need treatment before arthroplasty and does not increase periprosthetic joint infection — another common distractor.
    • In spine surgery, the factors most strongly associated with infection are patient factors (diabetes, obesity, malnutrition, prior surgery/instrumentation, long constructs to the sacrum, prolonged operative time) rather than theatre logistics.
  • Open fracture antibiotics run on a separate protocol:
    • Antibiotics as soon as possible — ideally within 1 hour of injury; this matters more than time to debridement.
    • Gustilo I–II: a first-generation cephalosporin. Gustilo III: add gram-negative coverage (aminoglycoside or piperacillin-tazobactam). Farm/soil or vascular injury: add penicillin for clostridial coverage.
    • Tetanus status must be addressed.
    • FLOW (Canadian-led, NEJM 2015): very-low-pressure irrigation with normal saline is as good as high pressure, and saline beats castile soap (soap had a higher reoperation rate).

Compartment syndrome: Diagnosis & management

Clinical diagnosis versus pressure monitoring, the ΔP threshold, at-risk injuries, and the technique and timing of fasciotomy.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S

  • The pathophysiology in one line: raised interstitial pressure within a closed osteofascial compartment reduces the arteriovenous perfusion gradient, not arterial inflow — which is why pulses are almost always present.
    • Muscle ischaemia begins at roughly 4 hours; irreversible necrosis by 6–8 hours.
    • Nerve is the most sensitive tissue — paraesthesia in the distribution of a nerve traversing the compartment (e.g. first web space, deep peroneal) is the earliest objective sign.
  • In the awake patient the diagnosis is CLINICAL:
    • Pain out of proportion and an escalating analgesic requirement.
    • Pain on passive stretch of the muscles in the compartment — the most sensitive clinical sign.
    • A tense, swollen compartment on palpation.
    • Pallor, paralysis, pulselessness and poikilothermia are LATE — waiting for them means the limb is already lost.
    • Serial examination by the same examiner beats a single pressure reading.
  • Pressure monitoring is for the patient who cannot be examined — obtunded, intubated, intoxicated, regional block, very young:
    • Use ΔP = diastolic blood pressure − compartment pressure. ΔP <30 mmHg is the threshold for fasciotomy (McQueen).
    • Measure within 5 cm of the fracture, where pressure is highest; measure all compartments.
    • Absolute thresholds (30 or 45 mmHg) over-diagnose and lead to unnecessary fasciotomy — the delta is the answer.
    • Continuous monitoring has a much better specificity than a single reading; a single high reading in isolation should prompt repeat measurement, not immediate surgery, if the patient is examinable.
    • Hypotension lowers the threshold at which ischaemia occurs — resuscitate the patient; do not let a low diastolic pressure be uncorrected.
  • Injuries and patients at highest risk:
    • Tibial shaft fracture is the commonest cause overall (~1–10%), particularly proximal third and high-energy patterns.
    • Young men under 35 are disproportionately affected — greater muscle bulk within a fixed compartment volume.
    • Forearm (paediatric supracondylar and both-bone fractures), tibial plateau Schatzker VI, knee dislocation and vascular injury with reperfusion, crush injury, and prolonged lithotomy positioning.
    • An open fracture does NOT protect against compartment syndrome — the fascial rent is rarely a decompressing one.
    • Bleeding diatheses/anticoagulation, and a tight circumferential cast or dressing.
  • Non-operative measures while you decide — the correct order matters:
    • Remove all circumferential dressings. Splitting a cast reduces pressure ~30%; splitting the cast AND the padding reduces it ~65%; complete removal/bivalving is best.
    • Keep the limb at the level of the heart — elevation lowers arterial inflow pressure and worsens the perfusion gradient.
    • Correct hypotension and hypoxia; give supplemental oxygen.
    • Avoid further compressive traction; a regional block will mask the diagnosis and should be avoided in an at-risk limb.
  • Fasciotomy technique:
    • Leg — four compartments. The two-incision technique is standard: an anterolateral incision ~2 cm lateral to the tibial crest (anterior and lateral compartments; protect the superficial peroneal nerve as it pierces the fascia in the distal third) and a posteromedial incision ~2 cm posterior to the posteromedial tibial border (superficial and deep posterior compartments; protect the saphenous vein and nerve).
    • The deep posterior compartment is the one most often missed — it must be entered by detaching soleus from the posteromedial tibia.
    • Forearm — volar (extended Henry / curvilinear with carpal tunnel release), plus dorsal if pressures remain elevated; the deep volar (pronator quadratus, FDP, FPL) compartment must be decompressed.
    • Foot — nine compartments; two dorsal incisions ± a medial incision. The calcaneal compartment is the critical one.
    • Thigh — three compartments; a single lateral incision decompresses anterior and posterior; a separate medial incision for adductors.
    • Incisions are left open, dressed (often with negative-pressure therapy), and closed by delayed primary closure, gradual approximation or split-thickness grafting.
  • Late presentation is a genuine dilemma:
    • Established necrosis beyond ~24–48 hours with no functional muscle: fasciotomy converts a closed dead-muscle problem into an open infected one, with a high rate of sepsis and amputation.
    • Late fasciotomy is therefore usually avoided; management is supportive (monitor for rhabdomyolysis/renal failure) with delayed reconstruction or tendon transfer for the resulting Volkmann ischaemic contracture.
    • Late radiographs may show calcification within necrotic muscle.
    • Volkmann contracture of the forearm produces the classic flexed wrist and fingers with the "Volkmann sign" (finger extension improves with wrist flexion); FDP and FPL are affected first and worst.
  • Paediatric differences:
    • The classic sign set is the "three A's": increasing Analgesia requirement, Anxiety and Agitation — children often cannot report pain out of proportion reliably.
    • A rising analgesic requirement is the single most useful early indicator in a child.
    • Highest-risk paediatric injuries: tibial shaft (especially with a floating knee), both-bone forearm, supracondylar humerus with vascular compromise, and femoral fractures treated with skin traction or a tight spica.
  • Post-fasciotomy sequelae and medicolegal reality:
    • Chronic pain, altered sensation, scarring, muscle weakness, and delayed union.
    • Missed compartment syndrome is among the most frequent sources of orthopaedic litigation — documentation of serial examinations is the single best protection.

Professionalism: Closing/terminating practice & duty of care

The Canadian obligations when winding down a practice or ending a physician–patient relationship — notice, continuity, records, and what does not justify termination.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2023 (x2), 2024S, 2025 (x2)

  • Closing or leaving a practice — the checklist Canadian colleges expect:
    • Give reasonable advance notice — most Canadian colleges specify a minimum of three months (some require 90 days in writing), and longer where patients are hard to place.
    • Notify patients directly, particularly those with ongoing/active problems and those awaiting results or surgery — a sign in the waiting room alone is not sufficient.
    • Arrange continuity of care: transfer active patients, ensure someone follows up outstanding investigations and pathology, and do not leave a post-operative patient without a surgeon.
    • Notify the College and the CMPA, and arrange appropriate ongoing liability protection (many claims arrive after retirement).
    • Secure the records. Retention periods are set provincially — commonly 10 years from the date of last entry for adults, and for minors 10 years past the age of majority. You remain the custodian, or must formally transfer custodianship, and must tell patients how to obtain copies.
    • Cancel or transfer prescriptions/repeats and inform pharmacies, referring physicians and the regional health authority.
  • You cannot abandon a patient — the governing principle:
    • Once a physician–patient relationship exists, unilateral withdrawal without notice and without alternative arrangements is abandonment, a College offence.
    • Ending the relationship requires: written notice, provision of urgent and interim care for a reasonable period, reasonable assistance in finding another provider, and transfer of the record on request.
    • Emergency care must always be provided, regardless of any prior termination.
  • Legitimate reasons to end a physician–patient relationship:
    • Irretrievable breakdown of trust.
    • Abusive, threatening or violent behaviour toward the physician or staff.
    • Persistent non-adherence that makes safe care impossible — but only after documented counselling.
    • A genuine conflict of interest, or the patient's needs being outside your scope of practice.
    • Practice relocation, retirement or a change in scope.
  • NOT legitimate reasons — the usual wrong answers:
    • The patient is complex, costly or has a poor prognosis.
    • The patient has filed a College complaint or a legal claim.
    • The patient has sought a second opinion or declined the recommended operation.
    • The patient has an infectious disease (HIV, hepatitis) or belongs to a protected group — this is discrimination.
    • The patient is on opioids or has a substance-use disorder — you may decline to prescribe, but not to care.
  • Non-adherence does not automatically discharge your duty:
    • Document that you counselled the patient on the risks, offered alternatives, confirmed capacity, and explored the reasons for non-adherence (cost, literacy, transport, mental health).
    • Only then give written notice with an interim care period.
    • Refusing an operation is a valid exercise of autonomy, not non-adherence.
  • Duty of care beyond the office — nuances that get tested:
    • A consultant on call has a duty once contacted about a patient in the emergency department, even before physically seeing them; advice given by phone can establish the relationship.
    • There is generally no legal duty to rescue a stranger in most Canadian common-law provinces (Quebec's Charter is the exception, which does impose one), but there is a strong ethical expectation. Good Samaritan legislation protects the physician who does assist.
    • A physician who starts to help must not stop until care is handed over to someone of equal or greater competence.
    • Locum and hand-over arrangements must be explicit — an unclear handover is a common source of claims.
  • Follow-up of results is a durable obligation:
    • You are responsible for tracking and acting on tests you ordered, including after your involvement ends.
    • Failure to follow up an abnormal result (a missed bone lesion on a radiograph ordered for another reason, a positive culture) is a leading CMPA claim category in orthopaedics.
    • A robust result-acknowledgement system is the expected standard, not personal memory.

Biomechanics: Stress-strain curve, modulus & material properties

Reading the curve, distinguishing material properties from structural properties, and the viscoelastic behaviour of bone and soft tissue.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2025

  • Definitions first — these are the answers to half the questions:
    • Stress (σ) = force / cross-sectional area (Pa or N/m²).
    • Strain (ε) = change in length / original length (dimensionless).
    • Young's modulus (E) = stress / strain = the slope of the elastic (linear) region. It is a measure of stiffness.
  • Material property versus structural property — the distinction that separates the good answer from the trap:
    • Material properties are independent of size and shape: modulus, yield stress, ultimate stress, toughness, ductility, hardness.
    • Structural properties depend on geometry: stiffness of a construct, bending rigidity, load to failure of an implant.
    • A titanium and a steel rod of the same shape differ in modulus; a thick and a thin steel rod have the same modulus but different rigidity.
    • Bending rigidity ∝ r⁴ for a solid cylinder (area moment of inertia = πr⁴/4) and torsional rigidity ∝ r⁴ (polar moment = πr⁴/2). For a rectangular plate, bending rigidity ∝ thickness cubed. Doubling a nail's diameter increases bending rigidity 16-fold.
  • Walking along the curve:
    • Elastic region — deformation is fully recoverable; slope = E.
    • Yield point — the transition; beyond it deformation is permanent (plastic). "Past the yield point the material will not return to its original shape" is the single most repeated stem.
    • Plastic region — permanent deformation; ductile materials have a long one.
    • Ultimate tensile strength — the highest stress reached on the curve (not necessarily the failure point).
    • Failure/fracture point — where the specimen breaks.
    • Toughness = area under the ENTIRE curve = energy absorbed to failure. Resilience = area under the ELASTIC portion = energy absorbed and released elastically.
  • Ductile versus brittle:
    • Brittle (PMMA, ceramic, cortical bone in a high-strain-rate injury): fails at or just past the yield point with little plastic deformation; low toughness.
    • Ductile (stainless steel, gold, annealed metals): substantial plastic deformation before failure; high toughness; can be contoured.
    • Strong ≠ stiff ≠ tough. A material can be very stiff and brittle (ceramic) or compliant and tough (UHMWPE).
  • Modulus ranking — worth memorising in order:
    • Ceramic (alumina ~380 GPa) > cobalt-chrome (~210) > stainless steel (~200) > titanium alloy (~110) > cortical bone (~15–20) > PMMA (~2–3) > cancellous bone (~0.1–1) > UHMWPE (~1) > tendon/ligament > cartilage.
    • The clinical corollary is stress shielding: the greater the mismatch between implant and bone modulus, the more load the implant carries and the more the bone resorbs (Wolff's law). This is why titanium and low-modulus/porous stems are used.
  • Bone is viscoelastic, anisotropic and asymmetric in strength:
    • Viscoelastic = its behaviour depends on strain rate. At a high strain rate bone is stiffer, stronger and absorbs more energy before failing — then releases that energy, producing comminution and soft-tissue injury.
    • Creep = progressive deformation under a constant load over time (the basis of serial casting and Ponseti correction).
    • Stress relaxation = a fall in stress over time under a constant deformation (the basis of halo/traction and of why a tensioned wire loses tension).
    • Hysteresis = energy lost as heat during a load–unload cycle.
    • Anisotropic = properties differ with the direction of loading; bone is strongest along the long axis of the osteons.
    • Strength order: strongest in COMPRESSION, weaker in TENSION, weakest in SHEAR. This underlies fracture patterns — a transverse fracture from tension, an oblique from compression/shear, spiral from torsion.
  • Fatigue failure — the mode most implants actually fail by:
    • Failure at a stress well below the ultimate strength, after a number of load cycles — described by the S–N (Wöhler) curve.
    • Endurance limit = the stress below which the material survives infinite cycles. Steel and titanium have one; aluminium and most polymers do not.
    • Fatigue life is destroyed by stress risers: screw holes, notches, scratches, corrosion pits, and sharp changes in geometry. Titanium is particularly notch-sensitive.
    • The clinical version: a nail or plate spanning a nonunion will eventually break — implant failure is a sign of nonunion, not the cause.
  • Soft-tissue curves look different, and that difference is testable:
    • Tendon and ligament have a "toe region" at low strain where crimped collagen straightens — very low stiffness — followed by a linear region.
    • Microscopic failure begins at roughly 4% strain; macroscopic rupture at around 8–10%.
    • Ligament insertion sites are the classic failure zone in the skeletally immature (avulsion) whereas the midsubstance fails in adults.
    • Cartilage is a biphasic viscoelastic material: instantaneous load is borne by the pressurised interstitial fluid, and creep occurs as fluid exudes.

Biostatistics: Bias, randomization & confounding

Which design feature controls which threat to validity — the distinction the exam repeatedly tests.

5 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024S

  • Randomisation's unique property — and the near-universal correct answer:
    • It balances both measured AND unmeasured/unknown confounders between groups. No other technique can do this.
    • It does not prevent measurement, observer, performance or attrition bias — those need blinding and complete follow-up.
    • It does not guarantee balance in any single small trial; imbalance by chance is expected.
    • It says nothing about external validity (generalisability) — a well-randomised trial with narrow eligibility criteria has excellent internal and poor external validity.
  • Confounding, defined properly:
    • A confounder is associated with the exposure, is an independent risk factor for the outcome, and is NOT on the causal pathway between them.
    • A variable on the causal pathway is a mediator — adjusting for it is a mistake (it removes the effect you are measuring).
    • Effect modification (interaction) is not confounding: the effect genuinely differs across strata (e.g. a treatment works in the young and not the old). Effect modification is reported by subgroup, not adjusted away.
  • Methods that control CONFOUNDING — and their limits:
    • Randomisation — measured and unmeasured; design stage.
    • Restriction (narrow eligibility) — simple, but harms generalisability.
    • Matching — design stage; measured confounders only; requires a matched analysis.
    • Stratification — analysis stage; measured only; limited by the number of strata.
    • Multivariable regression and propensity scores — analysis stage; measured confounders only. A propensity score does not approximate randomisation for unknown confounders, however often that is claimed.
    • Instrumental-variable analysis is the only observational method that can address unmeasured confounding, and it depends on strong assumptions.
  • Blinding versus allocation concealment — frequently confused, frequently tested:
    • Allocation concealment happens at enrolment: the person recruiting cannot know the next assignment. It prevents selection bias. It is always possible, even in surgical trials.
    • Blinding happens after allocation and prevents performance bias (patients/surgeons behaving differently) and detection/observer bias (outcome assessors).
    • In surgical trials the surgeon usually cannot be blinded — so the achievable protections are concealed allocation, blinded outcome assessors, and objective or patient-reported outcomes.
  • The bias catalogue — know the named ones:
    • Selection bias — groups differ systematically at entry (includes referral, volunteer and healthy-worker bias).
    • Recall bias — classic in case-control studies; those with the outcome remember exposures differently.
    • Detection/observer bias — the assessor's knowledge influences measurement.
    • Attrition bias — differential loss to follow-up. >20% loss threatens validity; the "5 and 20" rule of thumb.
    • Performance bias — co-interventions differ between groups.
    • Lead-time and length-time bias — screening studies; earlier detection appears to prolong survival without changing it.
    • Publication bias — positive trials are published preferentially; detected on a funnel plot (asymmetry) or Egger test.
    • Expertise bias — surgeons are more skilled in one arm; addressed by an expertise-based RCT design (an important concept in Canadian orthopaedic methodology).
  • Intention-to-treat versus per-protocol:
    • ITT analyses patients in the group to which they were randomised, regardless of what they actually received. It preserves randomisation, protects against attrition/crossover bias, and is the conservative analysis for a superiority trial.
    • Per-protocol analyses only those who completed the assigned treatment — it re-introduces selection bias and exaggerates the treatment effect.
    • Important nuance: in a non-inferiority trial, ITT is anti-conservative (crossover pushes results toward "no difference", i.e. toward non-inferiority), so both ITT and per-protocol should agree.
    • High crossover is the standard critique of surgical trials where patients randomised to non-operative care later have surgery (e.g. SPORT).
  • Baseline imbalance after randomisation:
    • A single imbalanced variable in an otherwise properly randomised trial is expected by chance — with 20 baseline variables, one will differ at p<0.05 by chance alone.
    • The correct response is to adjust for the prognostically important variable in the analysis, not to declare the randomisation failed or to re-randomise.
    • Do not run significance tests on baseline characteristics in an RCT — any difference is by definition due to chance.
    • Prevention: stratified randomisation or minimisation on key prognostic variables, and blocked randomisation to keep group sizes similar.
  • Hierarchy of evidence — and its caveats:
    • Level I: high-quality RCT with narrow confidence intervals, or systematic review of Level I RCTs. Level II: lesser-quality RCT (<80% follow-up, unblinded), prospective comparative study. Level III: case-control, retrospective comparative. Level IV: case series. Level V: expert opinion.
    • Levels describe design, not quality — a poorly executed RCT can be less trustworthy than a large, well-conducted cohort. GRADE formalises this by downgrading for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.
    • A systematic review is only as good as its included studies ("garbage in, garbage out").

Biostatistics: Outcome measures & PROMs

The psychometric vocabulary (reliability, validity, responsiveness), generic versus region-specific instruments, and which orthopaedic scores are actually validated.

5 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • The three properties every instrument must have — and the order in which they are tested:
    • Reliability = reproducibility. Sub-types: test–retest (same patient, stable condition, two time points), inter-observer and intra-observer agreement, and internal consistency (do the items measure one construct — Cronbach's α, target 0.70–0.95).
    • Validity = does it measure what it claims? Sub-types: face (looks sensible), content (covers the whole domain), criterion (agrees with a gold standard, concurrent or predictive), and construct (behaves as theory predicts — convergent and discriminant).
    • Responsiveness = the ability to detect true change over time. Quantified by effect size or the standardised response mean.
    • An instrument can be perfectly reliable and completely invalid — a scale that is consistently 5 kg out. This is the classic stem.
  • Statistics used to express agreement:
    • Kappa (κ) for categorical data (e.g. a fracture classification) — it corrects for chance agreement. Landis–Koch: <0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, >0.80 almost perfect. Weighted κ for ordered categories.
    • Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous data.
    • Pearson/Spearman correlation is the wrong tool for agreement — two observers who differ by a constant 10° correlate perfectly but agree poorly. Use a Bland–Altman plot.
  • Floor and ceiling effects:
    • A ceiling effect means a large proportion of patients score the maximum, so genuine improvement cannot be detected — the well-known limitation of the Harris Hip Score and Constant score in high-functioning patients.
    • A floor effect is the mirror problem in severely affected patients.
    • >15% of respondents at either extreme is the usual threshold for concern.
    • Computer-adaptive testing (PROMIS) largely solves this by selecting items from a calibrated bank based on prior answers — fewer questions, less floor/ceiling, comparable across conditions.
  • Generic versus condition-specific instruments:
    • Generic (SF-36/SF-12, EQ-5D, PROMIS Global): allow comparison across diseases and produce utilities for QALYs and cost-effectiveness, but are less responsive to a localised musculoskeletal problem.
    • Region- or condition-specific (WOMAC, KOOS, DASH/QuickDASH, ASES, FAAM, Oxford scores): more responsive to change, but not comparable across conditions and no utility value.
    • The standard recommendation is to report both, with the condition-specific one as primary.
  • Clinician-based versus patient-reported:
    • A true PROM is completed by the patient alone. Instruments that mix clinician measurements with patient report are hybrids and are psychometrically weaker.
    • The AOFAS score is the classic example — widely used but poorly validated, mixing surgeon-measured alignment and motion with patient-reported pain; FAAM and FAOS have far better psychometric support.
    • Similarly the Harris Hip Score and Knee Society Score contain clinician items; WOMAC, Oxford and HOOS/KOOS are patient-reported.
    • Patient-reported outcomes correlate poorly with radiographic outcomes — a repeated theme.
  • MCID and its relatives — distinguish them:
    • MCID (minimal clinically important difference) — the smallest change a patient perceives as beneficial. Derived by an anchor-based method (against a global rating of change) or a distribution-based method (e.g. 0.5 SD, or the standard error of measurement).
    • MDC (minimal detectable change) — the smallest change exceeding measurement error. If MDC > MCID, the instrument cannot reliably detect a clinically important change.
    • PASS (patient acceptable symptom state) — the score at which patients consider themselves well; an absolute state, not a change.
    • SCB (substantial clinical benefit) — a higher bar than MCID.
    • The key exam point: a statistically significant difference smaller than the MCID is not clinically meaningful. The HEALTH trial (THA vs hemiarthroplasty) is the standard illustration — WOMAC favoured THA statistically but by less than the MCID.
    • MCID is not a fixed property of an instrument — it varies with baseline severity, the derivation method and the population.
  • Practical problems with PROM programmes:
    • Missing data and low response rates are the dominant threat; non-responders systematically differ (worse outcomes).
    • Case-mix adjustment is essential before using PROMs to compare surgeons or hospitals.
    • Registry PROM collection (e.g. the Canadian Joint Replacement Registry, and provincial programmes) is increasingly used for quality reporting and appropriateness.

Metal hypersensitivity & implant allergy

A type IV reaction with no validated test — how to distinguish true hypersensitivity from adverse local tissue reaction, infection and loosening.

4 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2023, 2024S

  • The immunology:
    • Metal hypersensitivity is a type IV, delayed, T-cell (cell-mediated) reaction — not IgE-mediated, so it is not "an allergy" in the anaphylactic sense and cannot be tested for with IgE.
    • Metal ions act as haptens, binding host proteins to become antigenic; sensitised T-cells then drive a perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate.
    • Onset is delayed (48–72 hours after re-exposure clinically; months to years after implantation).
  • Which metals:
    • Nickel is by far the commonest sensitiser, then cobalt and chromium. Nickel–cobalt cross-reactivity is common.
    • Stainless steel (316L) contains 10–14% nickel — the relevant implant for a nickel-sensitive patient.
    • Titanium, tantalum, zirconium and ceramic are the least immunogenic; titanium hypersensitivity is described but very rare and controversial.
    • Cobalt-chrome contains cobalt and chromium but essentially no nickel — a point often missed.
  • Epidemiology — the numbers that make the point:
    • Cutaneous metal sensitivity affects roughly 10–15% of the general population, and is 2–3× more common in women (jewellery, piercings).
    • Sensitisation is higher in patients with an implant (~25%) and higher still with a failed implant (~60%) — but this is an association, and causation runs in both directions: metal debris from a failing implant can sensitise.
    • Population sensitisation vastly exceeds the rate of symptomatic implant failure — so a positive test in an asymptomatic patient predicts nothing.
  • Clinical presentation (when it is genuinely hypersensitivity):
    • Persistent, otherwise unexplained pain and effusion, often with stiffness.
    • An eczematous dermatitis over the implant or generalised, and delayed wound healing — the most suggestive finding, though present in a minority.
    • Sterile effusion with a lymphocyte-predominant aspirate.
    • Aseptic loosening on radiographs, without another explanation.
  • There is no validated diagnostic test — the central exam point:
    • Patch testing tests cutaneous, not peri-implant, immunity; poor specificity, and a small theoretical risk of sensitising or of flaring an implant reaction.
    • Lymphocyte transformation test (LTT) is more sensitive but has poor specificity and limited availability; a positive LTT does not confirm implant failure.
    • Serum metal ion levels reflect wear, not allergy.
    • Diagnosis is one of EXCLUSION — you must first rule out infection (the priority), loosening, instability, malalignment, extensor mechanism problems, referred pain (spine/hip) and CRPS.
  • Distinguish from adverse local tissue reaction (ALTR/ARMD) and ALVAL — a different disease:
    • ALTR is a metal-DEBRIS phenomenon (wear and corrosion products), not a classic allergy, although ALVAL (aseptic lymphocyte-dominated vasculitis-associated lesion) has a lymphocytic histology that overlaps.
    • Features: raised serum cobalt and chromium (a Co:Cr ratio >2 suggests trunnion/taper corrosion rather than bearing wear), a pseudotumour or fluid collection on MARS MRI, and often a metal-on-metal bearing or a large-head modular taper.
    • Health Canada and the MHRA/FDA advise surveillance of metal-on-metal bearings with symptoms, ion levels and cross-sectional imaging.
    • Treatment is revision with debridement of the pseudotumour to a ceramic-on-polyethylene bearing; outcomes are worse than routine revision because of abductor/soft-tissue destruction.
  • Management:
    • Routine pre-operative screening is NOT recommended in patients without a history.
    • Take a directed history: reaction to jewellery, watch straps, belt buckles, dental work.
    • In a patient with a convincing history of cutaneous metal allergy, using a "hypoallergenic" implant — oxidised zirconium (Oxinium), titanium-nitride or zirconium-nitride coated, ceramic, or an all-polyethylene tibia — is reasonable but not evidence-mandated.
    • In an established, well-worked-up case with no alternative explanation, revision to a hypoallergenic bearing improves symptoms in most reported series — but the evidence is low-level.
    • Never revise for a positive patch test alone.

Osteoporosis: Diagnosis, pharmacology & fragility fracture care

Diagnostic thresholds, the drug classes and their mechanisms, Canadian calcium/vitamin D guidance, and the surgeon's role in secondary prevention.

3 of 7 sittings2021, 2022 (x3), 2024S

  • Diagnosis — two independent routes:
    • BMD T-score ≤ −2.5 at the lumbar spine, femoral neck or total hip by DXA (T-score compares to a young adult; Z-score compares to age-matched peers and is used in premenopausal women, men <50 and children).
    • Osteopenia = T-score −1.0 to −2.5. Severe/established osteoporosis = T ≤ −2.5 plus a fragility fracture.
    • OR a hip or vertebral fragility fracture at any BMD — the clinical diagnosis stands alone.
    • Most fragility fractures occur in patients with osteopenia, not osteoporosis, because there are far more of them — which is why absolute risk tools exist.
  • Fracture risk tools — Canada uses two:
    • FRAX (10-year probability of major osteoporotic and hip fracture, Canadian-calibrated) and CAROC (the simpler Canadian tool based on age, sex, T-score, with fragility fracture or glucocorticoid use bumping the category up one level).
    • Categories: low (<10%), moderate (10–20%), high (>20%) 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk.
    • Treat if: high risk, OR a prior fragility hip/vertebral fracture, OR >1 fragility fracture, OR T ≤ −2.5 with additional risk factors.
    • FRAX does not account for fall risk, number/recency of fractures, or lumbar BMD — known limitations.
  • A fragility fracture is a sentinel event — the surgeon's responsibility:
    • Roughly half of hip fracture patients had a prior fragility fracture that was not investigated — the "care gap".
    • A distal radius fracture in a patient >50 is a classic index event: it mandates BMD testing and risk assessment. It typically precedes a hip fracture by 10–15 years, making it the best prevention opportunity.
    • Fracture Liaison Services (FLS) are the intervention with the strongest evidence for closing the gap; Osteoporosis Canada's "Make the FIRST break the LAST" programme is the Canadian model, and FLS are cost-effective.
    • Roughly 20% of patients die within a year of a hip fracture, and fewer than half regain their previous function.
    • Exclude secondary causes: vitamin D deficiency, hyperparathyroidism, multiple myeloma, hyperthyroidism, coeliac disease, hypogonadism, glucocorticoids, alcohol.
  • ANTIRESORPTIVE drugs — know each mechanism:
    • Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronate) — inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway, disrupting osteoclast cytoskeleton and inducing apoptosis. They bind hydroxyapatite and persist in bone for years.
    • Denosumab — a fully human monoclonal antibody against RANKL, preventing RANK activation and osteoclast differentiation. Given 6-monthly subcutaneously; effect is not retained in bone.
    • Raloxifene — a SERM; reduces vertebral (not hip) fracture; increases VTE risk; reduces breast cancer risk.
    • Calcitonin — weak, largely historical.
    • Oestrogen/HRT — effective but not first-line for osteoporosis alone.
  • ANABOLIC and dual-action drugs:
    • Teriparatide (PTH 1-34) and abaloparatide — intermittent PTH is anabolic (stimulates osteoblasts), while continuous PTH exposure is catabolic. This is the classic "which is NOT an antiresorptive" answer.
    • Teriparatide is used for severe osteoporosis, and off-label for atypical femoral fracture, delayed union and nonunion. Historically contraindicated in Paget's disease, prior skeletal irradiation, open physes and a history of bone tumour (the osteosarcoma signal in rats has not been borne out in humans, and the boxed warning has been removed).
    • Romosozumab — a sclerostin antibody; sclerostin normally inhibits Wnt signalling, so blocking it is dual action: increases formation AND decreases resorption. Carries a cardiovascular warning; avoid within a year of MI/stroke.
    • Sequence matters: anabolic first, then an antiresorptive to consolidate the gain. Using an anabolic after a bisphosphonate blunts its effect.
  • Denosumab must not be stopped abruptly — a high-yield safety point:
    • Discontinuation causes a rebound increase in bone turnover with multiple vertebral fractures within months.
    • If it is stopped, transition to a bisphosphonate. Do not simply omit doses; do not delay a dose for elective surgery without a plan.
  • Complications of long-term antiresorptives:
    • Atypical femoral fracture — lateral cortex, transverse, with medial spike and lateral cortical thickening; risk rises with duration and falls quickly after stopping.
    • Osteonecrosis of the jaw — rare at osteoporosis doses (~1 in 10,000–100,000 patient-years), far more common at oncologic doses.
    • Drug holiday: consider after 5 years of oral or 3 years of IV bisphosphonate in a patient who is no longer high risk; continue in high-risk patients. There is no drug holiday for denosumab.
    • Zoledronate causes an acute-phase reaction after the first infusion; oral bisphosphonates cause oesophagitis and require upright dosing with water.
  • Calcium and vitamin D — Osteoporosis Canada figures:
    • 1000–1200 mg elemental calcium daily from all sources, with dietary calcium strongly preferred over supplements (supplemental calcium has a possible cardiovascular signal and causes constipation/stones).
    • 800–2000 IU vitamin D3 daily for adults at risk; target serum 25-OH-D >75 nmol/L.
    • High-dose intermittent (annual bolus) vitamin D increases falls and fractures and is not recommended — a counter-intuitive, frequently tested finding.
    • Calcium and vitamin D alone are not adequate treatment for established osteoporosis — they are adjuncts to a pharmacologic agent.
    • Also: weight-bearing and resistance exercise, falls-risk assessment and medication review, smoking cessation, and limiting alcohol to <2 drinks/day.
  • Implant implications in osteoporotic bone:
    • Prefer locked/fixed-angle constructs, longer plates, cemented arthroplasty, augmentation (cement or allograft strut), and load-sharing rather than load-bearing designs.
    • There is no evidence that bisphosphonates impair fracture healing clinically, and delaying them after a fragility fracture is not necessary — another common misconception.

Tribology: Bearing surfaces, wear & lubrication

Lubrication regimes, wear mechanisms and modes, polyethylene processing trade-offs, and the biology that turns wear particles into osteolysis.

5 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Lubrication regimes — in order of increasing surface separation:
    • Boundary lubrication — the surfaces contact; a molecular lubricant layer is all that separates them. Metal-on-polyethylene runs in boundary/mixed lubrication.
    • Mixed — partial fluid film with intermittent asperity contact.
    • Fluid-film (hydrodynamic/elastohydrodynamic) — a continuous fluid layer fully separates the surfaces; the ideal, with essentially no wear. Approached by ceramic-on-ceramic and metal-on-metal.
    • The governing parameter is the lambda ratio (λ) = fluid film thickness / composite surface roughness. λ >3 = fluid film; λ <1 = boundary.
    • λ is improved by a larger head diameter, smoother surfaces, greater sphericity, a lower clearance and higher fluid viscosity.
  • Wear mechanisms:
    • Adhesive — local bonding then tearing away of the softer material (the dominant mechanism for polyethylene).
    • Abrasive — a harder, rougher surface ploughs the softer one.
    • Third-body — interposed particles (cement, bone, metal debris) scratch the bearing.
    • Fatigue — subsurface stress leads to delamination and pitting; classic in the tibial insert of a TKA, worsened by thin (<6–8 mm) polyethylene, gamma-in-air sterilisation and oxidation.
    • Corrosion — electrochemical; fretting/crevice corrosion at modular tapers is the current problem (mechanically assisted crevice corrosion).
  • Wear MODES (which surfaces are articulating):
    • Mode 1 — the intended bearing surfaces against each other (normal).
    • Mode 2 — a bearing surface against a non-bearing surface (e.g. a dislocated head on the shell rim; femoral head on an eroded acetabulum).
    • Mode 3 — the intended surfaces with third-body particles interposed.
    • Mode 4 — two non-bearing surfaces rubbing (e.g. backside wear of the insert on the tibial tray, taper fretting, stem on cement).
  • The geometry of wear — the head-size trade-off:
    • Linear wear (mm/year, the penetration depth) is largely independent of head size in modern crosslinked polyethylene.
    • Volumetric wear rises with the SQUARE of the head radius (V = πr²×linear wear) — a bigger head sweeps more surface.
    • So a larger head buys stability and range of motion at the cost of volumetric wear — a trade-off that highly crosslinked polyethylene has largely neutralised, which is why 36 mm heads are now routine.
    • The head–neck ratio and the jump distance both improve with a larger head, reducing dislocation.
  • Metal-on-metal — low volume, enormous particle number:
    • Volumetric wear is roughly 60× lower than metal-on-poly, but the particles are nanometre-sized, so the number of particles is orders of magnitude higher and the total surface area far greater.
    • Ions disseminate systemically; the failure mode is ALTR/pseudotumour, not classic osteolysis.
    • MoM has a run-in phase of higher wear, then a low steady state; malpositioned (high abduction angle >55°) cups cause edge loading and catastrophic wear.
    • Largely abandoned; surveillance is mandated for those in situ.
  • Highly cross-linked polyethylene (HXLPE) — the processing trade-off is the exam point:
    • Irradiation (gamma or electron beam, 50–100 kGy) creates cross-links, dramatically reducing adhesive/abrasive wear — but it also generates free radicals that oxidise over time and embrittle the material.
    • Remelting (heating above the melt transition, ~150°C) eliminates free radicals but reduces crystallinity, and therefore reduces mechanical strength and fatigue/fracture resistance.
    • Annealing (heating below the melt transition) preserves crystallinity and strength but leaves residual free radicals → late oxidation.
    • Vitamin-E (α-tocopherol) doping quenches free radicals without a thermal treatment, keeping both oxidation resistance and mechanical strength — the current best-of-both approach.
    • Never gas-plasma or gamma sterilise in air — gamma-in-air sterilisation was the cause of the historic delamination epidemic. Modern sterilisation is in an inert atmosphere or by gas plasma/ethylene oxide.
    • Thin HXLPE liners and locking-mechanism/rim fracture are the residual concerns; keep ≥6 mm of polyethylene where possible.
  • Ceramic bearings:
    • Extremely hard, scratch-resistant and hydrophilic (wettable), giving the lowest wear of any bearing; ideal for young patients.
    • Modern alumina–zirconia composite (Delta ceramic) has largely solved the fracture problem — head fracture rates are now well under 1 in 1000; liner fracture is rarer still.
    • Problems: squeaking (associated with edge loading, cup malposition, thin/short necks and taller heavier patients), stripe wear, and the need for meticulous taper cleaning at assembly.
    • After a ceramic fracture, revision must include thorough synovectomy and a ceramic-on-ceramic (not metal or poly) rebearing, because retained ceramic particles will cause third-body destruction of a softer bearing.
  • From particle to osteolysis — the biology:
    • Particles in the 0.2–0.8 µm range are the most biologically active — the optimal size for macrophage phagocytosis.
    • Macrophages phagocytose the debris → release TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, PGE2 → upregulate RANKL on osteoblasts/stromal cells → osteoclast differentiation and activation → periprosthetic bone resorption.
    • OPG (osteoprotegerin) is the decoy receptor that blocks RANKL — the RANKL:OPG ratio determines net resorption.
    • Osteolysis is a biological, not a mechanical, failure — and it is typically painless until the implant loosens or the bone fractures, which is the argument for radiographic surveillance.
    • The effective joint space concept: joint fluid under pressure carries particles anywhere it can reach, including along screw holes and the stem, explaining distant lytic lesions.

VTE prophylaxis in orthopaedics

Where prophylaxis is proven, where it is not, the Canadian aspirin evidence, and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2022 (x2), 2023, 2024S

  • The framing that gets the marks:
    • The outcome that matters is symptomatic VTE and fatal PE, not asymptomatic venographic DVT — older trials used the latter and vastly overstated benefit.
    • Every regimen is a balance against bleeding: wound haematoma, reoperation and infection. In arthroplasty, a bleeding-related reoperation is more likely than a fatal PE.
    • Early mobilisation is a genuine, evidence-supported component of every protocol.
  • Hip and knee arthroplasty:
    • EPCAT II (Anderson, NEJM 2018 — Canadian): after 5 days of rivaroxaban 10 mg, switching to aspirin 81 mg for the remainder was non-inferior to continuing rivaroxaban for symptomatic VTE, with similar bleeding.
    • The earlier EPCAT I showed extended aspirin was non-inferior to dalteparin after THA.
    • PEPPER and large registry data support aspirin as a reasonable default in standard-risk patients; aspirin is cheap, oral and needs no monitoring.
    • Duration: 10–14 days after TKA; up to 35 days after THA and after hip fracture surgery.
    • High-risk patients (previous VTE, active malignancy, thrombophilia, morbid obesity) should receive an anticoagulant, not aspirin alone.
    • Mechanical prophylaxis alone (IPC/stockings) after hip arthroplasty and warfarin have the weakest evidence — the recurring "least supported option" answer. Warfarin's slow onset and narrow window make it a poor choice.
  • Hip fracture:
    • The highest-risk orthopaedic group. LMWH is standard; extended prophylaxis to 28–35 days.
    • Do not delay surgery to reverse anticoagulation beyond what is necessary — delay itself increases mortality; DOACs generally require 24–48 h, and neuraxial anaesthesia timing is the usual constraint.
  • Isolated below-knee injury / lower-limb immobilisation:
    • Routine pharmacologic prophylaxis for ALL patients is NOT indicated — the number needed to treat for symptomatic VTE is high, and the POT-CAST/POT-KAST trials showed no benefit of unselected prophylaxis.
    • Risk-stratify instead (previous VTE, active cancer, thrombophilia, obesity, oestrogen therapy, older age, duration of immobilisation, non-weight-bearing status). Validated tools: TRiP(cast), Caprini.
    • Highest-risk within this group: Achilles tendon rupture and ankle fracture with prolonged non-weight-bearing cast immobilisation.
    • Knee arthroscopy in a healthy patient: no routine chemoprophylaxis.
  • Spine and paediatric practice:
    • Elective spine surgery: chemoprophylaxis is generally not routine — the risk of epidural haematoma is weighed against a low baseline VTE rate. Mechanical prophylaxis plus early mobilisation is standard; add chemoprophylaxis for long deformity constructs, anterior/combined approaches, malignancy or prolonged immobility, usually starting 24–48 h post-op.
    • Spinal cord injury is a major exception — among the highest VTE risks in medicine; anticoagulate.
    • Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis surgery in an otherwise healthy teenager: mechanical prophylaxis and early mobilisation, not anticoagulation. Baseline paediatric VTE risk is very low; risk factors are a central line, obesity, oral contraceptives, malignancy and prior VTE.
  • Regional anaesthesia and anticoagulation timing:
    • Neuraxial block should be ≥12 h after a prophylactic LMWH dose and ≥24 h after a therapeutic dose; catheter removal ≥12 h after the last dose and the next dose ≥4 h after removal.
    • DOACs generally require ≥72 h before neuraxial block. Aspirin alone is not a contraindication.
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — a favourite because it is counter-intuitive:
    • An immune, IgG-mediated reaction to heparin–platelet factor 4 complexes that activates platelets.
    • It causes THROMBOSIS, not bleeding, despite the thrombocytopenia.
    • Timing: platelet count falls day 5–10 after exposure (sooner with recent prior exposure); a fall of >50% from baseline is the trigger, even if the absolute count is still "normal".
    • Risk is higher with unfractionated heparin than LMWH, and higher in surgical than medical patients.
    • Use the 4Ts score; confirm with an anti-PF4 ELISA (sensitive) and a functional serotonin-release assay (specific).
    • Management: stop ALL heparin — including LMWH, flushes and heparin-bonded catheters — and start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, danaparoid, fondaparinux, or a DOAC).
    • Do NOT start warfarin acutely (protein C depletion → venous limb gangrene and skin necrosis) and do not transfuse platelets. Warfarin is added only once the platelet count has recovered above ~150.
  • Diagnosis and treatment of established VTE:
    • Duplex ultrasound for DVT; CT pulmonary angiography for PE. D-dimer is not useful post-operatively — it is always elevated.
    • Treatment is therapeutic anticoagulation for at least 3 months; a post-operative VTE is a provoked event, so indefinite anticoagulation is usually unnecessary.
    • IVC filters are for patients with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation or a PE despite adequate anticoagulation; retrievable filters must be retrieved.

Biomechanics: Working length, fracture gap strain & callus

Perren's strain theory made practical — how construct choices set the strain at the fracture and therefore what tissue forms.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Perren's strain theory:
    • Interfragmentary strain = change in gap length / original gap length.
    • A tissue cannot form in a gap whose strain exceeds that tissue's own strain tolerance.
    • Tolerances: lamellar/cortical bone <2%; woven bone/callus ~2–10%; cartilage ~10–15%; granulation tissue/fibrous tissue up to ~100%.
    • Corollary: a very small gap with a small amount of motion has HIGH strain — which is why a tiny residual gap in an otherwise rigid construct is a nonunion risk, and why a modest gap with the same motion heals.
  • Two ways to reduce strain — and this is the whole of fracture-construct planning:
    • Reduce the motion → a stiffer construct → absolute stability → primary (direct) bone healing, no callus.
    • Increase the gap (spread the motion over a longer zone) → relative stability → secondary healing with callus. This is why comminution paradoxically heals well with a bridge plate: the total motion is distributed over many small gaps.
    • The failure zone is a construct that is too stiff to make callus but not stiff enough for direct healing — the classic locked lateral distal femoral plate nonunion.
  • Working length — the definition:
    • Plate: the distance between the innermost screws on either side of the fracture — the unsupported span of plate.
    • Nail: the distance between the proximal and distal points of firm cortical contact across the fracture.
  • What working length does — the trade-off:
    • Longer working length → LOWER construct stiffness → MORE motion at the fracture → more callus.
    • Longer working length also increases the bending stress in the plate itself → higher risk of plate fatigue failure. Plate stress rises roughly with the square of the working length.
    • Shorter working length → stiffer, protects the implant, but suppresses callus.
    • The compromise: a long plate with few, widely spaced screws — long enough to lower stress per screw, sparse enough to allow motion.
  • Practical construct rules for bridge plating:
    • Plate span ratio (plate length ÷ fracture length): >2–3 for comminuted fractures, >8–10 for simple fractures.
    • Plate screw density <0.5 — fill fewer than half the holes.
    • At least 3 screws (6 cortices) per side in healthy diaphyseal bone; leave 2–3 empty holes adjacent to the fracture.
    • Avoid placing a screw immediately adjacent to the fracture — it shortens the working length and creates a stress riser at the fracture.
  • Nails follow the same logic with different levers:
    • Bending rigidity ∝ radius⁴ — a small increase in nail diameter dramatically stiffens the construct. Reaming allows a larger nail and improves the fit.
    • Nail–canal fit and cortical contact determine working length more than locking screws do; a well-fitted nail in an isthmal fracture is stable, a nail in a metaphyseal fracture is not.
    • An open-section (slotted) nail has dramatically reduced torsional rigidity — a historical design point still tested.
    • Dynamisation (removing static locking screws) shortens the working length by allowing cortical contact and increases axial load transfer — used for a hypertrophic delayed union with an intact ring.
    • Reaming produces an intramedullary autograft effect and increases periosteal blood flow after an initial endosteal insult; SPRINT (Canadian-led) supports reamed nailing for closed tibial shaft fractures.
  • The stages of secondary (callus) healing — a repeatedly asked sequence:
    • Inflammation (haematoma, cytokines, hours to days) → soft callus (fibrocartilage, days to weeks) → hard callus (mineralised woven bone via endochondral ossification, weeks) → remodelling (woven → lamellar bone along lines of stress, months to years).
    • "New bone forming on a scaffold" = the hard callus / mineralisation stage, and describes osteoconduction.
    • Periosteal callus is intramembranous at the periphery and endochondral centrally; the periosteum is the dominant source of osteoprogenitors in a young patient.
  • What increases and what decreases callus stiffness — the direct exam item:
    • Increase: progressive mineralisation, increasing callus diameter (a bigger callus is stiffer because rigidity scales with radius⁴), time, controlled micromotion/weight-bearing, and adequate blood supply.
    • Decrease / impair: excessive motion or a gap, rigid over-stabilisation preventing callus altogether, infection, smoking, NSAIDs and glucocorticoids, diabetes, malnutrition, radiation, and periosteal stripping.
    • Early, small-magnitude axial micromotion stimulates callus; shear and torsion are detrimental.

Bone biology: Remodelling, resorption & calcium homeostasis

Cell biology of the basic multicellular unit, the RANKL axis, matrix composition and collagen cross-linking, and the calcium-regulating hormones.

3 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2022

  • Bone composition — the numbers:
    • By dry weight: ~60–65% mineral (calcium hydroxyapatite, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2), ~25–30% organic matrix, ~5–10% water.
    • The organic matrix is ~90% type I collagen; the remaining ~10% is non-collagenous protein: osteocalcin (the most abundant; a marker of bone formation, vitamin-K dependent), osteonectin, osteopontin, bone sialoprotein, proteoglycans and growth factors.
    • Mineral gives compressive strength and stiffness; collagen gives tensile strength and toughness. Demineralise bone and it becomes rubbery; deproteinate it and it becomes chalky and brittle.
  • Type I collagen and cross-linking — a directly tested item:
    • Type I collagen is a triple helix of two α1 and one α2 chains with a Gly-X-Y repeat (X often proline, Y often hydroxyproline).
    • Hydroxylation of proline and lysine requires vitamin C as a cofactor — hence the defective collagen of scurvy.
    • Cross-linking is mediated by lysyl oxidase (a copper-dependent enzyme) acting on lysine/hydroxylysine residues in the telopeptides.
    • Cross-linking increases tensile strength and stiffness and makes collagen insoluble and resistant to degradation. That is the answer to "cross-linking of type I collagen results in what".
    • Failure of cross-linking: homocystinuria and Menkes disease (copper). Lathyrism is the toxin model.
    • Degradation products of cross-links (N-telopeptide, C-telopeptide, deoxypyridinoline) are urinary/serum markers of bone resorption.
  • The four bone cells:
    • Osteoblast — from mesenchymal stem cells; lays down osteoid; expresses alkaline phosphatase, RANKL, PTH receptor and type I collagen; driven by RUNX2/Cbfa1 and Osterix. Responds to PTH (the receptor is on the osteoblast, not the osteoclast).
    • Osteocyte — a terminally differentiated osteoblast entombed in lacunae, connected by canaliculi; the mechanosensor of bone and the most numerous bone cell (~90%). Secretes sclerostin (inhibits Wnt → inhibits formation) and FGF-23 (phosphaturic).
    • Osteoclast — from the haematopoietic monocyte/macrophage lineage; multinucleated; resorbs bone in Howship's lacunae via a ruffled border sealed by an actin ring / sealing zone (αvβ3 integrin).
    • Bone-lining cell — quiescent surface cell that covers resting bone and lifts off to initiate remodelling.
  • How the osteoclast actually resorbs bone:
    • Carbonic anhydrase II generates H+, pumped by a vacuolar H+-ATPase proton pump into the sealed resorption pit → pH ~4.5 dissolves the MINERAL.
    • Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and cathepsin K then degrade the ORGANIC matrix. Cathepsin K inhibition (odanacatib) was an antiresorptive strategy.
    • Deficiency of carbonic anhydrase II or the proton pump (TCIRG1) causes osteopetrosis — dense, brittle bone that fails to remodel; renal tubular acidosis accompanies the CA-II form.
  • The RANK / RANKL / OPG axis — the central control system:
    • RANKL is expressed by osteoblasts and stromal cells; it binds RANK on the osteoclast precursor → differentiation, fusion, activation and survival.
    • M-CSF is the other required signal for osteoclast precursor proliferation.
    • Osteoprotegerin (OPG) is a soluble decoy receptor made by osteoblasts that binds RANKL and blocks it. The RANKL:OPG ratio sets net resorption.
    • Everything that increases resorption works through this axis: PTH, 1,25-vitamin D, IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, PGE2 and glucocorticoids all increase RANKL. Oestrogen increases OPG — which is why oestrogen withdrawal at menopause accelerates resorption.
    • Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody to RANKL — a pharmacologic OPG.
  • Remodelling — the basic multicellular unit (BMU):
    • Sequence: Activation → Resorption → Reversal → Formation → Quiescence (ARRF). One cycle takes about 3–6 months; resorption is fast (2–4 weeks), formation slow (3–4 months).
    • Coupling means resorption and formation are linked — you cannot have one without the other, which is why antiresorptives also reduce formation markers.
    • Cortical bone remodels through a "cutting cone" (an advancing osteoclast front followed by osteoblasts laying a new osteon) — the same process that produces primary bone healing under absolute stability.
    • Cancellous bone has ~8× the metabolic turnover of cortical bone because of its enormous surface area, which is why metabolic bone disease shows first in the spine and why vertebral fractures precede hip fractures.
    • Modelling (change of shape, resorption and formation at different sites) is distinct from remodelling (same site) and dominates in the growing skeleton.
    • Wolff's law: bone adapts to the loads placed on it. The osteocyte network senses strain; disuse → resorption; loading → formation.
  • Calcium homeostasis — three hormones, three organs:
    • PTH (from a fall in ionised calcium): raises serum calcium, lowers serum phosphate. Bone → increases resorption indirectly via osteoblast RANKL. Kidney → increases distal tubular Ca reabsorption, inhibits proximal phosphate reabsorption, and stimulates 1-α-hydroxylase. Gut → indirectly via vitamin D.
    • Vitamin D: skin (7-dehydrocholesterol → cholecalciferol under UVB) or diet → 25-hydroxylation in the LIVER (this is the form you measure) → 1-α-hydroxylation in the KIDNEY to active 1,25-(OH)2D (calcitriol). It raises BOTH calcium and phosphate, chiefly by increasing gut absorption.
    • Calcitonin (parafollicular C cells of the thyroid): lowers calcium by directly inhibiting osteoclasts — the only hormone with a receptor directly on the osteoclast. Physiologically minor in humans.
    • FGF-23 (from osteocytes): phosphaturic; excess causes X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets and tumour-induced osteomalacia.
  • Vitamin D and fracture healing:
    • Deficiency is very common in fracture patients (well over half in Canadian series, with our latitude and winters).
    • It is required for normal mineralisation of callus; severe deficiency produces osteomalacia and impairs healing.
    • However, routine high-dose supplementation in replete patients has not been shown to accelerate union — correct a deficiency, do not megadose. High-dose intermittent bolus dosing increases falls.
  • Bone turnover markers — know which is which:
    • Formation: bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, P1NP (procollagen type I N-propeptide).
    • Resorption: CTX and NTX (C- and N-telopeptides), deoxypyridinoline, TRAP-5b.
    • They reflect turnover rate and treatment adherence, not BMD; a fall in CTX confirms an antiresorptive is working.

Bone graft, substitutes & BMP

Osteogenesis vs osteoinduction vs osteoconduction, what each graft actually provides, BMP mechanism and its complications.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2024F, 2025

  • The three properties — define them precisely, because the questions turn on the definitions:
    • Osteogenic — the graft contains living cells that form bone (osteoblasts, osteoprogenitors). Only fresh autograft and bone marrow aspirate are osteogenic.
    • Osteoinductive — the graft recruits and induces host mesenchymal stem cells to differentiate into osteoblasts, via BMPs and other growth factors. Autograft, demineralised bone matrix (DBM) and recombinant BMP are osteoinductive.
    • Osteoconductive — the graft provides a passive scaffold for ingrowth of vessels and bone from the host. All grafts and ceramics are osteoconductive.
    • A fourth property to mention: structural (mechanical) support — provided by cortical autograft/allograft and by some ceramics, but not by cancellous graft, DBM or BMP.
  • Autograft — still the gold standard:
    • Cancellous autograft (iliac crest): the best osteogenic and osteoinductive material; rapidly revascularised (days), but no structural strength. Incorporates by creeping substitution with an initial resorptive phase.
    • Cortical autograft: structural strength immediately, but revascularises slowly (months), few surviving cells, and passes through a weakening phase at ~6 weeks to 6 months as resorption precedes new bone.
    • Vascularised autograft (free fibula) survives as living bone, heals like a fracture rather than by creeping substitution, and hypertrophies — the choice for defects >6–8 cm or an irradiated/infected bed.
    • Iliac crest donor-site morbidity is real: persistent pain (up to 20–30% at 2 years), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (anterior), superior cluneal nerve injury and sacroiliac violation (posterior), haematoma, and rarely avulsion of the ASIS or ureteric/bowel injury.
    • Reamer-irrigator-aspirator (RIA) harvests a large volume (30–90 mL) of graft with a growth-factor profile at least equal to iliac crest and less chronic donor-site pain — but risks blood loss, iatrogenic fracture and cortical perforation.
  • Allograft:
    • Fresh-frozen retains the most BMP/osteoinductivity but is the most immunogenic and carries the highest (still tiny) disease-transmission risk.
    • Freeze-dried (lyophilised) is the least immunogenic and has the longest shelf-life, but is the weakest mechanically ("croutons").
    • Irradiation (>25 kGy) and ethylene oxide sterilisation degrade collagen, reduce strength and destroy osteoinductivity.
    • Allograft is osteoconductive, weakly osteoinductive and NOT osteogenic — all cells are dead.
    • Risk of HIV transmission from screened, processed allograft is on the order of 1 in 1.6 million; bacterial contamination is the more realistic problem.
    • Fresh osteochondral allograft is different: it must be stored refrigerated (not frozen) to keep chondrocytes viable, implanted within roughly 28 days, and the chondrocytes are immunoprivileged within the matrix.
  • Demineralised bone matrix (DBM):
    • Allograft bone treated with acid to remove mineral, exposing the BMPs and collagen.
    • Osteoconductive and osteoinductive; NOT osteogenic and NOT structural.
    • Highly variable between manufacturers and even between lots — BMP content differs by orders of magnitude; it is regulated as a tissue, not a drug, so efficacy data are limited.
  • Synthetic substitutes — match the material to the job:
    • Calcium sulphate (plaster of Paris) — resorbs fast (6–12 weeks), purely osteoconductive, little strength; useful as an antibiotic carrier; can cause a sterile drainage/seroma.
    • Calcium phosphate (tricalcium phosphate, hydroxyapatite) — osteoconductive with excellent compressive strength and slow resorption (hydroxyapatite is essentially permanent; β-TCP resorbs over months). Injectable calcium phosphate cement is the material of choice for metaphyseal void filling (e.g. tibial plateau) because it resists subsidence.
    • Bioactive glass — osteoconductive, bonds directly to bone, some antibacterial effect.
    • None of these are osteogenic or osteoinductive; combining a ceramic with bone marrow aspirate is the common workaround.
  • Bone morphogenetic proteins — the mechanism question:
    • BMPs are members of the TGF-β superfamily. They bind serine/threonine kinase receptors and signal through the SMAD 1/5/8 pathway to the nucleus.
    • Mechanism: they INDUCE the host's undifferentiated mesenchymal stem cells to differentiate into osteoblasts (and chondroblasts). They do not work by keeping donor cells alive and do not themselves form bone — the classic distractor.
    • BMP-2 and BMP-7 (OP-1) are the osteoinductive workhorses. BMP-3 (osteogenin) is INHIBITORY. BMP-13/GDF-6 is chondrogenic.
    • Approved uses: rhBMP-2 for single-level anterior lumbar interbody fusion and for acute open tibial shaft fractures treated with a nail (BESTT trial); rhBMP-7 historically for tibial nonunion (humanitarian device exemption, now largely withdrawn).
    • Complications, which are the most tested aspect: heterotopic/ectopic bone formation, seroma, transient osteolysis and graft subsidence, radiculitis, and — the notorious one — life-threatening cervical soft-tissue swelling and airway compromise with anterior cervical use, which is off-label and carries an FDA warning. Retrograde ejaculation with anterior lumbar approaches. Concerns about cancer risk have not been substantiated but remain a labelled caution.
    • Contraindicated in the presence of a tumour, active infection, pregnancy, and in skeletally immature patients.
  • Other biologics worth distinguishing:
    • Bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) — osteogenic; the number of connective-tissue progenitors is what matters, and it falls with age; aspirate small volumes (2–4 mL) from multiple sites to avoid dilution with peripheral blood. Hernigou's data link the number of progenitors delivered to nonunion healing.
    • Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) — a concentrate of platelet growth factors (PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF); it is not osteoinductive in the BMP sense and the evidence for accelerating bone healing is weak. Preparations vary enormously (leukocyte-rich vs leukocyte-poor), which is the main reason trials conflict. Best evidence is in lateral epicondylitis and knee osteoarthritis, not bone healing.
    • Teriparatide is used off-label for delayed union, atypical femoral fracture and stress fracture.
  • The diamond concept — the framework for a nonunion:
    • Successful healing needs osteogenic cells + osteoinductive signal + osteoconductive scaffold + mechanical stability (plus vascularity and the host's biology).
    • Identify which corner is missing: an atrophic nonunion is a biology problem (graft it); a hypertrophic nonunion is a stability problem (stabilise it — grafting alone will fail); an oligotrophic nonunion often needs both. Always exclude infection and correct metabolic/endocrine abnormalities (a substantial proportion of "aseptic" nonunions have an undiagnosed metabolic abnormality, most often vitamin D deficiency).
    • Masquelet (induced membrane) technique for a segmental defect: stage 1 — radical debridement, stable fixation and a PMMA spacer which induces a vascularised, BMP-2 and VEGF-secreting pseudo-membrane over ~6–8 weeks; stage 2 — open the membrane, preserve it, and fill with cancellous autograft. It works for defects up to ~10–15 cm; the alternative for large defects is bone transport/distraction osteogenesis or a vascularised fibula.

Cartilage biology: Composition, chondrocytes & injury response

Zonal architecture and matrix composition, why cartilage does not heal, and what each restoration technique actually produces.

3 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021, 2025

  • Composition of articular (hyaline) cartilage:
    • Water 65–80% of wet weight — the highest in the superficial zone; water content INCREASES in early osteoarthritis (a key early change, not a decrease).
    • Type II collagen is 90–95% of the collagen and ~10–20% of wet weight — it provides tensile strength and traps the proteoglycans. Minor collagens: IX and XI (regulate fibril diameter), VI (pericellular).
    • Proteoglycans (~10–15%) — chiefly aggrecan, a core protein bearing chondroitin sulphate and keratan sulphate glycosaminoglycans, bound via link protein to a hyaluronic acid backbone. Their fixed negative charge draws in water, generating the swelling pressure that resists COMPRESSION.
    • Chondrocytes are only ~1–5% of the volume, are avascular, aneural and alymphatic, and are nourished by diffusion from synovial fluid driven by cyclic loading.
    • They function in a low-oxygen (hypoxic), largely anaerobic environment — HIF-1α dependent.
  • Zonal architecture — superficial to deep:
    • Superficial (tangential) zone (10–20%) — collagen fibres parallel to the surface, flattened chondrocytes, highest water and collagen, lowest proteoglycan. Resists shear; secretes lubricin (PRG4). This zone is lost first in osteoarthritis.
    • Middle (transitional) zone (40–60%) — oblique/random fibres, round chondrocytes, highest proteoglycan content.
    • Deep (radial) zone (~30%) — collagen perpendicular to the surface, chondrocytes in columns, largest-diameter collagen fibres, lowest water. Resists compression.
    • Tidemark — the basophilic line separating uncalcified from calcified cartilage.
    • Calcified cartilage zone — anchors cartilage to subchondral bone; contains type X collagen (also the marker of hypertrophic chondrocytes in the growth plate).
  • Cartilage is biphasic and viscoelastic:
    • Under instantaneous load, the pressurised interstitial fluid carries most of the load; as fluid exudes, load transfers to the solid matrix (creep).
    • Lubrication is a mixture of boundary (lubricin, hyaluronan) and fluid-film; the coefficient of friction of cartilage on cartilage (~0.002) is lower than any engineered bearing.
  • Why cartilage does not heal — and the critical depth:
    • It is avascular, so there is no inflammatory/haematoma phase and no access for marrow-derived progenitors; chondrocytes have very limited mitotic capacity.
    • Partial-thickness (chondral) defects that do NOT cross the tidemark do not heal at all — they produce a local matrix response and then stop.
    • Full-thickness defects that PENETRATE subchondral bone bleed, so marrow mesenchymal cells enter and produce a repair tissue — but it is fibrocartilage, dominated by type I collagen, which is mechanically inferior and degrades over time.
    • This distinction is the entire rationale for marrow-stimulation techniques.
  • Osteoarthritis — the biochemical sequence:
    • Water content INCREASES (matrix swells as the collagen network is damaged) — the earliest change.
    • Proteoglycan content DECREASES and the aggregates shorten; keratan sulphate falls and chondroitin sulphate ratios shift.
    • Type II collagen content is relatively preserved early but the network is disorganised; type I appears in repair tissue.
    • Chondrocytes initially proliferate and increase synthesis (an anabolic attempt) before failing.
    • Degradation is driven by MMPs (collagenase MMP-13, stromelysin MMP-3), ADAMTS-4/5 (aggrecanases) and cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α; these are counter-regulated by TIMPs.
    • Contrast with ageing cartilage, where water content DECREASES and proteoglycan/keratan sulphate rises — the classic "OA vs ageing" discriminator.
  • Grading systems:
    • Outerbridge (arthroscopic): 0 normal; I softening/swelling; II fissuring <1.5 cm not to subchondral bone; III fissuring to subchondral bone, >1.5 cm; IV exposed subchondral bone.
    • ICRS grading is the modern equivalent (0–4, with sub-grades by depth).
    • Kellgren–Lawrence is the radiographic grade (osteophytes, joint space narrowing, sclerosis, deformity).
  • Restoration options and what tissue each produces:
    • Microfracture / marrow stimulation → fibrocartilage. Best for small (<2–2.5 cm²) lesions in younger patients; results deteriorate after 2–5 years; creates subchondral cysts/overgrowth that compromise later procedures.
    • Osteochondral autograft transfer (OATS/mosaicplasty) → true hyaline cartilage, but limited by donor-site availability and morbidity and by matching surface contour; best <2–4 cm².
    • Osteochondral allograft → hyaline cartilage; for large (>4 cm²) or deep osteochondral lesions; requires a fresh graft with viable chondrocytes.
    • Autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI/MACI) → hyaline-like cartilage; two-stage; for large lesions; slow maturation and prolonged rehabilitation; historically periosteal patch hypertrophy (now avoided with collagen membranes).
    • Always correct the background problem: malalignment (osteotomy), instability (ligament reconstruction) and meniscal deficiency. A cartilage procedure on a malaligned or unstable knee fails.
  • Other cartilage types worth distinguishing:
    • Fibrocartilage — type I collagen; meniscus, annulus fibrosus, labrum, TFCC, tendon/ligament insertions and repair tissue.
    • Elastic cartilage — ear, epiglottis.
    • Meniscus: only the peripheral red-red third is vascularised (perimeniscal capillary plexus from geniculate arteries) — hence "red-red heals, white-white does not".

Necrotizing soft tissue infection

Recognition when the skin looks deceptively benign, the microbiological types, and why surgery — not imaging or scores — drives management.

3 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2022, 2024F

  • Classification by microbiology:
    • Type I — POLYMICROBIAL (aerobes + anaerobes; ~70–80% of cases). The commonest type. Typical host: diabetic, immunosuppressed, obese, peripheral vascular disease, post-abdominal/perineal surgery. Fournier gangrene is the perineal form.
    • Type II — MONOMICROBIAL, classically Group A Streptococcus pyogenes ± S. aureus (including MRSA). Occurs in previously healthy young patients, often after minor trauma, a varicella lesion, an insect bite, or with no identifiable portal at all. Associated with streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.
    • Type III — Vibrio vulnificus (seawater exposure or raw shellfish, especially with cirrhosis) or Aeromonas (fresh water). Fulminant, high mortality.
    • Type IV — fungal (Candida, Mucorales) in the immunocompromised or after severe trauma.
    • Clostridial myonecrosis (gas gangrene) — C. perfringens; deep penetrating/crush injury or farm contamination; alpha toxin (lecithinase); muscle, not just fascia, and radiographic gas.
  • The Group A Streptococcus specifics — commonly examined:
    • Virulence is driven by M protein (anti-phagocytic) and pyrogenic exotoxins A, B and C, which act as SUPERANTIGENS.
    • A superantigen bridges the MHC class II molecule to the T-cell receptor outside the antigen-binding groove, activating a huge fraction of T cells non-specifically → a cytokine storm → streptococcal toxic shock.
    • Group A Strep remains universally sensitive to penicillin — resistance has never been documented. The failure of penicillin in overwhelming infection is the "Eagle effect": in a high-inoculum, stationary-growth-phase infection, penicillin (which needs actively dividing organisms) is ineffective.
    • Hence clindamycin must be added: it is a protein-synthesis inhibitor, so it works independent of growth phase and suppresses toxin production and M protein.
    • Mortality is high (20–40%, higher with toxic shock), and it is higher in previously healthy patients who present late because the diagnosis is not suspected.
  • Clinical recognition — the skin lies:
    • Pain out of proportion to the physical findings is the single most important early feature, and it can precede any skin change.
    • Systemic toxicity out of proportion to the local findings: tachycardia, fever, hypotension, confusion.
    • Rapid progression — hours, with a demarcated advancing margin.
    • Late "hard" signs (do not wait for them): bullae (especially haemorrhagic), skin necrosis, crepitus, anaesthesia of the overlying skin (from destruction of cutaneous nerves) and a woody, indurated feel extending beyond the erythema.
    • Failure to respond to appropriate IV antibiotics within 24–48 h.
    • The classic error is diagnosing "cellulitis" and observing.
  • Investigations — useful but never a reason to delay:
    • LRINEC score (CRP, WBC, haemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, glucose): ≥6 is suspicious, ≥8 is strongly predictive. But its sensitivity is poor (well under 60% in validation studies) — a low LRINEC does NOT exclude the diagnosis. This is the exam point.
    • Hyponatraemia (Na <135) and a markedly raised CRP are the most useful individual laboratory findings.
    • Plain radiographs may show soft-tissue gas — specific but insensitive.
    • CT/MRI may show fascial thickening and non-enhancing fascia, but obtaining them must not delay theatre in a patient you already suspect.
    • The "finger test" at the bedside or in theatre: a small incision to deep fascia; lack of bleeding, "dishwater" grey exudate and easy separation of tissue planes by blunt finger dissection confirm it.
    • Frozen section can confirm, but is rarely needed and never worth a delay.
  • Treatment — the priority order:
    • IMMEDIATE, RADICAL SURGICAL DEBRIDEMENT is the only intervention that changes mortality. Time to first debridement is the strongest modifiable predictor of survival — delay beyond 12–24 hours roughly doubles to quadruples mortality.
    • Debride back to bleeding, contractile, healthy tissue; expect to take skin, fat, fascia and muscle. Under-debridement is the commonest error.
    • Planned re-look at 24 hours, and repeatedly, until no further necrosis — most patients need 2–4 procedures.
    • Amputation may be life-saving in a limb with extensive myonecrosis.
    • Empiric antibiotics: broad-spectrum (piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) + vancomycin (MRSA) + CLINDAMYCIN for antitoxin effect. Narrow once cultures return.
    • Aggressive resuscitation and ICU support; these patients are profoundly septic.
    • IVIG is considered in streptococcal toxic shock (it neutralises superantigen) — evidence is limited and it is an adjunct only.
    • Hyperbaric oxygen remains controversial, has never been shown to substitute for debridement, and must not delay surgery; it has the best rationale in clostridial myonecrosis.
  • Reconstruction and aftercare:
    • Large soft-tissue defects are managed with negative-pressure dressings and then split-thickness grafting or flap coverage once the infection is controlled.
    • Long-term morbidity is substantial: disfigurement, contracture, functional loss, and a significant psychological burden.
    • Household contacts of invasive Group A Strep may warrant public-health notification and chemoprophylaxis — invasive GAS is a reportable disease in Canadian provinces.

Professionalism: Informed consent & capacity

The elements of valid consent, how capacity is assessed, who decides when the patient cannot, and the emergency exception.

4 of 7 sittings2021, 2022, 2024S, 2025

  • The three components of valid consent — all three must be present:
    • Voluntary — free from coercion or undue influence (including from family or the surgeon).
    • Capacity — the patient is able to make this particular decision at this time.
    • Informed — the patient has been given the information a reasonable person in their position would want.
  • What must be disclosed — the steps of informed consent:
    • The diagnosis.
    • The proposed treatment or investigation, and what it involves.
    • The material risks and benefits.
    • The alternatives, including their risks and benefits.
    • The natural history / consequences of NO treatment — the element most often omitted and most often the answer.
    • An opportunity to ask questions, answered honestly.
  • The Canadian standard of disclosure is the MODIFIED OBJECTIVE (reasonable patient) standard — not the professional standard:
    • From Reibl v Hughes (SCC 1980) and Hopp v Lepp: a risk is material if a reasonable person in the patient's particular position would want to know it — not merely what other surgeons customarily disclose.
    • A rare but serious risk (death, paralysis, nerve injury, loss of limb) must be disclosed even at low probability; a common but trivial risk should also be mentioned.
    • If the patient asks a specific question, it must be answered fully regardless of materiality.
    • Risks specific to that patient's circumstances (an occupation, hobby, or a musician's hand) become material because of who they are.
    • Causation in a consent claim is judged on whether a reasonable person in the patient's position would have declined the procedure had they been told — an objective test with subjective elements.
  • Who obtains consent, and how it is documented:
    • The surgeon performing the procedure is responsible for the consent discussion — it should not be delegated to a junior trainee who cannot answer questions about the operation.
    • The signed form is evidence that a discussion occurred, not the consent itself. The documented discussion in the chart is what protects both parties.
    • Consent is a process, not an event, and can be withdrawn at any time, including in the anaesthetic room.
    • Consent covers the specific procedure discussed; extending an operation beyond it is only permissible if not doing so would be immediately life- or limb-threatening. "While I'm in there" additions require new consent.
    • Right or left side, digit and level must be confirmed — site marking and the surgical safety checklist are consent-adjacent obligations.
  • Capacity — the five elements Canadian colleges/CMPA use:
    • The patient understands the nature of the proposed treatment.
    • The patient understands the anticipated effects of that treatment.
    • The patient is aware of and understands the alternatives.
    • The patient appreciates the consequences of refusing treatment.
    • The patient can re-phrase and describe the information in their own words.
  • Capacity — the principles that generate the exam answers:
    • Capacity is DECISION-SPECIFIC and TIME-SPECIFIC. A patient can have capacity for one decision and not another, and can regain it.
    • Capacity is presumed in adults until there is reason to doubt it.
    • An unwise or refused decision is NOT evidence of incapacity — the single most tested point. A capable patient may refuse a life-saving operation.
    • A psychiatric diagnosis, a mental-health-act certification, intoxication, or an age alone do not automatically remove capacity.
    • Any physician can assess capacity — a psychiatry consult is helpful in ambiguous cases but is not a legal requirement.
    • Optimise before deciding: treat delirium and pain, use an interpreter, provide hearing aids/glasses, choose a lucid time of day.
  • When the patient lacks capacity:
    • Look for a prior capable wish or advance directive — a valid, applicable prior expressed wish generally binds.
    • Otherwise turn to the substitute decision-maker, following the provincial statutory hierarchy (typically: court-appointed guardian → attorney for personal care/power of attorney → spouse/partner → child or parent → sibling → other relative → the public guardian).
    • The SDM must decide on the patient's prior expressed wishes, and if unknown, on the patient's best interests — not on the SDM's own preference.
    • If the SDM appears to be acting against the patient's wishes or interests, the route is the provincial consent and capacity board / tribunal or the public guardian — not unilateral action by the surgeon.
  • The emergency exception:
    • Consent is not required when the patient lacks capacity, no substitute decision-maker is reasonably available, and delay would put life or limb at serious risk.
    • Treat only what is necessary to address the emergency — do not extend to elective components.
    • A known prior capable refusal applies even in an emergency (the classic example being a Jehovah's Witness advance directive refusing transfusion, which must be respected).
    • Document thoroughly why the exception applied.
  • Minors:
    • Most Canadian provinces have no fixed age of consent to treatment; the governing concept is the MATURE MINOR — a young person with sufficient maturity and understanding may consent to (and refuse) treatment. Quebec sets 14; some provinces set a statutory age for specific contexts.
    • Assess and document the minor's understanding, not simply their age.
    • If a parent refuses clearly necessary treatment for a child who cannot consent, the route is child protection services and, if needed, a court order.

  • The governing principle: the same professional standards apply online as in person, but the audience is permanent, searchable and unbounded.
    • Assume anything posted is public, permanent and attributable — privacy settings are not a defence.
    • Content can be screenshotted, shared and taken out of context; deletion does not undo distribution.
    • Your College can and does act on online conduct, including conduct on a personal account.
  • Clinical photography — the rules:
    • Explicit, specific, documented consent is required for clinical photographs, and the consent must state the intended use: record-keeping, teaching, publication, or social media. Consent for one use is not consent for another.
    • Photographs taken for care are part of the medical record and are governed by provincial health-privacy legislation (PHIPA in Ontario and equivalents elsewhere).
    • Do not store patient images on a personal device or in a personal cloud account; use institution-approved, encrypted systems. Personal-phone photography is a very common breach.
    • De-identification is more than cropping the face — tattoos, jewellery, scars, background, radiograph headers, accession numbers, date/time stamps and metadata (EXIF/GPS) all identify.
    • Consent must be freely given and revocable, and refusal must not affect care. The patient must not feel that the surgeon operating on them is asking as a condition of care — ideally the request is made by someone else, or at a separate time.
    • Intra-operative photography and video follow the same rules; recording for surgeon self-assessment still involves patient data.
  • Boundaries and online conduct:
    • Do not accept "friend" requests from current patients on personal social media; the personal/professional boundary is the issue.
    • Do not give individualised clinical advice on social media, in direct messages, or by text — it creates a physician–patient relationship without a record, examination or continuity.
    • Never discuss an identifiable patient, and remember that a case can be identifiable to the patient and their circle even when it is not identifiable to you — the combination of injury, date, hospital and photograph is often enough.
    • Do not disparage colleagues, trainees, institutions or patients online. Complaints about a workplace and "venting" posts are a common source of College complaints.
    • Advertising and endorsement must be truthful, verifiable and not create unjustified expectations; disclose any commercial relationship or industry sponsorship. Testimonials are prohibited by several Canadian colleges.
    • Correcting public misinformation is a legitimate and encouraged use of social media, provided it is evidence-based and does not involve an identifiable patient.
  • When a patient records you:
    • Canada is a one-party consent jurisdiction for recording a conversation — a patient may lawfully record their own consultation without your permission. It is not a criminal offence.
    • The professional response is to remain calm, not to refuse care, and to explore why — usually the patient wants an accurate record to share with family or to remember complex information, which is a legitimate need.
    • Offer better alternatives: a written summary, a copy of the consultation note, inviting a family member, or agreeing to a recording openly.
    • Do not confiscate the device, do not demand deletion, and do not terminate the relationship over it. Do document that recording occurred.
    • You must not record a patient without their consent — the obligation is not symmetric, because you hold their health information.
  • Generative AI and large language models in scholarly work — the current consensus (ICMJE, COPE, WAME, and the major journals):
    • An AI tool CANNOT be an author. Authorship requires accountability, the ability to approve the final version and to respond to questions about integrity — an AI cannot do any of these.
    • Use must be DISCLOSED in the methods or acknowledgements, stating the tool, version and how it was used.
    • The human authors are fully responsible for the entire content, including anything an AI produced — including plagiarism and fabricated citations ("hallucinations"), which are a documented and serious problem.
    • Every AI-generated fact and reference must be independently verified against the primary source.
    • Do not paste identifiable patient data or unpublished peer-review material into a public AI tool — the data leaves your control and may be used for training. Using an LLM to write a peer review is prohibited by most journals for this reason.
    • AI-generated images for figures are generally not permitted.
  • AI in clinical practice:
    • AI tools (imaging triage, ambient scribes, risk calculators) are decision-support, not decision-makers; the physician retains responsibility for the decision and for verifying an AI-generated note before signing it.
    • Understand the training population — a model developed elsewhere may not generalise, and algorithmic bias can systematically disadvantage under-represented groups.
    • Patients should be told when AI materially contributes to their care, and ambient recording of a clinical encounter requires the patient's consent.
    • Health data used with AI must remain within a privacy-compliant, ideally Canadian-hosted environment.

    • Would I be comfortable if this post appeared in the newspaper with my name and my institution attached?
    • Could this patient, or anyone who knows them, recognise themselves?
    • Do I have documented consent for this specific use?
    • Am I the accountable human for every claim in this document?

Radiation safety & imaging in pregnancy

Dose units and magnitudes, the practical rules that reduce exposure in theatre, and how to image a pregnant patient safely.

4 of 7 sittings2020, 2021, 2022, 2025

  • Units — keep them straight:
    • Gray (Gy) = absorbed dose (energy per unit mass).
    • Sievert (Sv) = equivalent/effective dose — absorbed dose weighted for radiation type and tissue sensitivity. This is the unit used for risk.
    • Becquerel = radioactivity of the source. Rad and rem are the older units (1 Gy = 100 rad; 1 Sv = 100 rem).
  • Two kinds of radiation effect:
    • Deterministic (tissue reaction) — has a threshold, and severity rises with dose: skin erythema (~2 Gy), cataract (the ICRP lowered the lens threshold to 0.5 Gy), hair loss, sterility. Relevant to prolonged fluoroscopy.
    • Stochastic — no threshold; the probability (not the severity) rises with dose: carcinogenesis and heritable effects. This is why ALARA applies at any dose.
  • Dose magnitudes worth having in your head:
    • Natural background: ~3 mSv/year in Canada. A transatlantic flight ~0.05 mSv.
    • Chest radiograph ~0.02 mSv; extremity radiograph ~0.001–0.01 mSv (negligible); pelvis radiograph ~0.6–1 mSv; lumbar spine series ~1.5 mSv.
    • CT abdomen/pelvis ~10 mSv; CT chest ~7 mSv; CT head ~2 mSv. CT is the dominant source of medical radiation.
    • Occupational limit for a radiation worker: 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years (50 mSv in any single year); 1 mSv/year for the public. Lens of the eye: 20 mSv/year.
    • Declared pregnancy: ≤1 mSv to the fetus over the remainder of the pregnancy in most Canadian jurisdictions.
  • The three pillars: TIME, DISTANCE, SHIELDING — and distance dominates:
    • Inverse square law: dose falls with the SQUARE of the distance. Doubling your distance from the source cuts exposure to one quarter; stepping back 2 metres reduces scatter dose by roughly 100-fold. Stepping back is more effective than any apron.
    • Time: use pulsed fluoroscopy, short taps rather than continuous screening, last-image hold, and collimate tightly. Collimation reduces both patient and scatter dose.
    • Shielding: a 0.25–0.5 mm lead-equivalent apron attenuates ~90–95% of scatter. Add a thyroid shield, leaded glasses (the lens is the dose-limiting organ for the surgeon) and consider leaded gloves and a ceiling-suspended shield.
  • C-arm geometry — the single most tested practical point:
    • The patient is the main source of SCATTER; the greatest scatter comes off the side where the X-ray tube (emitter) is.
    • Keep the tube UNDER the table and the image intensifier ON TOP, as close to the patient as possible. Tube-over-table dramatically increases the dose to the surgeon's head, eyes and thyroid.
    • In the lateral position, stand on the image-intensifier side, not the tube side.
    • Moving the image intensifier closer to the patient reduces dose and improves image quality; raising the table / increasing the distance from tube to patient also reduces skin dose.
    • Magnification and oblique/steep angles increase dose substantially. Digital magnification is preferable to geometric.
    • Keep hands out of the primary beam — use instruments, radiolucent retractors and drill guides.
    • Mini C-arm: much lower dose (roughly 1/10 to 1/100 of a standard unit) for hand, wrist, foot and ankle — but the operator's hands are often much closer to the beam, so hand dose can be comparable if technique is careless.
  • Monitoring:
    • Wear a dosimeter; if wearing one badge only, it goes at collar level OUTSIDE the apron (measuring dose to the unshielded head/neck).
    • Two-badge protocol: one at collar outside the apron, one at waist under the apron; a ring dosimeter if hands are near the beam.
    • Surgeons performing high-volume deformity, percutaneous or endovascular work have measurably higher lifetime exposure; annual review of dosimetry is a professional obligation.
  • Imaging the pregnant patient — the framework:
    • No single diagnostic study exceeds the threshold for fetal harm. The commonly quoted deterministic threshold is 50–100 mGy (5–10 rad); below 50 mGy there is no measurable increase in fetal loss or anomaly.
    • Highest sensitivity to teratogenesis is organogenesis, weeks 2–8; CNS sensitivity (intellectual disability, microcephaly) is greatest at weeks 8–15. Pre-implantation (<2 weeks) is an "all or none" period.
    • The stochastic (childhood cancer) risk is the residual concern — roughly a doubling of a very small baseline at ~10–20 mGy, i.e. an absolute risk that remains under 1 in 1000.
    • Any radiograph remote from the abdomen/pelvis (chest, extremity, head) delivers a negligible fetal dose and can be done with abdominal shielding.
    • MRI is safe in pregnancy at any trimester (no ionising radiation), but gadolinium is avoided — it crosses the placenta and is associated with adverse outcomes; use non-contrast MRI.
    • Ultrasound is first-line where it will answer the question.
    • Never withhold an indicated, potentially life-saving study in a pregnant trauma patient — the correct answer to the trauma stem is to image; the mother's survival is the fetus's survival. Involve a medical physicist to estimate fetal dose when multiple CTs are needed.
    • Document the discussion, quantify the expected fetal dose, and involve obstetrics.
  • Occupational exposure for the pregnant surgeon or staff member:
    • The fetal limit is 1 mSv (some jurisdictions 4 mSv) over the declared pregnancy; a standard lead apron plus the inverse-square law makes this readily achievable.
    • Provide a second, fetal dosimeter worn at the waist UNDER the apron, consider a wrap-around apron, and continue normal duties — there is no requirement to stop operating.
    • Declaration of pregnancy is voluntary; once declared, the employer must monitor and, if necessary, adjust duties.

    • Generalised increase in cortical thickness of the diaphysis.

    • Prodromal thigh or groin pain in a majority of patients — always ask.
    • ~30–50% are bilateral, so image the contralateral femur in every case.
    • Use full-length femoral radiographs; if these are negative and pain persists, MRI or bone scan.

    • Long-duration bisphosphonate use — risk rises after roughly 3–5 years and falls quickly after stopping.
    • Denosumab and glucocorticoids.
    • Asian ethnicity.
    • Femoral bowing and varus (lateral tensile stress concentration).
    • Absolute risk remains low and is far outweighed by the fracture-prevention benefit for most treated patients — do not use this to argue against treating osteoporosis.
  • Management:
    • Stop the antiresorptive; optimise calcium and vitamin D; consider teriparatide (anabolic).
    • Complete fractures: cephalomedullary nailing.
    • Incomplete fractures with a dreaded black line and pain, or that fail conservative treatment, should be prophylactically nailed.
    • Healing is slow and delayed union/nonunion is common; ream generously and correct varus/bow.

    • A bowed, varus femur with thick diaphyseal cortices will not accept a straight nail on a straight path.
    • Forcing it with a lateral start point produces malreduction in varus, anterior cortical perforation and failure.
    • Plan the start point and consider a more anatomically bowed nail, a lateral entry with careful reduction, or plate fixation.

Biostatistics: Study design — RCT, observational, meta-analysis

Hierarchy of evidence, what each design can and cannot answer, and threats to meta-analysis validity.

3 of 7 sittings2020, 2023, 2024F

  • Match the design to the question:
    • RCT — the strongest design for a therapeutic question, because randomisation balances known and unknown confounders. Poorly suited to rare outcomes, harms and prognosis.
    • Cohort study — best for prognosis and incidence; can measure multiple outcomes from one exposure.
    • Case-control — best for rare outcomes and aetiology; efficient, but yields an odds ratio, not a relative risk or an incidence.
    • Cross-sectional — prevalence and diagnostic test performance; no temporality, so no causation.
  • What observational studies can and cannot do:
    • They cannot establish causation and are vulnerable to selection, information and confounding bias.
    • They frequently have better external validity than a narrowly enrolled RCT, and are the only feasible design for rare harms and long-term registry outcomes.
  • Threats to the validity of a meta-analysis:
    • Publication bias — assessed with a funnel plot (asymmetry) or the Egger test.
    • Clinical and statistical heterogeneity between the included studies.
    • Poor quality of the included studies — "garbage in, garbage out".
    • Inappropriate pooling of dissimilar interventions or outcomes.
    • Duplicate or overlapping data from the same cohort published more than once.
  • Heterogeneity (I²) — what the number means:
    • I² >50–75% is substantial heterogeneity, and the pooled estimate may be meaningless.
    • The correct response is to explore subgroups/meta-regression or use a random-effects model.
    • It does not simply mean "more studies are needed" — a common distractor.
  • Fixed-effects versus random-effects:
    • Fixed-effects assumes there is one true effect and all variation is sampling error.
    • Random-effects assumes a distribution of true effects; it gives wider confidence intervals and weights small studies more heavily.
    • Use random-effects when heterogeneity is present.

Biostatistics: Type I/II error, power & sample size

Error types, power, and what drives sample size.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2024S (x2)

  • The two errors — define them precisely:
    • Type I (α) error = rejecting a TRUE null hypothesis = a false positive ("finding a difference that isn't there"). α is conventionally set at 0.05.
    • Type II (β) error = failing to reject a FALSE null = a false negative ("missing a real difference").
    • Power = 1 − β, conventionally 0.80 (an accepted 20% chance of missing a real effect).
  • The classic clinical scenario:
    • A small trial shows a benefit that a larger multicentre trial then fails to confirm → the first study made a type I error.
    • The mirror image: a small negative trial that a large trial later shows was a real effect → the first study was underpowered (type II error).
    • Multiple comparisons/subgroup analyses inflate the type I error rate — correct with Bonferroni or a hierarchical testing plan.
  • What makes a trial need MORE patients:
    • A smaller expected effect size — the dominant driver.
    • Low event rates in the outcome of interest.
    • Greater variance (standard deviation) in the outcome.
    • A lower α (0.01 rather than 0.05).
    • Higher power (90% rather than 80%).
    • A two-sided rather than one-sided test.
    • Anticipated dropout/loss to follow-up (inflate the calculated n).
    • The smaller the difference you want to detect, the larger the trial must be.
  • Interpreting a negative trial:
    • An underpowered trial does not prove there is no difference — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
    • A confidence interval is more informative than a p-value: it shows the range of effects compatible with the data, including clinically important ones the trial could not exclude.
    • To claim equivalence or non-inferiority you need a pre-specified margin and an appropriately powered design, not a failed superiority trial.

Bone healing: Stages, primary vs secondary & modifiers

Phases of fracture healing and factors that modify it.

2 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2021

  • Secondary (indirect) healing — the four stages in order:
    • Haematoma and inflammation (0–7 days) — cytokines, macrophages, recruitment of mesenchymal stem cells.
    • Soft callus / fibrocartilage (chondrogenesis, ~2–3 weeks).
    • Hard callus — endochondral ossification: new bone laid down on a cartilage scaffold.
    • Remodelling — woven bone to lamellar bone along lines of stress (Wolff's law), months to years.
    • The "new bone forming on a scaffold" stem = hard callus / endochondral ossification, and describes osteoconduction.
  • Primary (direct) healing:
    • Occurs only with absolute stability and interfragmentary strain <2%.
    • Cutting cones cross the fracture (Haversian remodelling) — NO callus is formed.
    • Radiographic callus after compression plating of a simple fracture means the construct is failing, not that it is healing well.
    • Contact healing (gap <0.01 mm) versus gap healing (up to ~800 µm, lamellar bone fills the gap first, then remodels).
  • Inflammation is essential, not incidental:
    • A controlled inflammatory phase drives healing — suppressing it entirely is harmful.
    • NSAIDs (both non-selective and COX-2 selective) impair early healing in animal models and are associated with nonunion in clinical series.
    • Practical position: use short courses, and avoid them in high-risk fractures, nonunions and spinal fusions.
    • Glucocorticoids impair healing throughout.
  • Negative modifiers of healing:
    • Smoking — the strongest modifiable factor (nicotine-induced vasoconstriction plus direct osteoblast inhibition).
    • Diabetes, malnutrition (albumin <35 g/L, lymphocyte count <1500/mm³) and infection.
    • NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, and bisphosphonates (which delay remodelling of callus, though clinical union is not impaired).
    • Radiation, peripheral vascular disease and periosteal stripping.
    • Vitamin D deficiency is very common in fracture patients and should be corrected — though supplementation in a replete patient has not been shown to accelerate union.

    • Traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury — the strongest systemic risk factors.
    • Patient factors: male sex, ankylosing spondylitis, DISH, prior HO and hypertrophic osteoarthritis.
  • Pathophysiology and the diagnostic timeline:
    • Mesenchymal cells differentiate into osteoblasts under BMP signalling, and mature lamellar bone forms in soft tissue.
    • Bone scan turns positive earliest (2–4 weeks) — the most sensitive early test.
    • Alkaline phosphatase rises but is non-specific.
    • Radiographic changes appear at ~4–6 weeks; maturity takes 6–12 months.
  • Prophylaxis — two effective options:
    • Single-dose radiation, 700–800 cGy, given within 24 hours pre-operatively or 72 hours post-operatively.
    • Indomethacin (or another NSAID) for 2–6 weeks.
    • Both work equally well. Radiation avoids the GI/renal effects of NSAIDs but may impair fracture or trochanteric osteotomy healing and is contraindicated in the young because of the malignancy risk.
    • Shield any uncemented, ingrowth-dependent surfaces from the radiation field.
  • Excision:
    • Only once the HO is mature and the symptoms — loss of motion, impingement, nerve compression — justify it.
    • Historically 6–12 months; earlier excision is increasingly accepted at the elbow where stiffness is disabling and recurrence is manageable.
    • Excision must be followed by prophylaxis (radiation and/or indomethacin) or it recurs.
  • Classification and natural history:
    • Brooker classification is used at the hip (I islands of bone → IV apparent ankylosis).
    • Most HO is asymptomatic and radiographic only.
    • It does NOT all resolve by 6 months — a common false statement.

Nerve injury: Classification, degeneration & recovery

Seddon/Sunderland, Wallerian degeneration and the order of recovery.

3 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S, 2025

  • Seddon classification:
    • Neurapraxia — a conduction block with an intact axon; no Wallerian degeneration; full recovery in days to weeks.
    • Axonotmesis — the axon is disrupted but the endoneurium is intact; Wallerian degeneration occurs, and recovery is by regeneration along the intact tube.
    • Neurotmesis — complete transection of the nerve; requires surgical repair.
  • Sunderland grades map onto Seddon:
    • I = neurapraxia.
    • II = axon disrupted, endoneurium intact — complete recovery expected.
    • III = endoneurium disrupted, perineurium intact — incomplete recovery.
    • IV = perineurium disrupted, epineurium intact — neuroma-in-continuity; requires resection and grafting.
    • V = neurotmesis, complete transection.
    • (VI, Mackinnon — a mixed injury with different grades in different fascicles.)
  • The rate and pattern of recovery:
    • ~1 mm/day (1 inch/month) after a lag of about 3–4 weeks while the growth cone forms.
    • Recovery proceeds proximal to distal — the muscle closest to the injury recovers first.
    • Sensory recovery order: pain/temperature → moving touch → constant touch → vibration → two-point discrimination.
  • The two classic recovery-order questions:
    • Radial nerve palsy with a humeral shaft fracture: brachioradialis recovers FIRST; the last to recover is typically extensor indicis proprius / extensor pollicis longus.
    • Mid-forearm ulnar nerve laceration: the intrinsics (interossei, adductor pollicis) recover LAST, and frequently never — which is the rationale for early tendon transfer or supercharged end-to-side nerve transfer.
  • Investigation:
    • An advancing Tinel sign indicates regenerating axons progressing distally.
    • EMG/NCS is most informative at ~3–4 weeks, when fibrillation potentials appear.
    • A normal study before 3 weeks does NOT exclude injury — Wallerian degeneration has not yet occurred distally.
    • Ultrasound and MR neurography can localise a lesion and identify a neuroma or entrapment.
  • Timing of repair:
    • Clean, sharp laceration → primary repair (tension-free, epineurial, within days).
    • Blunt or avulsion injury → tag the ends and repair at ~3 weeks, once the zone of injury has declared itself.
    • No recovery by 3–6 months → explore, with intra-operative nerve conduction, and perform nerve grafting or nerve transfer.
    • Motor end-plates degenerate irreversibly by ~18–24 months — after that, reinnervation will not restore useful motor function and tendon transfer or free functioning muscle transfer is the option.

Tranexamic acid & blood management

Mechanism, routes, efficacy and safety of TXA.

3 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • Mechanism of tranexamic acid:
    • TXA is a lysine analogue.
    • It competitively blocks the lysine-binding sites on plasminogen, preventing plasminogen from binding to fibrin.
    • The result is inhibition of fibrinolysis — it stabilises existing clot.
    • It does NOT promote new clot formation — the key point behind its safety profile.
  • Efficacy and route in arthroplasty:
    • Reduces blood loss and transfusion by roughly 30–50%.
    • IV, topical/intra-articular and oral routes are equivalent for TKA and THA.
    • Combined routes give little additional benefit over a single route.
    • Oral TXA is the cheapest and is as effective — an increasingly common exam answer.
    • Typical IV dosing: 10–20 mg/kg (or 1 g) before incision, with a second dose at 3 hours or at closure.
  • Safety — the evidence has moved:
    • Multiple large studies and meta-analyses show no increase in VTE.
    • This includes patients with a history of VTE, coronary stents or previous stroke — current AAOS/ICM consensus considers these NOT to be absolute contraindications.
    • True contraindications are limited: acquired defective colour vision, active intravascular clotting, and subarachnoid haemorrhage in some guidance.
    • Dose reduction is required in significant renal impairment (it is renally cleared); seizures are a dose-related risk at very high cardiac-surgery doses.
  • TXA in trauma — timing is everything:
    • CRASH-2 showed a mortality benefit when given within 3 hours of injury.
    • There was HARM if given after 3 hours — increased bleeding-related death.
    • Earlier is better; pre-hospital administration is now common.
    • CRASH-3 extended the principle to traumatic brain injury, with benefit in mild-to-moderate TBI treated early.
  • Transfusion strategy:
    • Restrictive transfusion (threshold ~70–80 g/L, or symptoms) is as safe as liberal transfusion after hip fracture surgery — the FOCUS trial.
    • Transfuse for symptoms, not for a number.
    • The best transfusion strategy is pre-operative correction of anaemia — iron deficiency is common and treatable.
    • Cell salvage and controlled hypotensive anaesthesia are adjuncts in high-blood-loss cases (deformity, revision, pelvic).

Trunnionosis, modularity & adverse local tissue reaction

Mechanically assisted crevice corrosion at modular junctions.

2 of 7 sittings2020 (x2), 2024F

  • What trunnionosis is:
    • Fretting and crevice corrosion at the head–neck (or neck–stem) taper — mechanically assisted crevice corrosion.
    • It releases metal debris and ions and produces an ALTR/pseudotumour.
    • Crucially, it can look identical to metal-on-metal disease even with a CERAMIC or POLYETHYLENE bearing — the taper, not the bearing, is the source.
  • Factors that increase risk:
    • Larger head diameter — higher frictional torque at the taper.
    • Longer head/neck offset, particularly skirted heads.
    • Dissimilar metals (mixed alloy) at the junction — galvanic corrosion.
    • Smaller and shorter trunnion designs (the trend to skinnier trunnions).
    • High patient BMI and activity level.
    • Contamination of the taper at assembly — blood, fat or debris.
  • Minimising risk — the technical points:
    • Assemble a clean and DRY taper (wipe and dry it before the head goes on).
    • Impact firmly once, along the axis of the neck.
    • Avoid skirted or extra-long heads and avoid large metal heads.
    • Avoid unnecessary modularity — modular neck stems have a poor track record and several have been recalled.
    • Consider a ceramic head (or a ceramic head with a titanium sleeve at revision).
  • Work-up of a painful metal-bearing or modular THA:
    • Rule out infection FIRST — ESR/CRP and aspiration (send for cell count, differential and culture; note that metal debris can falsely elevate automated cell counts).
    • Serum cobalt and chromium levels.
    • MARS MRI for a pseudotumour, fluid collection and abductor destruction.
    • Cobalt elevated disproportionately to chromium (Co:Cr >2) suggests TAPER corrosion rather than bearing wear — the discriminating result.
    • Also exclude loosening, instability, iliopsoas impingement and spinal referral before attributing pain to the taper.

Biostatistics: Diagnostic test performance (sens/spec/PPV/NPV)

2×2 table calculations and the effect of prevalence.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2023

  • Sensitivity and specificity — properties of the TEST:
    • Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN) — "of those with the disease, how many test positive".
    • Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) — "of those without the disease, how many test negative".
    • SnNout: a highly Sensitive test, when Negative, rules out.
    • SpPin: a highly Specific test, when Positive, rules in.
    • Sensitivity and specificity are independent of prevalence.
  • Predictive values — properties of the POPULATION:
    • PPV = TP/(TP+FP) — "of those who test positive, how many have the disease".
    • NPV = TN/(TN+FN).
    • PPV and NPV DEPEND ON PREVALENCE.
    • As prevalence falls, PPV falls and NPV rises — the core concept behind screening a low-prevalence population and the most frequently tested point.
  • Likelihood ratios — the prevalence-independent alternative:
    • LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity).
    • LR− = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity.
    • LR+ >10 or LR− <0.1 substantially changes the post-test probability; values near 1 are useless.
    • Combine with pre-test odds (a Fagan nomogram) to get post-test probability.
  • Other terms that appear in the same questions:
    • Accuracy = (TP+TN)/total — misleading when the disease is rare.
    • ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1−specificity across thresholds; the area under the curve (AUC/c-statistic) summarises discrimination (0.5 = useless, 1.0 = perfect).
    • Moving the threshold trades sensitivity against specificity — it does not improve both.
  • Practical tip for the 2×2 question:
    • Build the table with disease across the top and test result down the side.
    • Fill in the row and column totals first.
    • Then compute along rows for predictive values and down columns for sensitivity/specificity.

Biostatistics: Incidence, prevalence & epidemiology

Definitions and their relationship.

2 of 7 sittings2022, 2025

  • Definitions:
    • Incidence = the number of NEW cases in a defined population over a defined time period — a rate.
    • Prevalence = the TOTAL number of existing cases at a point in time (point prevalence) or over a period — a proportion.
    • Cumulative incidence (risk) = new cases / population at risk at the start; incidence rate = new cases / person-time at risk.
  • The relationship between them:
    • Prevalence ≈ incidence × average duration of disease.
    • Chronic conditions of long duration (osteoarthritis) have HIGH prevalence with modest incidence.
    • Rapidly fatal or rapidly cured conditions have LOW prevalence despite high incidence.
  • Which one you need, and when:
    • Prevalence — for interpreting a diagnostic test (it is the pre-test probability) and for health-services planning and resource allocation.
    • Incidence — for causation, risk-factor studies and measuring the effect of prevention.
  • The classic distractor:
    • Anything that improves SURVIVAL without curing a disease INCREASES prevalence without changing incidence.
    • Conversely, an effective cure lowers prevalence while incidence is unchanged, and effective prevention lowers both.
    • Improved detection raises apparent incidence and prevalence without changing the underlying disease.

Casting & splinting materials

Material properties and the cast index.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2023

  • Fibreglass versus plaster of Paris:
    • Fibreglass: stronger for the same weight, lighter, water-resistant, radiolucent, sets faster and is more durable.
    • Plaster of Paris: cheaper, and has better moulding and conformability; lower exothermic risk if applied correctly.
    • Superior moulding is the advantage of PLASTER, not fibreglass — the standard distractor.
    • Plaster is therefore preferred for the acute, reduced fracture; fibreglass for the definitive, stable cast.
  • Plaster burns — the three contributing factors:
    • High dip-water temperature (use cool water; the reaction is exothermic).
    • Excessive thickness — more than ~8 plies.
    • Setting the limb on a pillow, which insulates and traps the heat.
    • Support the cast on the palms, not the fingertips, to avoid pressure indentations and ulcers.
  • Cast index — the number to know:
    • = internal cast width on the LATERAL view ÷ internal width on the AP view, measured at the fracture.
    • >0.8 predicts loss of reduction in paediatric distal radius and forearm fractures.
    • Related measures: the three-point index, the gap index and the padding index.
  • The overall principle:
    • Good moulding beats a thick cast.
    • A well-moulded, three-point cast with an appropriate cast index is the single best predictor of a maintained reduction.
    • Minimise padding over bony prominences but never leave the skin unprotected; avoid circumferential constriction in the acute injury.

Chronic exertional compartment syndrome

Diagnosis and risk factors for CECS.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2021

  • Presentation:
    • Exercise-induced, reproducible pain and tightness relieved by rest, coming on at a predictable time or distance.
    • Usually the anterior compartment of the leg; the deep posterior compartment is the next most common.
    • May have transient paraesthesia or foot drop during exercise.
    • The examination is NORMAL at rest — the patient must be examined immediately after exercise to find a tense compartment and a fascial defect.

    • Resting pressure >15 mmHg.
    • 1 minute post-exercise >30 mmHg.
    • 5 minutes post-exercise >20 mmHg.
    • Exclude the differential first: medial tibial stress syndrome, stress fracture, popliteal artery entrapment, nerve entrapment and claudication.
  • Risk factors:
    • Young athletes and military recruits.
    • Running and repetitive-impact sport.
    • Anabolic steroid or creatine use (increased muscle volume in a fixed compartment).
    • Eccentric training and rapid increases in training volume.
    • Being older and sedentary is protective — the odd one out in any list of risk factors.
  • Treatment:
    • Activity modification first, with a graded return.
    • Gait retraining — a forefoot-strike running pattern reduces anterior compartment pressures and is genuinely effective.
    • Definitive treatment is FASCIOTOMY of the involved compartments (open or endoscopically assisted).
    • Results are good anteriorly (~80–90%) and less predictable for the deep posterior compartment.
    • Recurrence is usually from incomplete release or scarring; protect the superficial peroneal nerve.

Muscle physiology: Fibre types & contraction mechanics

Fibre typing and modes of contraction.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2024S

  • Fibre types — the two that matter:
    • Type I (slow twitch): oxidative; high mitochondrial, myoglobin and capillary density; fatigue-resistant; low force; red. Postural muscles — soleus.
    • Type IIa: fast oxidative-glycolytic — intermediate, and the type most responsive to endurance training.
    • Type IIb/IIx: glycolytic; high force, rapid fatigue; white. Sprinting and power.
    • Immobilisation preferentially atrophies type I fibres; ageing and denervation preferentially lose type II.
  • Contraction modes:
    • Eccentric = the muscle LENGTHENS while generating force (the load exceeds the force produced).
    • Eccentric contraction generates the HIGHEST force of the three modes, uses the least metabolic energy per unit force, and produces the most delayed-onset muscle soreness and microdamage.
    • Concentric = shortening while generating force.
    • Isometric = force with no change in length.
    • Eccentric loading programmes (Alfredson) are the basis of tendinopathy rehabilitation.
  • The two fundamental relationships:
    • Force–velocity: force falls as concentric shortening velocity increases, and rises with eccentric lengthening velocity.
    • Length–tension: peak active force occurs at optimal sarcomere overlap of actin and myosin; passive tension rises at long lengths.
    • Total force = active + passive; this underlies the effect of tendon transfer tensioning and of muscle shortening after limb-length change.
  • Denervation and reinnervation on EMG:
    • Denervated muscle FIBRILLATES (spontaneous fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves) — appearing at ~2–3 weeks.
    • Reinnervation produces polyphasic potentials and, later, fibre-type grouping on biopsy.
    • Chronically denervated muscle undergoes irreversible fibrofatty change after ~18–24 months.

Osteogenesis imperfecta

Sillence classification, inheritance and orthopaedic management.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2023

  • The defect:
    • A defect in type I collagen QUANTITY or QUALITY, from COL1A1 / COL1A2 mutations.
    • Quantitative (haploinsufficiency) defects tend to be milder; qualitative (structural) defects are more severe.
  • Sillence classification:
    • Type I — mildest, autosomal dominant, blue sclerae, normal stature.
    • Type II — perinatal LETHAL; autosomal recessive or a new dominant mutation — the classic "which type is recessive" answer.
    • Type III — progressively deforming; the most severe survivable form.
    • Type IV — moderate severity, with normal/white sclerae.
  • Clinical features:
    • Fragility fractures and progressive long-bone deformity.
    • Blue sclerae (types I and II).
    • Dentinogenesis imperfecta and hearing loss (otosclerosis).
    • Ligamentous laxity.
    • Wormian bones in the skull and basilar invagination.
    • Scoliosis, and olecranon apophyseal avulsion fractures (nearly pathognomonic in a child).
  • Medical treatment:
    • Bisphosphonates increase BMD and reduce fracture rate and pain, especially in types III and IV.
    • They do NOT correct deformity and do not change collagen quality.
    • Optimise calcium and vitamin D; physiotherapy and weight-bearing to build muscle and bone.
  • Surgery:
    • Realignment osteotomy with telescoping/expandable intramedullary rods (Fassier-Duval) rather than plates.
    • Plates create stress risers in brittle bone and fail at the plate ends — the technical exam point.
    • Scoliosis progresses and bracing is INEFFECTIVE (the brace deforms the compliant rib cage); early instrumented fusion is often required.
    • Anaesthetic considerations: cervical spine fragility, basilar invagination, chest wall restriction, and a hypermetabolic response (not true malignant hyperthermia).
  • Distinguishing OI from non-accidental injury — the key differential:
    • Suggesting OI: family history, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, wormian bones, low BMD, short stature and a consistent history.
    • Suggesting NAI: metaphyseal corner (bucket-handle) fractures, posterior rib fractures, fractures of differing ages, and injuries inconsistent with the developmental stage.
    • Genetic testing resolves the difficult case; a normal skeletal survey does not exclude either.

Definitions, obligations and the specific epidemiology of burnout in orthopaedics.

2 of 7 sittings2023, 2024S

  • Defining impairment:
    • Impairment is the inability to practise medicine safely and reliably because of a physical, mental or substance-use condition.

    • Emotional exhaustion.
    • Depersonalisation / cynicism.
    • Reduced sense of personal accomplishment.

    • Effective interventions are largely organisational: workload and schedule control, adequate staffing, scribes/administrative support, and team-based care.
    • Individual measures (mindfulness, resilience training) help but are secondary — offering only these is the classic wrong answer.
    • Every physician should have their own family physician and should not self-treat or rely on informal corridor care.

Professionalism: Prescribing for family/self

Boundaries of treating oneself, family and close friends.

2 of 7 sittings2020, 2023

  • The rule:
    • Canadian College policies and the CMA Code of Ethics direct that physicians should NOT treat themselves or members of their immediate family.
    • The only exceptions are a minor, self-limited condition or a genuine emergency when no other physician is available.
    • This includes prescribing, and it includes ordering investigations and giving informal advice that substitutes for proper care.

    • Loss of objectivity and impaired clinical judgement.
    • Incomplete history and examination — you will not do a proper genital, mental-health or substance-use history on a family member.

    • Failure to address sensitive issues the patient would raise with a stranger.
    • Inadequate follow-up, and the family member may feel unable to refuse or to seek a second opinion.

Tendon repair biology & suture materials

Repair strength determinants and suture characteristics.

2 of 7 sittings2021, 2023

  • The primary determinant of repair strength:
    • Strength is proportional to the NUMBER OF CORE SUTURE STRANDS crossing the repair site.
    • A 4-strand repair is roughly twice as strong as a 2-strand repair.
    • Adding a peripheral epitendinous suture adds ~20–50% strength and improves gliding by tidying the repair edge.
  • Other determinants of repair strength:
    • Suture calibre and material (braided polyester or a modern high-strength suture over plain nylon).
    • Locking versus grasping configuration — locking loops resist pull-through.
    • Purchase length ~1 cm from the cut end; shorter purchase pulls out.
    • Knot placement — inside the repair site avoids a gliding obstruction but slightly weakens it.
    • Gap formation of >3 mm predicts failure and adhesion.
    • Repairs are WEAKEST at 5–21 days, as the tendon softens before remodelling begins — the period of highest rupture risk.
  • Tendon-to-tendon anastomosis:
    • A PULVERTAFT WEAVE is the strongest construct and is the standard for tendon transfers.
    • End-to-end and Kessler-type repairs are weaker.
    • Side-to-side (loop) repairs are an alternative where the tendons are of similar calibre.
  • Suture reactivity — a straightforward ranking:
    • Monofilament non-absorbable (nylon, polypropylene) causes the LEAST tissue reaction and does not harbour bacteria.
    • Absorbable braided (Vicryl/polyglactin) is intermediate.
    • Braided multifilament (silk, braided polyester) causes the MOST reaction and harbours bacteria in its interstices.
    • Silk is the most reactive of the common sutures.
    • Monofilament handles and knots less well — the trade-off for low reactivity.
  • Rehabilitation:
    • Early controlled motion improves excursion and ultimate strength versus immobilisation.
    • Motion is the strongest modifier of adhesion formation — the biology of tendon healing is both intrinsic (tenocytes) and extrinsic (adhesions), and motion favours the intrinsic pathway.
    • The repair must be strong enough to tolerate the chosen protocol — this is why 4-strand-plus repairs enabled early active motion.

Biomechanics: Joint reaction force & offloading (cane use)

Free-body analysis of the hip and the mechanics of offloading.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • Why the hip joint reaction force is so high:
    • In single-leg stance the hip JRF is roughly 2.5–3× body weight.
    • The abductor moment arm is short (~5 cm) relative to the body-weight moment arm (~15 cm).
    • That 3:1 mechanical disadvantage means the abductors must generate about 2× body weight, and the JRF is the sum of that plus body weight.
  • The cane question — the recurring exam item:
    • A cane held in the CONTRALATERAL hand markedly reduces the joint reaction force — by up to about 50%.
    • It works by lengthening the effective moment arm across the body, so much less abductor force is required.
    • A cane in the IPSILATERAL hand shortens the lever arm and INCREASES the joint reactive force — the answer they are looking for.
  • Other ways to reduce hip joint reaction force:
    • Increasing femoral offset — lengthens the abductor lever arm.
    • Medialising the acetabular cup — shortens the body-weight moment arm.
    • Weight loss.
    • Carrying loads on the IPSILATERAL side (the load's moment arm then partly offsets body weight).
    • Trendelenburg lurch is the body's own compensation — shifting the centre of gravity over the affected hip shortens the body-weight moment arm.
  • The trade-off with offset:
    • Increasing offset reduces JRF and improves abductor efficiency, and improves stability and range of motion before impingement.
    • But it increases torque on the stem and trunnion (a driver of trunnionosis and of stem loosening) and increases polyethylene wear rate.
    • Excessive offset can also cause trochanteric bursitis and iliotibial band irritation.

Biostatistics: Health economics & cost analysis

Choosing the right economic analysis.

1 of 7 sittings2020

  • Choose the right analysis for the question:
    • Cost-MINIMISATION analysis — used when two interventions have EQUIVALENT clinical outcomes; you simply compare costs. This is the standard exam answer.
    • Cost-EFFECTIVENESS analysis — cost per natural unit of outcome (cost per fracture prevented, cost per infection avoided).
    • Cost-UTILITY analysis — cost per QALY, a preference-weighted unit; the only one that permits comparison across diseases.
    • Cost-BENEFIT analysis — converts everything, including health, to dollars.
  • Interpreting the result:
    • The output is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) = difference in cost ÷ difference in effect.
    • Willingness-to-pay thresholds in Canada are commonly cited around $50,000 per QALY (with $100,000 sometimes used).
    • An intervention that is both cheaper and better is "DOMINANT"; one that is more expensive and worse is dominated.
    • A QALY = one year of life in perfect health; utilities run from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health), derived from EQ-5D, SF-6D or standard-gamble/time-trade-off methods.
  • What must be specified in any economic evaluation:
    • The costs included: direct medical, direct non-medical (travel, home care) and indirect (lost productivity).
    • The perspective: societal (broadest, includes productivity), payer (the provincial health system), or hospital/institutional — the same intervention can be cost-saving from one perspective and costly from another.
    • The time horizon, which must be long enough to capture the relevant outcomes.
    • Discounting of future costs and benefits (conventionally ~1.5% in Canadian guidelines, 3% internationally).
    • Sensitivity analysis to test how robust the conclusion is to the assumptions.

Biostatistics: MCID & clinical vs statistical significance

Interpreting a small but statistically significant difference.

1 of 7 sittings2023

  • What the MCID is:
    • The smallest change in an outcome score that PATIENTS perceive as beneficial.
    • A difference that is statistically significant (p<0.05) but SMALLER than the MCID is NOT clinically important.
    • The classic illustration: a distal radius trial reporting a DASH difference of 5 when the MCID is ~10 — statistically real, clinically meaningless.
  • Why sample size distorts this:
    • A large trial can make a trivial difference statistically significant.
    • A small trial can miss an important one.
    • Always report and interpret the effect size and confidence interval alongside the p-value, and compare both against the MCID.
    • The best practice is to power the trial to detect the MCID, not an arbitrary difference.
  • Related concepts — distinguish them:
    • MDC (minimal detectable change) — the smallest change that exceeds measurement error. If MDC > MCID, the instrument cannot reliably detect a clinically important change.
    • PASS (patient acceptable symptom state) — an absolute score at which patients consider themselves well, not a change score.
    • SCB (substantial clinical benefit) — a higher bar than MCID.
    • MCID is anchor-based or distribution-based and varies with the population and baseline severity — it is not a fixed property of the instrument.
  • Common orthopaedic MCIDs worth recognising:
    • DASH ~10.
    • Constant score ~10.
    • WOMAC / HOOS / KOOS ~8–10.
    • VAS pain ~2/10 (or ~20 mm).
    • Oxford hip/knee scores ~5; PROMIS Physical Function ~3–8.

Biostatistics: Number needed to treat & effect measures

Converting risk differences into NNT.

1 of 7 sittings2021

  • Absolute measures — start here:
    • Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = control event rate − treatment event rate.
    • NNT = 1 / ARR.
    • Worked example: reamed nails 5% nonunion versus unreamed 10% → ARR = 0.05 → NNT = 20 (treat 20 patients with a reamed nail to prevent one nonunion).
  • Relative measures — and why they mislead:
    • Relative risk reduction (RRR) = ARR / control event rate — in the example above, 50%.
    • "A 50% reduction" sounds far more impressive than "an NNT of 20", yet they describe the same data.
    • Relative measures exaggerate benefit when the baseline risk is low — always ask for the absolute numbers.
    • Relative risk comes from cohort/RCT data; the odds ratio from case-control data, and it approximates RR only when the outcome is rare.
  • Number needed to harm and the caveats:
    • NNH is calculated the same way from the absolute risk INCREASE.
    • NNT is time-dependent and population-dependent — an NNT quoted without a time frame is incomplete.
    • NNT cannot be meaningfully pooled across trials with different follow-up durations or baseline risks.
  • Interpreting the magnitude:
    • A SMALLER NNT means a MORE effective treatment.
    • NNT approaching infinity means no effect (ARR approaching zero).
    • Judge an NNT against the seriousness of the outcome prevented and the burden/cost of the treatment — an NNT of 100 is excellent for preventing death and poor for relieving mild pain.

Biostatistics: Survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier)

Interpreting survivorship curves in arthroplasty and oncology.

1 of 7 sittings2025

  • What Kaplan-Meier does, and its main advantage:
    • It estimates the probability of an event-free interval over time.
    • It correctly handles CENSORED data — patients lost to follow-up, still event-free at the end of the study, or who die of unrelated causes.
    • Handling censoring is its main advantage over a simple percentage, which either discards those patients or wrongly counts them as successes.
  • Reading the curve:
    • The curve steps down only when an EVENT occurs.
    • Censored patients appear as tick marks and reduce the number at risk without counting as failures.
    • The right-hand tail is the LEAST reliable part, because few patients remain at risk and each event produces a large step.
    • Always inspect the number-at-risk table and the widening confidence interval before quoting "95% survivorship at 20 years".
  • The end point must be defined explicitly:
    • Revision for any cause versus revision for aseptic loosening versus radiographic loosening versus "revision or awaiting revision".
    • Changing the end point changes the curve dramatically — the commonest way survivorship figures are made to look better than they are.
    • Registry survivorship usually uses "revision for any reason", which is the most conservative and most comparable.
  • Competing risks — the registry caveat:
    • Death in an elderly cohort is a COMPETING risk: a patient who dies can never be revised.
    • Treating death as simple censoring INFLATES the Kaplan-Meier failure estimate.
    • A competing-risks (cumulative incidence function) analysis is more accurate in registry data, and gives a lower, truer revision rate in the elderly.
  • Comparing curves:
    • Log-rank test compares two or more curves — unadjusted.
    • Cox proportional hazards regression permits adjustment for covariates and yields a hazard ratio.
    • Cox assumes the hazards are proportional over time; crossing curves violate that assumption.

When gadolinium adds information and its risks.

1 of 7 sittings2020

  • How gadolinium works:
    • It is paramagnetic and shortens T1, so enhancing tissue appears BRIGHT on T1 post-contrast images.

    • Routine meniscal, ligamentous and most degenerative imaging.

The four principles and how they are worded on exams.

    • The POSITIVE duty to act for the patient's good.

    • The four-principles ("principlism") approach is the one Canadian exams use as the default.

    • Fidelity (keeping promises), veracity (truthfulness) and confidentiality are derived from the four principles.
    • Most exam vignettes hinge on AUTONOMY versus BENEFICENCE — the capable patient who refuses what you believe is best.

    • URGENT joint drainage — arthroscopic or open lavage; serial aspiration is acceptable for some joints (such as the knee) in selected cases.
    • Plus IV antibiotics, guided by Gram stain and then culture.
    • Cartilage damage from chondrolytic enzymes begins within HOURS — delay is the key predictor of poor outcome.
    • Repeat washout if the effusion and inflammatory markers do not settle; the hip and shoulder generally warrant formal drainage rather than aspiration alone.
  • Prosthetic joints are a different disease:
    • Much lower synovial WBC cut-offs — roughly >3,000/mm³ with >80% neutrophils in a chronic PJI (higher thresholds in the acute post-operative period).
    • Different management pathways: DAIR for acute infection with a well-fixed implant, versus one- or two-stage revision for chronic infection.
    • Use the MSIS/ICM criteria, including alpha-defensin and synovial leukocyte esterase, rather than the native-joint thresholds.
