Distal humerus fracture: ORIF vs total elbow arthroplasty

Fixation principles, the elderly distal humerus, and when to choose arthroplasty.

9 of 10 sittings2017 (x2), 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023 (x2), 2024F (x2), 2025

  • The fracture is intra-articular until proven otherwise — get a CT.
    • The AO/OTA 13-C pattern (complete articular, both columns) is the one that dominates decision-making; plain films consistently under-call comminution and coronal-plane articular splits.
    • A coronal shear component (Dubberley / "McKee type IV" capitellum-trochlea fragment) is the specific thing a CT is looking for — it is invisible or subtle on the AP and only hinted at by the "double-arc sign" on the lateral.
    • Traction views or 3-D reconstruction help plan whether the articular surface is reconstructible at all, which is the pivot point between ORIF and arthroplasty.
  • Exposure: the olecranon osteotomy buys articular visualisation, and nothing else comes close.
    • Cadaveric comparison of posterior approaches gives roughly 57% of the distal humeral articular surface with a chevron olecranon osteotomy, versus about 46% triceps-reflecting (Bryan-Morrey / TRAP) and only ~35% triceps-splitting.
    • The osteotomy must be an apex-distal chevron made at the bare area of the sigmoid notch (roughly 2–3 cm from the tip) — that non-articular strip is chosen deliberately so the saw cut does not violate cartilage. Pre-drill and pre-tap the fixation before cutting.
    • Complete the osteotomy with an osteotome rather than the saw, so the interdigitating cancellous surfaces key back together anatomically.
    • Cost of the osteotomy: osteotomy-site problems (non-union, symptomatic hardware, delayed union) are the price, in the range of 5–10%, and hardware removal is common. It also burns the olecranon if TEA later becomes necessary — so do not perform an olecranon osteotomy if you might convert to arthroplasty; use a paratricipital or triceps-reflecting exposure instead.
    • Extra-articular or simple articular patterns do not need it — a paratricipital (Alonso-Llames) approach preserves the extensor mechanism entirely.
  • Fixation: O'Driscoll's principles, and parallel beats orthogonal for the low fracture.
    • The principle set: every screw in the distal fragments should pass through a plate, engage a fragment on the opposite side that is also fixed to a plate, be as long as possible, engage as many articular fragments as possible, and the screws should interdigitate/lock the columns together like an arch; the plates must achieve compression at the supracondylar level and be strong enough and stiff enough to resist breaking or bending before union.
    • Parallel (medial and lateral, both in the sagittal plane) plating allows longer distal screws that cross from column to column, and biomechanically and clinically outperforms orthogonal (90-90) plating for very distal or comminuted articular fractures.
    • Orthogonal plating (posterolateral + medial) remains reasonable for higher supracondylar patterns and can be easier when the lateral column is short.
    • Never sacrifice articular reduction to preserve the supracondylar bone; if there is metaphyseal comminution, it is acceptable to shorten slightly at the supracondylar level to gain column-to-column contact, provided the olecranon and coronoid fossae are not filled with metal or bone — filling them blocks terminal flexion and extension.
    • Coronal shear (Dubberley) fragments are fixed with headless compression screws placed anterior-to-posterior, or posterior-to-anterior countersunk screws with posterior buttress plating for Dubberley type 3 (with posterior comminution) — the ones that displace, avascularise or are left unsupported are the ones that go on to AVN and collapse.
  • Total elbow arthroplasty in the elderly: supported by a Canadian randomised trial, but with a permanent lifting restriction.
    • The COTS multicentre RCT (McKee and colleagues) randomised patients over 65 with displaced intra-articular distal humeral fractures to ORIF or TEA. TEA gave better Mayo Elbow Performance Scores at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months and better DASH at 6 weeks and 6 months, with shorter operative time; ROM, complications and reoperation rates were similar.
    • Crucially, five of 20 patients randomised to ORIF were converted intra-operatively to TEA because the fracture proved unreconstructible — the intention-to-treat design is why this trial is quoted so often.
    • The TEA is a semiconstrained (linked) implant and carries a lifetime restriction of roughly 2.3 kg (5 lb) of repetitive lifting and 4.5 kg (10 lb) single-event lifting. This is the reason TEA is not offered to an active, independent or heavy-demand patient regardless of age.
    • Best indications for primary TEA: low, comminuted, unreconstructible articular fracture in a low-demand elderly patient; pre-existing inflammatory arthritis (a near-absolute indication — rheumatoid bone will not hold fixation); severe osteopenia.
    • Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty is an alternative when the columns and both collateral ligaments are intact and reconstructible but the articular segment is not; it avoids the lifting restriction but risks ulnar and radial head wear, and the evidence base is far thinner.
  • The ulnar nerve: identify and protect it always; transposition is not automatic.
    • Post-operative ulnar neuropathy after distal humerus ORIF is reported in the region of 10–15%, and the nerve must be identified and decompressed in every case.
    • Comparative data suggest routine anterior transposition does not reduce — and may increase — the rate of post-operative ulnar neuritis; the pragmatic position is in-situ decompression, with transposition reserved for a nerve that subluxates or would sit on hardware.
    • Warn the patient about ulnar symptoms pre-operatively; it is the single commonest neurological complication and the commonest source of dissatisfaction after an otherwise united fracture.
  • Heterotopic ossification and stiffness are the medium-term enemies.
    • HO after distal humerus fracture surgery is common (up to a third of series) and is driven by associated head injury, burns, delayed fixation and additional elbow trauma.
    • Indomethacin has not shown convincing benefit after elbow trauma and radiation prophylaxis is associated with non-union of the fracture and the olecranon osteotomy — routine prophylaxis is not recommended; early motion is the effective prophylaxis.
    • Fixation must therefore be stable enough for immediate active-assisted motion — if the construct cannot take that, reconsider arthroplasty.
  • Open fractures and the "distal humerus in the polytrauma patient".
    • Spanning external fixation or a hinged fixator is a legitimate temporising step; definitive articular reconstruction is best done once soft tissues allow, and delay of a week or two does not compromise ORIF outcomes.
    • An open fracture is a relative contraindication to acute TEA because of the infection risk in a linked implant with a large intramedullary footprint.

Radial head fracture & arthroplasty (overstuffing)

Mason classification, ORIF vs replacement, and detecting an overstuffed implant.

6 of 10 sittings2019, 2020 (x2), 2021, 2022, 2024S, 2024F

  • Classification: Mason, as modified by Broberg and Morrey and by Hotchkiss.
    • Type I — non-displaced or minimally displaced (<2 mm), no mechanical block.
    • Type II — displaced (>2 mm) partial articular fracture involving more than 30% of the head, potentially reconstructible.
    • Type III — comminuted, whole-head, not reconstructible.
    • Type IV (Johnston's addition) — any of the above with an associated elbow dislocation.
    • The classification is only useful once you have asked the two questions that actually drive treatment: is there a mechanical block to rotation, and is the elbow stable?
  • The stable, isolated fracture does well without surgery.
    • Long-term follow-up of uncomplicated Mason II and III fractures treated non-operatively shows good or excellent results in the large majority at 19 years, with a small loss of extension that patients do not notice.
    • Aspiration of the haemarthrosis with local anaesthetic is diagnostic and therapeutic: it converts pain-limited rotation into a true test for mechanical block.
    • Early motion is the treatment — immobilisation beyond a few days is the main modifiable cause of a stiff elbow after a radial head fracture.
  • ORIF versus replacement: the three-fragment rule.
    • Ring's series is the source of the working rule — fractures with MORE THAN THREE articular fragments had unsatisfactory results and a high rate of early failure, non-union and reoperation after ORIF, whereas fractures with three or fewer fragments did well.
    • So: ≤3 fragments, good bone, reducible → ORIF; >3 fragments, impaction, metaphyseal bone loss, or an unstable elbow → arthroplasty.
    • Do not excise the radial head acutely if the elbow is unstable or the interosseous membrane may be injured — delayed excision of an isolated head in a stable elbow is well tolerated, but acute excision in an unstable elbow produces proximal radial migration, valgus instability and chronic pain.
    • Fix through the "safe zone": with the forearm in neutral, the arc of the head from the radial styloid to Lister's tubercle (roughly the lateral 90°–110° arc) does not articulate with the lesser sigmoid notch, so hardware there does not block rotation.
  • Overstuffing: the single most tested point about radial head arthroplasty.
    • Overlengthening is NOT reliably diagnosed on the lateral radiograph or by the radiocapitellar joint — the radiocapitellar relationship looks normal even with several millimetres of overlength, which is exactly why it is a favourite examination point.
    • The reliable radiographic sign is INCONGRUITY / GAPPING of the LATERAL (ulnohumeral) joint on the AP view — the medial ulnohumeral joint hinges shut and the lateral side wedges open. Canadian work from Western showed the lateral ulnohumeral joint space widens measurably once the implant is overlong.
    • A CONTRALATERAL (normal-side) elbow radiograph is the reference standard for detecting overlengthening — comparison of ulnohumeral joint congruity between the two sides reliably identifies overlengthening, and this comparison should be used whenever there is doubt.
    • Intra-operatively the reference is the PROXIMAL EDGE OF THE LESSER SIGMOID NOTCH, which lies at, or roughly 1 mm distal to, the articular margin of the native radial head. Trial to that landmark, not to the resected fragments (which are impacted and undersize you) and not to the capitellum.
    • Consequences of overstuffing: capitellar cartilage wear and erosion, painful loss of terminal extension, pain with rotation, ulnohumeral arthrosis and early implant failure. Even 2 mm is enough to alter contact mechanics; 4 mm produces marked capitellar overload and a fixed flexion contracture.
    • Undersizing is far better tolerated than overstuffing — when in doubt, err small.
  • Implant choice: metal, and generally smooth-stemmed / loose-fitting.
    • Silicone radial head implants are obsolete — they fragment, produce synovitis and provide no meaningful longitudinal or valgus stability.
    • Monopolar heads give better resistance to subluxation when the soft tissues are deficient; bipolar heads self-align and may reduce capitellar wear when the collaterals are intact. Cadaveric work shows bipolar designs are less able to stabilise the radiocapitellar joint when soft tissues are disrupted — a relevant caution in the terrible triad.
    • Smooth "loose-fitting" stems are designed to be intentionally non-fixed; a lucency around a smooth stem is expected and is not by itself evidence of failure, whereas progressive lucency around an ingrowth stem is.
    • The implant is a spacer, not a reconstruction of the ligaments — it restores the secondary valgus and longitudinal restraint, but the LUCL and, where relevant, the MCL still have to be dealt with.
  • Longitudinal forearm instability (Essex-Lopresti) must be excluded before the head is removed.
    • Comminuted radial head fracture + wrist/DRUJ pain = suspect interosseous membrane disruption; get wrist radiographs and compare ulnar variance with the other side.
    • The intra-operative radius pull test (traction on the radius with the head excised; >3 mm of proximal migration is abnormal) confirms it.
    • If present, the radial head must be replaced, never excised, and the DRUJ addressed — a missed Essex-Lopresti is one of the worst functional outcomes in upper-limb trauma.
  • The radial head that will not stay reduced — think Monteggia and think ulna.
    • A radial head dislocation in the presence of a proximal ulna fracture is a Monteggia lesion; anatomic reduction of the ULNA reduces the radial head in almost every case, so persistent subluxation means the ulna is still malreduced — usually residual ulnar bow, shortening, or loss of the normal proximal ulna dorsal angulation.
    • Genuine mechanical blocks to reduction: interposed annular ligament, infolded capsule or periosteum, an interposed osteochondral fragment, and interposition of the radial head itself through a capsular buttonhole.
    • The point that is easy to get backwards: a PIN palsy is a consequence of the dislocation, not a cause of failed reduction — a nerve does not block a joint from reducing, and its presence is not an indication for exploration in a closed injury with an anatomically reduced ulna.
    • Always check the radiocapitellar line (a line drawn along the radial neck must pass through the capitellum on every view); a Monteggia is missed by not drawing it.

Terrible triad & complex elbow instability

The three-component injury and its stepwise surgical algorithm.

5 of 10 sittings2019, 2020, 2022 (x2), 2023 (x2), 2025

  • Definition and why it earned the name.
    • Posterior elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture. Before a structured protocol existed, this combination had such high rates of recurrent instability, stiffness, non-union and early arthrosis that it was labelled "terrible".
    • All three of the elbow's stabilisers are gone at once: the coronoid (primary bony buttress against posterior translation), the radial head (principal secondary valgus and axial stabiliser) and the LUCL (primary restraint to posterolateral rotatory subluxation).
    • The mechanism is the same Horii circle as a simple dislocation — axial load, valgus and supination, with soft-tissue failure progressing from LATERAL to MEDIAL — but with enough energy to fracture the radial head and shear the coronoid.
  • The standard surgical protocol (Canadian, and the reason outcomes changed).
    • Step 1 — coronoid. Fix or reattach it if it is a large fragment or if the elbow remains unstable; the anterior capsule inserts on the coronoid, so a lasso/suture repair of the capsule can be enough for a small tip fragment.
    • Step 2 — radial head. Fix if ≤3 fragments; REPLACE if comminuted. Excision is contraindicated in this injury — the head is the load-sharing valgus restraint that is holding the repaired coronoid and LUCL out of tension.
    • Step 3 — LUCL, repaired LAST, back to its isometric point at the centre of the capitellum, usually with a suture anchor or transosseous sutures, together with the common extensor origin.
    • Step 4 — reassess stability through a full arc with the forearm in neutral. If the elbow still subluxates before 30° of extension, add the MCL repair, and if it is still unstable, apply a hinged external fixator.
    • Step 5 — early active motion, in an overhead protocol if there is any residual concern, with the forearm positioned in pronation if the lateral side was the problem and supination if the medial side was.
  • The coronoid: O'Driscoll classification is the one that drives approach.
    • Type 1 — TIP fracture (subtypes by 2 mm or >2 mm). This is the terrible-triad coronoid.
    • Type 2 — ANTEROMEDIAL FACET (subtypes: rim; rim + tip; rim + sublime tubercle). This is the varus posteromedial rotatory instability pattern, not a terrible triad.
    • Type 3 — BASAL (body/base, >50% of the coronoid height), including the transolecranon and Monteggia-associated patterns.
    • The older Regan-Morrey system (tip / <50% / >50%) still appears, but it misses the anteromedial facet entirely, which is precisely why O'Driscoll's system replaced it.
    • Approach follows the fragment: a tip fragment is reachable through the lateral (Kocher or extensor-split) window created by the radial head fracture; an anteromedial facet or basal fragment needs a MEDIAL approach — the "over-the-top" (Hotchkiss) or the FCU-splitting Taylor-Scham interval, with the ulnar nerve identified first. Do not try to buttress an anteromedial facet from the lateral side.
  • Selective non-operative treatment is legitimate in a defined subgroup.
    • Canadian work from Western has shown that carefully selected terrible triads can be treated non-operatively when the joint is concentrically reduced on a true lateral, there is a stable arc of at least 30°–130°, the radial head fracture does not block rotation and is minimally displaced, and the coronoid fragment is small (type 1).
    • Such patients need weekly radiographs for the first 2–3 weeks to confirm the reduction is maintained, and any loss of concentricity means surgery.
    • Conversely, any residual subluxation, a "drop sign" (widened ulnohumeral space), or a large coronoid fragment means operative treatment.
  • Does the MCL have to be repaired?
    • The MCL is torn in essentially every terrible triad, but operative series show good results WITHOUT routine MCL repair provided the lateral column, radial head and coronoid have been restored and the elbow is stable intra-operatively.
    • Repair it selectively: persistent instability after the lateral-side reconstruction, or an elbow that requires more than 30° of flexion to stay reduced.
    • Adding a medial approach for its own sake increases stiffness, HO and ulnar nerve morbidity.
  • Getting the sequence and the ligament wrong — the classic pitfalls.
    • The LUCL originates from the ISOMETRIC POINT AT THE CENTRE OF THE CAPITELLUM, not from the lateral epicondyle tip and not anteriorly or distally — a non-isometric repair either over-constrains flexion or fails in extension.
    • Repairing the LUCL before the coronoid and radial head is the classic error — the lateral repair then blocks access, and it is tensioned against a joint that is not yet reduced.
    • Excising a comminuted radial head "because it will be fixed by the ligament repair" leads to recurrent instability and is the mistake this protocol was written to eliminate.
    • Prolonged immobilisation to protect the repair produces a stiff elbow with a flexion contracture — the whole point of achieving stability in theatre is to permit motion at once.
  • Outcomes and what to warn about.
    • With the protocol, most patients achieve a functional arc, with a residual flexion contracture in the order of 10°–20° that is well tolerated.
    • Complications to counsel for: stiffness (commonest), heterotopic ossification, ulnar neuropathy, post-traumatic arthrosis, recurrent instability and reoperation for hardware or contracture release.
    • Post-traumatic arthrosis correlates with residual subluxation — a concentrically reduced elbow tolerates surprisingly imperfect articular reconstruction, whereas a subluxated one deteriorates quickly.

Distal biceps tendon rupture & repair

Diagnosis, one- vs two-incision technique, and the consequences of non-operative treatment.

7 of 10 sittings2017, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Who ruptures it, and how it presents.
    • Typical patient: a man in his 40s, dominant arm, eccentric load on a flexed, supinated elbow (catching a falling weight). Smoking and anabolic steroid use are the recognised risk factors.
    • The tendon fails at the radial tuberosity insertion. Pain and a "pop", then ecchymosis and a proximally retracted muscle belly (reverse Popeye) if the lacertus fibrosus (bicipital aponeurosis) is also torn.
    • An intact lacertus is the great masquerader — it prevents retraction, so the contour looks normal and the diagnosis is missed.
  • Examination: the hook test is the one to know.
    • O'Driscoll's hook test — with the elbow flexed 90° and actively supinated, the examiner hooks an index finger under the lateral edge of the tendon from the lateral side. A cord-like structure that can be hooked = intact; nothing to hook = complete rupture. Reported 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity in the original series, outperforming MRI.
    • Hook from the LATERAL side: hooking from medial can catch the lacertus fibrosus and give a false-negative.
    • Supporting signs: passive forearm rotation test (no tendon excursion), the biceps crease interval, and weakness that is disproportionately in supination.
    • MRI in the FABS position (Flexed elbow, ABducted shoulder, forearm Supinated) lays the tendon out along its length and is the imaging of choice when the examination is equivocal or the tear is partial; ultrasound is operator-dependent.
  • The consequences of leaving it alone — the numbers.
    • Morrey's biomechanical and clinical work gives the classic figures: an unrepaired complete rupture loses roughly 40% of SUPINATION STRENGTH and 30% of FLEXION STRENGTH, with endurance losses that are larger still (in the order of 50% for supination and 40% for flexion).
    • Supination is affected more than flexion because brachialis and brachioradialis compensate for flexion, but nothing substitutes for the biceps as the principal supinator with the elbow flexed.
    • The strength deficit is greatest in supination with the elbow FLEXED at 90°, which is the mechanical advantage position for the biceps.
    • Non-operative management is therefore a reasonable choice only for the elderly, low-demand, non-dominant, or medically unfit patient, and they must be counselled specifically about fatigue and endurance with repetitive supination — not just peak strength — plus cramping and cosmetic deformity.
  • One incision versus two: the Canadian randomised trial.
    • A randomised clinical trial from Western (Grewal, Athwal, King and colleagues) compared the single anterior incision (with a cortical button) against the modified two-incision (Boyd-Anderson, muscle-splitting) technique. Final strength, ROM and patient-reported outcomes were EQUIVALENT; the two-incision group had marginally better final flexion strength, and the single-incision group had more transient neurapraxias.
    • Single anterior incision — higher rate of nerve injury, overwhelmingly LATERAL ANTEBRACHIAL CUTANEOUS NERVE (LABCN) neurapraxia, which is the COMMONEST COMPLICATION of distal biceps repair overall; the posterior interosseous nerve is the feared injury and is at risk with lateral retraction and with exposure of the far side of the tuberosity.
    • Two-incision (muscle-splitting modification) — historically associated with heterotopic ossification and proximal RADIOULNAR SYNOSTOSIS, which is why Boyd-Anderson's original subperiosteal exposure of the ulna was abandoned in favour of a muscle-splitting approach through the extensor mass without exposing the ulna. Modern rates are low but the risk is not zero.
    • So the honest summary: the techniques are equivalent in outcome; they differ in their complication profile — nerve on the anterior side, ossification on the posterior side.
  • Anatomy that gets tested: the footprint and the 90° of rotation.
    • The musculotendinous unit rotates approximately 90° EXTERNALLY (supination-ward) as it travels from the muscle belly to the radial tuberosity — the surface that faces anteriorly at the musculotendinous junction faces laterally at the insertion. Reattaching the tendon without respecting this rotation makes the repair non-anatomic.
    • The footprint is a ribbon on the ULNAR/POSTERIOR aspect of the radial tuberosity, roughly 21 × 7 mm; the SHORT head inserts DISTALLY (a more effective elbow flexor because of its longer moment arm) and the LONG head inserts PROXIMALLY and further from the axis of rotation (a more effective SUPINATOR).
    • Anatomic footprint restoration matters for supination torque, and this is the mechanical argument in favour of the two-incision technique — it reaches the posteroulnar footprint directly, whereas an anterior approach tends to place the tendon on the anterior aspect of the tuberosity.
    • The forearm must be fully PRONATED to bring the tuberosity anterolaterally into view and to move the PIN away from the operative field during an anterior approach.
  • Fixation choice.
    • Biomechanically, cortical button (endobutton) fixation is the strongest construct, followed by interference screw, then suture anchors, then bone tunnels.
    • Cortical button requires drilling the far cortex — the specific hazard is PIN injury from over-penetration or from an oblique trajectory; drill in maximal pronation and aim slightly ulnarly.
    • Combining a button with an interference screw adds compression to the footprint; the practical outcome differences among constructs are small once the repair survives early rehabilitation.
    • Repair within 3–4 weeks — beyond that the tendon retracts, the sheath scars, and reconstruction with an Achilles, semitendinosus or hamstring allograft may be required.
  • Complications and rehabilitation.
    • LABCN neurapraxia is the commonest complication overall (largely transient); then PIN palsy, HO/synostosis, re-rupture (low, ~1–5%), and posterior interosseous or superficial radial paraesthesia.
    • Radioulnar synostosis is the complication that ruins the outcome, because it removes rotation permanently; prophylactic indomethacin is often used after a two-incision repair, though the evidence is weak.
    • Rehabilitation with modern fixation is early active-assisted motion with no formal immobilisation, resisted supination and flexion deferred to about 6–8 weeks, and unrestricted loading at around 4–6 months.

Medial ulnar collateral ligament injury & Tommy John

The valgus stabiliser of the elbow, diagnosis, and reconstruction.

6 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2022, 2023, 2024S, 2024F

  • Anatomy: the anterior bundle is the ligament that matters.
    • The MUCL complex has three parts — anterior bundle, posterior bundle and transverse (Cooper's) ligament. The transverse ligament runs olecranon-to-coronoid on the same bone and contributes nothing to stability.
    • The ANTERIOR BUNDLE is the primary restraint to valgus stress through the functional arc (roughly 30°–120° of flexion). It has a tighter anterior band (taut in extension through mid-flexion) and a posterior band (taut in flexion beyond ~60°), giving reciprocal function across the arc.
    • Origin: the anteroinferior aspect of the medial epicondyle, just distal to the axis of rotation. Insertion: the SUBLIME TUBERCLE of the coronoid — the footprint that must be recreated in reconstruction.
    • The posterior bundle is a thickening of the posterior capsule, taut beyond 90° only, and is the structure released in valgus extension overload.
  • Valgus stability in EXTENSION versus FLEXION — the repeatedly tested distinction.
    • IN FULL EXTENSION, valgus load is shared roughly EQUALLY IN THIRDS between the MCL, the BONY ULNOHUMERAL ARTICULATION and the ANTERIOR CAPSULE. With the elbow extended, the anterior capsule is a genuine valgus stabiliser and the olecranon is engaged in its fossa.
    • IN FLEXION (beyond about 30°, and especially at 90°), the ANTERIOR BUNDLE OF THE MCL becomes the PRIMARY valgus restraint, providing more than half of the resistance, because the olecranon disengages from its fossa and the anterior capsule goes slack.
    • The RADIAL HEAD is the principal SECONDARY valgus stabiliser — it does not matter much with an intact MCL, but it becomes critical once the MCL is deficient. This is why the radial head must never be excised in a valgus-unstable elbow.
    • The flexor-pronator mass (particularly FCU, and to a lesser degree FDS) is the dynamic valgus stabiliser overlying the ligament — strengthening it is the basis of non-operative management, and the FCU is the muscle that must be elevated or split to expose the ligament.
    • The common trap: structures that are not valgus stabilisers — the transverse (Cooper's) ligament, the anconeus and the LUCL — are the ones offered alongside genuine restraints.
  • The moving valgus stress test is the best physical examination test.
    • O'Driscoll's moving valgus stress test: with the shoulder abducted 90° and a constant modest valgus torque applied, the elbow is quickly extended from full flexion. The test is positive when medial elbow pain is reproduced, is maximal at the medial epicondyle, and occurs within the "SHEAR ANGLE" of roughly 120° down to 70° — the same arc in which late cocking and acceleration occur.
    • Reported sensitivity 100% and specificity 75% against surgical exploration — the highest of any clinical test for MUCL insufficiency, and the answer when asked for the "best" or "most sensitive" test for medial elbow instability.
    • Other tests: the milking manoeuvre (patient's thumb pulled with the elbow flexed >90°), and the static valgus stress test at 30° of flexion (unlocks the olecranon), which is insensitive because the deficit is usually a few millimetres of laxity, not gross opening.
    • Imaging: MR ARTHROGRAPHY is the imaging standard and reveals the "T-sign" — contrast tracking around the distal insertion at the sublime tubercle, indicating a partial undersurface tear that a plain MRI misses. Dynamic ultrasound with valgus stress quantifies medial joint gapping and is increasingly used in throwing athletes.
  • The throwing elbow: valgus extension overload.
    • The late-cocking and acceleration phases generate valgus torque near the tensile failure load of the native ligament, so the MUCL fails by repetitive attritional microtrauma rather than a single event; the athlete describes a "pop" and the sudden inability to throw at velocity.
    • The associated triad: medial tension (MUCL, ulnar nerve traction, medial epicondyle apophysitis in the skeletally immature), lateral compression (capitellar OCD, radiocapitellar chondral wear) and posterior shear (posteromedial olecranon osteophyte, olecranon fossa chondromalacia).
    • Aggressive resection of a posteromedial olecranon osteophyte increases valgus strain on the MUCL and can precipitate frank ligament failure — resect conservatively and only the osteophyte, never native olecranon.
    • In the skeletally immature thrower the same load avulses the medial epicondyle apophysis ("little leaguer's elbow") rather than tearing the ligament — pitch counts and rest, not reconstruction.
  • Non-operative management first, in almost everyone.
    • Rest from throwing, NSAIDs, then a supervised flexor-pronator strengthening and kinetic-chain / throwing-mechanics programme, with a staged interval throwing protocol. Roughly half of throwers return to their previous level.
    • PRP has been studied for partial tears with modest supportive evidence, mainly in partial undersurface tears in younger athletes; it is not a substitute for reconstruction in a complete tear in a high-level thrower.
    • The non-throwing patient with MUCL insufficiency after an elbow dislocation rarely needs reconstruction — valgus instability of daily living is unusual once the radial head and coronoid are intact.
  • Reconstruction: Jobe, its modifications, and the docking technique.
    • Jobe's original figure-of-eight reconstruction detached the flexor-pronator mass and routinely transposed the ulnar nerve — effective, but with a high rate of ulnar neuropathy.
    • Modified Jobe / muscle-splitting approaches split the FCU without detaching the origin and transpose the nerve only selectively, which dropped the neurological complication rate substantially.
    • The docking technique (Altchek) passes the graft limbs into a single humeral tunnel and ties over a bony bridge; it uses smaller humeral tunnels, avoids the vulnerable posterior humeral cortex, and allows the graft to be tensioned before final fixation. Reported return to play at or above previous level of roughly 90% in throwers.
    • Graft choice: ipsilateral palmaris longus is first choice (absent in ~15%), then gracilis, plantaris or a contralateral palmaris.
    • Tunnel placement is the technical crux: the ulnar tunnel straddles the sublime tubercle and the humeral tunnel sits at the anteroinferior medial epicondyle at the isometric point. The ULNAR NERVE is the structure at risk with the posterior ulnar tunnel and with the posterior humeral cortex.
    • UCL REPAIR WITH INTERNAL BRACE AUGMENTATION has re-emerged for proximal or distal avulsions with good tissue quality in younger, non-professional athletes; biomechanically it matches modified Jobe reconstruction and permits a faster return (roughly 6 months versus 12–18 months for reconstruction). It is not for a mid-substance attritional tear in a professional thrower.
    • Complications: ulnar neuropathy (the commonest, ~5–15% depending on nerve handling), medial epicondyle fracture through the tunnels, stiffness, and graft-site morbidity.

Simple elbow dislocation & posterolateral rotatory instability

The Horii circle, examination for PLRI and treatment of the simple dislocation.

4 of 10 sittings2018 (x2), 2020 (x2), 2021 (x2), 2025

  • The Horii circle: soft tissue fails from LATERAL to MEDIAL.
    • O'Driscoll's circle describes a three-stage progression of soft-tissue disruption produced by axial load + valgus + external rotation (supination) of the forearm on the humerus, most often a fall on the outstretched hand.
    • Stage 1 — the LUCL fails, giving posterolateral rotatory subluxation which reduces spontaneously.
    • Stage 2 — the anterior and posterior capsule fail, giving an incomplete (perched) dislocation with the coronoid resting on the trochlea.
    • Stage 3 — the MCL fails (3A posterior bundle, 3B whole MCL, 3C the entire distal humerus stripped of soft tissue, leaving a grossly unstable elbow), giving complete posterior dislocation.
    • The direction is LATERAL to MEDIAL, and the FIRST structure to fail is the LUCL, NOT the MCL — this is the single most examined fact about simple dislocations, and the answer options routinely offer the reverse.
    • The LUCL avulses from its HUMERAL origin at the isometric point on the lateral epicondyle/capitellar centre far more often than from its ulnar (supinator crest) insertion, which is why repair is usually a humeral-sided reattachment.
  • "Simple" means no fracture, and simple dislocations do well.
    • A simple dislocation is a pure soft-tissue injury. Post-reduction radiographs and a true lateral are mandatory to confirm concentric reduction and to exclude a coronoid or radial head fracture — the presence of either changes the injury and the treatment entirely.
    • After closed reduction under sedation, assess the stable arc: the elbow is taken through flexion-extension in neutral, pronation and supination under fluoroscopy. Most simple dislocations are stable through 30°–130°.
    • Early motion beats immobilisation. Randomised and comparative evidence shows that immobilisation beyond about a week produces more flexion contracture and worse early function with no reduction in recurrent instability. Recurrent instability after a simple dislocation is genuinely rare (in the order of 1–2%).
    • Splint in 90° of flexion for a few days at most, then begin active motion with an extension block only if a stable arc could not be achieved. Avoid passive stretching, which provokes heterotopic ossification.
    • Position of forearm rotation for rehabilitation follows which side is deficient: PRONATION protects a lateral (LUCL) injury; SUPINATION protects a medial (MCL) injury; neutral if both.
    • Operative treatment for a simple dislocation is reserved for the elbow that will not stay reduced within a functional arc, will not reduce concentrically (interposed tissue), or is a stage 3C injury — and then it is a lateral (± medial) ligament repair, sometimes with a hinged external fixator.
  • Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI): the chronic sequel.
    • PLRI is the commonest pattern of chronic elbow instability. The LUCL is incompetent, so the ULNA AND RADIUS ROTATE AWAY FROM THE HUMERUS AS A UNIT, externally rotating and subluxating posterolaterally, with the radial head displacing posterior to the capitellum and the annular ligament and PRUJ remaining intact.
    • Causes: previous elbow dislocation (commonest), iatrogenic (over-aggressive lateral epicondylitis release or arthroscopic release extending posterior to the midline of the radial head), chronic cubitus varus, and repeated corticosteroid injection.
    • Symptoms are mechanical — clunking, locking or apprehension with the elbow in extension and supination while pushing up out of a chair, rather than pain alone.
  • Examination for PLRI, and the test that CANNOT assess it.
    • Lateral pivot-shift test of the elbow (O'Driscoll) — supine, arm overhead, forearm SUPINATED, apply valgus and axial compression while flexing from extension; the radial head subluxates in extension and reduces with a palpable clunk at roughly 40°–70° of flexion. Usually only reproducible under general anaesthesia in the awake patient; in clinic it produces apprehension only.
    • Posterolateral rotatory drawer test — at 40° of flexion, an anteroposterior rotatory force subluxates the ulna and radius off the humerus.
    • Chair push-up (chair sign) and the push-up / table-top relocation tests — the patient pushes up with the forearms supinated and the elbows abducted; apprehension or a clunk near 40° of extension is positive, and it is abolished when the examiner's thumb blocks the radial head.
    • The VARUS STRESS TEST CANNOT ASSESS PLRI — PLRI is a rotatory instability, and a pure varus stress does not reproduce the external-rotation/axial-load mechanism. Similarly, the valgus stress test and the moving valgus stress test assess the MEDIAL side and are irrelevant to PLRI. This is the standard "which of these cannot be used" point.
    • Radiographic adjunct: stress radiographs or fluoroscopy during the pivot shift under anaesthesia, and MRI (or MR arthrography) showing LUCL discontinuity at the humeral origin.
  • Treating chronic PLRI.
    • Acute or subacute avulsion with good tissue → direct LUCL repair to the isometric point, with or without internal-brace augmentation.
    • Chronic attenuation → LUCL RECONSTRUCTION with a tendon graft (palmaris, gracilis or triceps fascia) in a docking or yoke configuration, from the supinator crest of the ulna to the ISOMETRIC POINT AT THE CENTRE OF THE CAPITELLUM.
    • The isometric point is the crux: it lies at the centre of the capitellar arc on a true lateral, and a graft placed anterior, posterior or distal to it will either block motion or stretch out.
    • If the instability arises from cubitus varus, correct the deformity (distal humeral osteotomy) as well — reconstructing the ligament against a malaligned humerus fails.
    • Reported good/excellent outcomes are around 80–90%; the residual problem is a small flexion contracture.
  • Complications and things to look for later.
    • Loss of terminal extension of 10°–15° is the rule, not a complication, after any elbow dislocation, and should be part of the consent conversation.
    • Heterotopic ossification (particularly with associated head injury, repeated manipulation or passive stretching), ulnar neuropathy, and recurrent or residual instability.
    • Persistent or recurrent pain after a "simple" dislocation should prompt a CT — a missed coronoid or radial head fracture, or a non-concentric reduction, is the usual explanation.

Total elbow arthroplasty

Indications, design, and why TEA loosens sooner than TKA.

5 of 10 sittings2019, 2020, 2022, 2024F, 2025

  • Linked (semiconstrained) versus unlinked (unconstrained) — the fundamental design choice.
    • Linked / semiconstrained (e.g. Coonrad-Morrey) — a sloppy hinge that permits roughly 7°–10° of varus-valgus and rotational laxity, so soft tissues still share load. It does not require competent collateral ligaments or intact bone stock, which is why it is the implant for acute trauma, revision, tumour resection and gross instability. The trade-off is that load transmitted through the hinge drives bushing wear and aseptic loosening.
    • Unlinked / unconstrained (e.g. Souter-Strathclyde, Kudo, Latitude in unlinked mode) — a resurfacing design that depends entirely on intact collateral ligaments, an intact flexor-pronator and extensor origin, and adequate bone stock. Lower loosening rates, but instability/dislocation is the characteristic failure mode.
    • Convertible designs allow an unlinked implant to be linked later without revising the stems, which is the modern pragmatic compromise.
    • The radial head component is optional in most systems; it is generally retained/replaced in unlinked designs (to share valgus load) and omitted in linked designs.
  • Indications, and the one restriction that defines the operation.
    • Established indications: inflammatory (rheumatoid) arthritis with intractable pain, acute unreconstructible distal humeral fracture in a low-demand elderly patient, distal humeral non-union, post-traumatic arthritis in a low-demand patient, and tumour reconstruction.
    • Every TEA carries a permanent lifting restriction of approximately 2.3 kg (5 lb) repetitively and 4.5 kg (10 lb) as a single event. This is the reason TEA is inappropriate for an active patient, a manual worker, or anyone using a walking aid through that arm.
    • Relative contraindications: young age, high demand, a patient who cannot comply with the weight restriction, and a non-functioning or absent extensor mechanism.
    • Absolute contraindication: active infection; also a Charcot elbow and an insensate limb.
    • Outcomes are consistently better in rheumatoid than post-traumatic elbows — the RA patient's low demand is protective, while the post-traumatic patient is younger, more active, and often has stiffness, prior surgery and colonised tissue.
  • Why TEA loosens and fails sooner than hip or knee arthroplasty.
    • The elbow is a non-weight-bearing joint that nonetheless sees very high joint reaction forces — up to three times body weight across the ulnohumeral joint with the arm used as a lever, because the muscle moment arms are short and the external moment arm (the forearm and hand) is long.
    • The soft-tissue envelope is thin, the bone stock in the distal humerus and proximal ulna is narrow and osteopenic, and the cement mantle is correspondingly small.
    • Torsional load through a hinge in a small-diameter canal generates high stresses at the cement-bone interface — hence loosening (usually of the ULNAR component first in a linked design) and periprosthetic fracture.
    • Survivorship: broadly ~90% at 10 years in rheumatoid patients and roughly 70–85% at 10 years in post-traumatic elbows, which is materially worse than a contemporary TKA or THA.
  • Approach and the extensor mechanism.
    • Bryan-Morrey (triceps-reflecting) — the tricess is elevated in continuity with the forearm fascia and periosteum from medial to lateral; excellent exposure but risks triceps insufficiency.
    • Triceps-splitting (Campbell / van Gorder) and the TRAP (triceps-reflecting anconeus pedicle) approach preserve the anconeus blood supply.
    • Triceps-sparing / paratricipital (Alonso-Llames) approaches work either side of an intact triceps and are increasingly preferred for acute fracture TEA, because the fracture fragments can simply be removed and the extensor mechanism never violated — earlier active extension and no triceps insufficiency.
    • Triceps insufficiency is a specific and difficult complication: the patient cannot extend against gravity, and reconstruction (anconeus rotation, Achilles allograft) is unreliable.
    • The ULNAR NERVE must be identified and protected in every TEA; ulnar neuropathy is among the commonest complications, and the nerve is routinely decompressed and often transposed.
  • Complications, in order of importance.
    • Infection is the headline number — roughly 3–8%, the HIGHEST of any major joint arthroplasty, driven by the thin subcutaneous soft-tissue envelope, prior surgery and immunosuppressed rheumatoid patients. Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci dominate.
    • Aseptic loosening and bushing/polyethylene wear — the commonest reason for late revision in a linked implant; presents as progressive pain with activity and radiographic lucency, and an isolated bushing exchange is possible if the stems are well fixed.
    • Periprosthetic fracture — classified by the Mayo system (site: humeral/ulnar; type I periarticular around a well-fixed stem, type II along the stem, type III proximal/distal to the stem); management follows the same logic as elsewhere, namely fixation for a well-fixed stem and revision for a loose one.
    • Ulnar neuropathy, triceps insufficiency, wound problems and instability (the last essentially confined to unlinked implants).
    • Overall complication and reoperation rates are high — commonly quoted in the 20–40% range at medium-term follow-up.
  • Managing the infected TEA.
    • Acute infection with a well-fixed implant → debridement, antibiotics and implant retention with a bushing/polyethylene exchange, though success is lower than at the hip or knee.
    • Chronic infection → two-stage revision with an antibiotic spacer; the intervening period is functionally poor because the elbow is flail.
    • Salvage options when reimplantation is not possible: resection arthroplasty (a flail but often surprisingly functional elbow), arthrodesis (poorly tolerated — the elbow cannot be positioned to serve both hand-to-mouth and hygiene) or, rarely, amputation.

Cubital tunnel syndrome & ulnar nerve transposition

Sites of compression and the commonest site of persistent symptoms after transposition.

4 of 10 sittings2018, 2020, 2022, 2025

  • The five sites of compression, proximal to distal.
    • Arcade of Struthers — a fascial band roughly 8 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle between the medial head of triceps and the medial intermuscular septum. (Not to be confused with the ligament of Struthers, which tethers the MEDIAN nerve to a supracondylar process.)
    • Medial intermuscular septum — the structure that must be excised whenever the nerve is transposed, or it becomes a new compression point.
    • Medial epicondyle / retrocondylar groove — where a valgus deformity or an osteophyte tents the nerve.
    • Osborne's ligament (the cubital tunnel retinaculum / arcuate ligament) spanning the two heads of FCU — the commonest site of compression, and the roof that tightens with flexion.
    • The deep flexor-pronator aponeurosis (FCU fascia) at the exit of the tunnel — this is the classic site of PERSISTENT or RECURRENT symptoms after an inadequate decompression, because it is the most distal and the easiest to leave behind through a short incision.
  • Why elbow flexion is the aggravating position.
    • With flexion the cubital tunnel narrows because the arcuate ligament tightens and the medial collateral ligament bulges into the floor; the cross-sectional area falls by more than half.
    • Intraneural pressure rises several-fold with the elbow flexed, and further still with the shoulder abducted and the wrist extended. The nerve is also stretched roughly 5–8 mm as it courses posterior to the axis of rotation.
    • This explains the history — night symptoms with the elbow flexed under the pillow, and paraesthesia while holding a phone or driving — and the treatment, which begins with night extension splinting at 30°–45° and activity modification.
  • Clinical findings and how to localise the lesion.
    • Sensory: numbness of the small finger and ulnar half of the ring finger, and critically the DORSAL ULNAR HAND — the dorsal cutaneous branch arises 5–8 cm proximal to the wrist, so dorsal numbness localises the lesion PROXIMAL to the wrist and excludes Guyon's canal.
    • Motor: first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis wasting, Froment's sign (thumb IP flexion substituting adductor pollicis with FPL), Jeanne's sign (MCP hyperextension), Wartenberg's sign (abducted small finger from unopposed EDM), and clawing of the ring and small fingers.
    • The ulnar paradox: a more proximal lesion produces LESS clawing, because FDP to the ring and small fingers is also denervated and cannot flex the DIP joints.
    • Provocative tests: elbow flexion test (best when combined with pressure over the tunnel), Tinel's over the cubital tunnel, and the scratch-collapse test.
    • McGowan grading — I sensory symptoms only, no weakness; II weakness and measurable intrinsic wasting; III severe weakness with marked atrophy and paralysis. Grade III has the worst prognosis for recovery regardless of what is done, and intrinsic atrophy at presentation is the strongest negative prognostic factor.
    • Electrodiagnostics: slowing of conduction velocity across the elbow (<50 m/s, or a drop of >10 m/s compared with the forearm segment), with a conduction block across the elbow. Normal studies do not exclude the diagnosis in early disease.
  • Non-operative treatment works in mild disease.
    • Night extension splinting, elbow pads, avoidance of sustained flexion and of leaning on the elbow, and nerve-gliding exercises; roughly half of McGowan grade I patients settle.
    • Corticosteroid injection into the cubital tunnel has not been shown to help and risks direct nerve injury — it is not recommended.
  • In-situ decompression versus transposition — the evidence says they are equivalent.
    • Randomised trials and the Cochrane review show NO significant difference in clinical or neurophysiological outcome between simple in-situ decompression and anterior transposition, while transposition carries more wound complications and more surgical morbidity.
    • Consequently simple in-situ decompression (open or endoscopic) is the default for idiopathic cubital tunnel syndrome.
    • Transposition is reserved for a nerve that SUBLUXATES over the epicondyle, a valgus or post-traumatic deformity, revision surgery, or when the nerve must be moved out of the way of planned hardware.
    • Subcutaneous versus submuscular transposition: submuscular gives the deepest, best-padded bed and is favoured in thin patients and revision cases, at the cost of a longer recovery; subcutaneous is quicker and adequate in most.
    • The cardinal technical rule for any transposition: release the nerve from the ARCADE OF STRUTHERS to the FCU APONEUROSIS and EXCISE THE MEDIAL INTERMUSCULAR SEPTUM. Transposing a nerve while leaving the septum intact simply creates a sharp new kink — the classic cause of a worse result after surgery.
    • Medial epicondylectomy decompresses and removes the tension point without devascularising the nerve, but risks medial elbow instability if the MCL origin is violated (limit resection to less than about 20% of the epicondyle, staying anterior to the MCL origin) and can cause persistent medial elbow pain.
  • Persistent or recurrent symptoms after surgery.
    • Persistent symptoms (never improved) usually mean incomplete decompression — most often the distal FCU aponeurosis, or a retained medial intermuscular septum after transposition.
    • Recurrent symptoms (improved then returned) usually mean perineural scarring, a kink at the septum, or subluxation of a decompressed nerve.
    • Revision options: complete re-release with submuscular transposition, and vascularised or synthetic nerve wrapping for scarring.
    • Advanced disease with fixed intrinsic wasting may need tendon transfers for clawing and pinch rather than further nerve surgery.

Olecranon fracture & tension band fixation

Tension band mechanics, plate fixation, and the elderly comminuted olecranon.

6 of 10 sittings2017, 2018 (x2), 2019, 2024S, 2024F, 2025

  • Classification and what actually drives the decision.
    • Mayo classification — Type I undisplaced; Type II displaced with a stable ulnohumeral joint; Type III displaced with an unstable joint (a fracture-dislocation). Each is subdivided A (non-comminuted) and B (comminuted).
    • The two questions that determine treatment are: is the ulnohumeral joint STABLE, and is the fracture COMMINUTED? Everything else follows.
    • Schatzker's descriptive system (transverse, transverse-impacted, oblique, comminuted, oblique-distal, fracture-dislocation) is the older alternative and is still quoted for the oblique fracture extending DISTAL to the midpoint of the sigmoid notch, which is the pattern that defeats a tension band.
  • Tension band wiring: how it works and when it fails.
    • The principle: the triceps generates a tensile (distraction) force on the dorsal cortex; a dorsally placed band converts that tension into COMPRESSION at the articular (volar) cortex as the elbow flexes. This requires an intact volar cortical buttress to push against.
    • Indications: a simple TRANSVERSE, NON-COMMINUTED Mayo type IIA fracture at or proximal to the midpoint of the sigmoid notch, with a stable elbow.
    • Tension banding is CONTRAINDICATED for comminuted fractures, oblique fractures distal to the midpoint of the sigmoid notch, and any fracture-dislocation (Mayo III) — there is no volar buttress, so the construct simply shortens the ulna and collapses the articular surface, producing incongruity and arthrosis.
    • Technique details that get tested: two parallel 1.6 mm K-wires engaging the ANTERIOR (volar) cortex of the proximal ulna, with the wire tips bent and impacted into the olecranon; a figure-of-eight 18-gauge wire through a transverse drill hole in the dorsal ulna, tightened on both limbs. Intramedullary K-wire placement is associated with backing out and hardware prominence.
    • Hardware prominence is the dominant complication of ANY olecranon fixation — reported in the region of 70–80% for tension band wiring, with removal rates commonly above 30–50%. This single fact drives most of the contemporary alternatives.
  • Tension band SUTURE versus tension band WIRE.
    • Heavy non-absorbable suture (or braided high-strength suture, sometimes with suture anchors) used as the tension band has been shown in biomechanical and clinical comparative studies to give equivalent fracture stability, union and functional outcome to metal wire in simple transverse fractures.
    • The advantage of suture is a substantial reduction in symptomatic hardware and in the reoperation rate for implant removal, which is the main morbidity of the wire technique.
    • The suture construct is not a solution for comminution — the same indication limits apply, and comminuted or unstable patterns still need a plate.
  • Plate fixation.
    • Indications: comminution, oblique fractures extending distal to the coronoid, Mayo type III fracture-dislocations, transolecranon fracture-dislocations, and Monteggia patterns.
    • Use a DORSALLY applied contoured (locking) plate wrapping over the tip of the olecranon, with a long screw down the intramedullary canal and screws capturing the coronoid fragment. The dorsal position is the tension side of the ulna.
    • Restore the length and the articular congruity of the sigmoid notch first; the bare area means a small central gap is acceptable but shortening is not.
    • Do not compress across a comminuted zone — bridge it, or the sigmoid notch narrows and the elbow becomes incongruent and stiff.
    • Plates are also prominent, but symptomatic hardware rates are lower than tension band wiring.
  • The elderly, comminuted olecranon fracture: non-operative treatment is a real option.
    • Duckworth's randomised trial in Edinburgh comparing operative fixation with non-operative treatment in patients over 75 was stopped early because of the complication rate in the operative arm: similar functional outcomes (DASH, Mayo score) but far more complications and reoperations after surgery.
    • Non-operative treatment means a short period of immobilisation (about a week) followed by early active motion, accepting a fibrous non-union or malunion with an extension lag of roughly 20°–30°, which is well tolerated in a low-demand patient. Active extension against gravity is usually preserved by the intact triceps expansion.
    • The prerequisite is a STABLE ulnohumeral joint — a fracture-dislocation must be fixed regardless of age.
    • Fragment excision with triceps advancement is the alternative for a comminuted proximal fragment; up to about 50% of the olecranon can be excised provided the coronoid and the collateral ligaments are intact and the joint is stable. Excising more, or excising in the presence of an unstable elbow or a coronoid deficiency, produces instability.
  • Transolecranon fracture-dislocation versus Monteggia — a distinction worth being clear about.
    • Transolecranon fracture-dislocation: a high-energy anterior fracture-dislocation in which the forearm (radius AND ulna together) translates anteriorly through a comminuted olecranon, but the PROXIMAL RADIOULNAR JOINT REMAINS INTACT — the radial head stays with the ulna.
    • Monteggia: a proximal ulna fracture with DISRUPTION OF THE PRUJ and dislocation of the radial head. Bado I anterior radial head dislocation with anterior ulnar angulation; Bado II posterior; Bado III lateral; Bado IV anterior dislocation with fractures of both the proximal ulna and radius. The Jupiter subclassification of Bado II (IIA through the sigmoid notch/coronoid; IIB at the metaphysis distal to the coronoid; IIC diaphyseal; IID comminuted) matters because IIA involves the coronoid and needs it addressed.
    • Adult Monteggia is an operative injury: anatomic ORIF of the ulna, restoring LENGTH, ROTATION and the normal PROXIMAL ULNA DORSAL ANGULATION and varus bow. Anatomic ulnar reduction reduces the radial head in the overwhelming majority — the radial head is not routinely opened.
    • If the radial head remains subluxated after ulnar fixation, the ULNA IS MALREDUCED until proven otherwise (residual bow, shortening, malrotation, or loss of the dorsal angulation); the other genuine causes are interposed annular ligament, infolded capsule or periosteum, and an interposed osteochondral fragment, addressed through a lateral (Kocher) approach.
    • A PIN palsy is a CONSEQUENCE of the radial head dislocation, not a CAUSE of failed reduction, and in a closed injury with an anatomically reduced ulna it is observed — most recover spontaneously over about 3 months.
    • Associated injuries to look for in Bado II (the adult, osteoporotic, low-energy pattern): LUCL disruption, coronoid fracture and radial head fracture — effectively a spectrum with complex elbow instability. The olecranon fracture itself provides the "osteotomy" through which the coronoid can be fixed.
  • Complications and rehabilitation.
    • Symptomatic hardware (dominant), stiffness and loss of terminal extension, ulnar neuritis, non-union (uncommon, ~1–5%), infection and post-traumatic arthrosis.
    • Loss of extension of 10°–20° is expected; a residual arc of roughly 45°–100° is functionally limiting and should prompt assessment for heterotopic ossification, hardware impingement in the olecranon fossa, articular incongruity or capsular contracture rather than simply more physiotherapy.
    • Rehabilitation: immediate active-assisted flexion and extension with a stable construct; avoid resisted extension for about 6 weeks so the triceps does not distract the fixation.

Posterior interosseous nerve & radial tunnel syndrome

PIN compression sites and how to avoid iatrogenic injury.

3 of 10 sittings2020, 2023, 2025

  • Anatomy: where the radial nerve divides and where the PIN is vulnerable.
    • The radial nerve pierces the lateral intermuscular septum roughly 10 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle and divides at about the level of the radiocapitellar joint into the superficial radial (sensory) and the posterior interosseous (motor) nerve.
    • The PIN then enters the radial tunnel, which extends from the radiocapitellar joint to the distal edge of the supinator, and passes between the two heads of supinator, exiting to supply the extensor compartment.
    • Motor supply order matters: the radial nerve proper supplies brachioradialis and ECRL proximal to the division, so a PIN palsy SPARES brachioradialis, ECRL and wrist extension power; the PIN supplies ECRB (variable), supinator, EDC, EDM, ECU, APL, EPL, EPB and EIP.
    • The PIN lies an average of 3–4 cm distal to the radiocapitellar joint on the anterior radial neck with the forearm in PRONATION, and moves closer with SUPINATION — hence "pronate to protect the PIN" during a lateral approach or radial head fixation.
  • The five compression sites within the radial tunnel (proximal to distal).
    • Fibrous bands at the radiocapitellar joint / anterior to the radial head.
    • Leash of Henry — the recurrent radial vessels crossing the nerve.
    • Medial (fibrous) edge of the ECRB.
    • Arcade of Frohse — the fibrous proximal edge of the superficial head of supinator; the commonest site of compression.
    • Distal edge of the supinator.
  • Radial tunnel syndrome versus PIN syndrome — the distinction that is repeatedly tested.
    • RADIAL TUNNEL SYNDROME = PAIN WITHOUT WEAKNESS. Aching in the extensor mass with no motor deficit and no sensory loss; electrodiagnostic studies are typically NORMAL, so it remains a clinical diagnosis.
    • PIN SYNDROME (PIN PALSY) = WEAKNESS WITHOUT SENSORY LOSS. The PIN is purely motor, so there is NO NUMBNESS — the characteristic finding is finger drop (inability to extend the MCP joints) and thumb extension weakness, with WRIST EXTENSION PRESERVED but in RADIAL DEVIATION, because ECRL (radial nerve proper) is intact while ECU (PIN) is not.
    • Wrist extension in radial deviation with an inability to extend the fingers, and normal sensation, is the classic examination picture of a PIN palsy, and it distinguishes it immediately from a high radial nerve palsy (which gives a true wrist drop plus dorsal first-web numbness).
    • Wartenberg's syndrome is the third entity — compression of the SUPERFICIAL radial (sensory) nerve between brachioradialis and ECRL in the distal forearm, giving dorsoradial numbness with NO weakness.
  • Separating radial tunnel syndrome from lateral epicondylitis.
    • They coexist in perhaps 5% of resistant "tennis elbows", and radial tunnel syndrome is the standard explanation for a lateral epicondylitis that fails treatment.
    • Point of maximal tenderness: lateral epicondylitis is tender over the lateral epicondyle / ECRB origin; radial tunnel syndrome is tender 3–5 cm DISTAL to the epicondyle, over the mobile wad and supinator.
    • Provocative tests for radial tunnel syndrome: RESISTED SUPINATION with the elbow extended, and RESISTED LONG-FINGER (middle-finger) EXTENSION with the elbow extended and wrist neutral, which loads the ECRB edge against the nerve.
    • A DIAGNOSTIC LOCAL ANAESTHETIC BLOCK into the radial tunnel that abolishes the pain (and produces a temporary PIN palsy) while a separate block at the epicondyle does not, is the most useful confirmatory test.
    • Non-operative treatment (rest, splinting, activity modification, NSAIDs) is tried for 3–6 months; surgical decompression of the radial tunnel gives less predictable results than most other nerve decompressions (good outcomes commonly quoted around 60–70%), and patients must be counselled accordingly.
  • Causes of PIN palsy, and when to operate.
    • Traumatic — Monteggia fracture-dislocation (the classic association, particularly Bado type I and III), radial head/neck fracture and dislocation, and iatrogenic injury.
    • Compressive/space-occupying — lipoma, ganglion arising from the radiocapitellar joint, and rheumatoid synovitis of the radiocapitellar joint (a PIN palsy in a rheumatoid patient is synovitis until proven otherwise, and the differential is extensor tendon rupture).
    • Inflammatory — Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy), in which severe pain precedes a patchy motor palsy; treatment is non-operative and most recover.
    • A closed PIN palsy associated with a fracture or dislocation is OBSERVED, provided the bones are anatomically reduced; recovery is expected over about 3 months, and EMG at 6 weeks establishes the baseline.
    • Exploration is indicated for an OPEN injury, a palsy that appears AFTER manipulation or fixation, a palsy with no clinical or electrical recovery by 3–6 months, or a suspected mass lesion.
  • Avoiding iatrogenic PIN injury.
    • PRONATE the forearm fully for any lateral (Kocher or Kaplan) approach to the radial head or neck — this moves the nerve distally and ulnarly, away from the operative field.
    • Do not place retractors or dissect on the anterior radial neck more than about 3 cm distal to the radiocapitellar joint, and keep subperiosteal.
    • The nerve is also at risk from anterior cortical drill or button over-penetration in distal biceps repair, from bicortical plate screws in the proximal radius, and from arthroscopic anterolateral portal placement.
    • In a Kocher interval (anconeus / ECU), keep the capsular incision ANTERIOR to the midline of the radial head and avoid extending posteriorly past the equator — drifting posteriorly detaches the LUCL and produces iatrogenic PLRI; the Kaplan (ECRB/EDC) interval is more anterior and correspondingly closer to the PIN.

Elbow arthroscopy: Portals & nerve risk

Portal anatomy and which nerve is at greatest risk.

3 of 10 sittings2018, 2021, 2024S

  • General principles that make elbow arthroscopy safer than its reputation.
    • The elbow has the highest density of neurovascular structures immediately adjacent to its portals of any joint, and the capsule is thin, so nerves sit only millimetres away.
    • Distend the joint with 20–30 mL of saline before the first portal — distension displaces the anterior neurovascular structures anteriorly, increasing the nerve-to-capsule distance, and it is the single most protective manoeuvre.
    • Flex the elbow to 90° for anterior portals: flexion relaxes and displaces the anterior neurovascular bundle away from the capsule. Extending the elbow brings the nerves closer.
    • Incise skin only, then spread bluntly with a haemostat; use blunt trocars, never sharp ones, and avoid suction on the shaver near the anterior capsule.
    • Contraindications: any distortion of the normal anatomy that removes the safety margin — most importantly a previously TRANSPOSED (or subluxating) ulnar nerve, which must be identified and protected or the procedure abandoned, plus prior soft-tissue reconstruction, severe ankylosis and local infection.
  • The anteromedial portals: median nerve territory.
    • PROXIMAL ANTEROMEDIAL portal — approximately 2 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle and just anterior to the medial intermuscular septum, aimed at the radial head. This is the standard and SAFEST anterior working/viewing portal, with the median nerve typically 12–22 mm away.
    • ANTEROMEDIAL (distal, "direct" anteromedial) portal — roughly 2 cm anterior and 2 cm distal to the medial epicondyle. THIS IS THE PORTAL CLOSEST TO A NEUROVASCULAR STRUCTURE: the MEDIAN NERVE lies only about 4–7 mm away, and the brachial artery is close behind it. It is the answer whenever the question asks which portal is nearest a nerve or carries the highest risk.
    • The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (MABC) is the structure most often actually injured on the medial side (it lies within a few millimetres of both medial portals) — a painful neuroma or numbness is the usual result.
    • The ULNAR NERVE is the structure at risk when the portal drifts POSTERIOR to the medial intermuscular septum, and it is also at risk during posteromedial gutter debridement and capsulectomy.
  • The anterolateral portals: radial nerve / PIN territory.
    • PROXIMAL ANTEROLATERAL portal — about 2 cm proximal and 1–2 cm anterior to the lateral epicondyle. The safest of the lateral portals, with the radial nerve roughly 10–14 mm away.
    • ANTEROLATERAL (distal) portal — about 3 cm distal and 1 cm anterior to the lateral epicondyle. The RADIAL NERVE / PIN is only about 3–7 mm away, making it the most dangerous of the lateral portals and, in some cadaveric series, the closest of all portals to a major nerve.
    • MID-LATERAL / "soft spot" (direct lateral) portal — in the triangle bounded by the lateral epicondyle, radial head and olecranon tip. It is used for the posterior radiocapitellar joint and the posterolateral gutter; the posterior antebrachial cutaneous nerve is the structure at risk, and injury is usually a cutaneous nuisance rather than a functional loss.
  • Posterior portals.
    • POSTEROCENTRAL / straight posterior (transtriceps) portal — 3 cm proximal to the olecranon tip in the midline, through the triceps tendon; the safest posterior portal, with the ulnar nerve a comfortable distance medially.
    • POSTEROLATERAL portal — 3 cm proximal to the olecranon tip, just lateral to the triceps; the standard posterior viewing portal for the olecranon fossa and posterior compartment.
    • A DIRECT POSTEROMEDIAL PORTAL IS NOT USED — the ulnar nerve sits directly in the way. Work in the posteromedial gutter is done from the posterocentral or posterolateral portal, and the ulnar nerve should be identified (or formally decompressed) before any posteromedial capsular release or osteophyte resection.
  • Indications and complications.
    • Established indications: loose body removal (the best indication and the most predictable result), synovectomy, capsular release for contracture, debridement of osteoarthritis (osteocapsular arthroplasty / arthroscopic Outerbridge-Kashiwagi), lateral epicondylitis release, osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum, and plica excision.
    • Overall complication rates are around 5–15%; the vast majority are TRANSIENT nerve palsies. Permanent nerve injury is rare (well under 1%) but catastrophic.
    • Risk factors for nerve injury: contracture release / capsulectomy, rheumatoid arthritis (thin, adherent capsule and distorted anatomy) and a previously operated or transposed ulnar nerve.
    • Persistent drainage from a portal is the commonest minor complication; deep infection is rare.
    • For arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis release, stay ANTERIOR to the equator of the radial head — extending the release posteriorly detaches the LUCL and creates iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability.

Elbow stiffness, contracture & hinged external fixation

Post-traumatic contracture and the axis of rotation.

2 of 10 sittings2020, 2024F

  • Define the target: the functional arc.
    • Morrey's classic functional arc is 30°–130° of flexion-extension and 50° of pronation with 50° of supination, derived from the range needed for 15 activities of daily living.
    • Contemporary work using modern tasks (mobile phones, keyboards, personal hygiene) suggests the real requirement is wider — closer to 25°–145° and 65° of pronation with 80° of supination.
    • Practically, a flexion contracture greater than about 30° or flexion less than about 120° is what patients notice, and either is a reasonable threshold for offering a release.
    • Loss of EXTENSION is far better tolerated than loss of FLEXION — flexion is what brings the hand to the face; patients with limited flexion compensate poorly.
  • Classify the contracture, because it determines the operation.
    • INTRINSIC — the block is inside the joint: articular incongruity, intra-articular malunion, osteophytes, loose bodies, or an ossified capsule. Needs the joint itself addressed.
    • EXTRINSIC — the joint surface is preserved and the block is capsular, ligamentous, muscular (myostatic) or heterotopic bone outside the joint. This is the group that does best with a capsular release.
    • MIXED is the commonest reality after trauma; most "extrinsic" contractures have acquired some intrinsic component by the time they present.
    • A hard, abrupt end-feel suggests a bony block (osteophyte, HO, malunion); a soft, springy end-feel suggests capsular contracture. Get a CT before any release — it distinguishes the two and maps heterotopic bone.
  • Non-operative treatment first, and static progressive splinting beats dynamic.
    • The elbow's capsule responds to prolonged low-load stress (stress relaxation and creep), not to forceful stretching.
    • STATIC PROGRESSIVE splinting (the joint is held at a fixed angle that is incrementally advanced, allowing the tissue to relax at each increment) outperforms DYNAMIC splinting (a constant spring load) for post-traumatic contracture, and is better tolerated.
    • Serial casting/turnbuckle splinting is a valid alternative. Allow 6–12 months of a supervised programme before considering surgery, since spontaneous improvement continues well past 6 months.
    • Aggressive passive stretching and manipulation under anaesthesia are counterproductive — they provoke pain, microtrauma and heterotopic ossification.
  • Operative release: open versus arthroscopic.
    • Open lateral "column procedure" (Mansat & Morrey) — anterior capsulectomy and coronoid/radial fossa debridement through a lateral column exposure, extended medially if flexion is limited. Reliable, and the workhorse.
    • Medial "over-the-top" release is preferred when the posteromedial gutter, the posterior bundle of the MCL, or the ulnar nerve must be addressed — and the posterior bundle of the MCL is the specific structure to release for LOST FLEXION.
    • Outerbridge-Kashiwagi / "ulnohumeral arthroplasty" (fenestration of the olecranon fossa, with excision of olecranon and coronoid osteophytes) is the classic operation for the primary osteoarthritic (male, manual worker) elbow, which characteristically loses terminal extension and flexion but retains a comfortable mid-arc.
    • Arthroscopic osteocapsular arthroplasty achieves comparable gains in experienced hands with less morbidity, but has a higher risk of nerve injury — capsular release is precisely when the anterior nerves are least protected.
    • The ulnar nerve must be decompressed (and often transposed) whenever pre-operative flexion is less than about 90–100°, when the patient has pre-existing ulnar symptoms, or when flexion will be increased substantially — a large intra-operative gain in flexion acutely stretches a chronically shortened nerve and precipitates a post-operative palsy.
    • Radial head excision is avoided at the time of contracture release unless the elbow is stable and the radiocapitellar joint is the pain generator, because it removes a stabiliser from an already-compromised joint.
  • Hinged external fixation, and the axis of rotation.
    • Indications: maintaining a concentric reduction after release of a severe contracture, protecting ligament reconstruction in chronic or recurrent instability, and distraction interposition arthroplasty in the young arthritic elbow.
    • THE HINGE MUST BE ALIGNED WITH THE ELBOW'S TRUE FLEXION-EXTENSION AXIS, which is the line drawn from the CENTRE OF THE CAPITELLUM (its arc of curvature) to the CENTRE OF THE TROCHLEA — that line emerges laterally at the centre of the capitellar arc and medially at the ANTEROINFERIOR aspect of the medial epicondyle, i.e. at the origin of the anterior bundle of the MCL, not at the tip or the centre of the medial epicondyle.
    • The axis is found intra-operatively by placing a guidewire under a PERFECT LATERAL fluoroscopic image, in which the capitellum, trochlea and the arcs of the trochlear sulcus form CONCENTRIC CIRCLES ("bull's-eye"); the wire is placed at the centre of those circles.
    • Even a few millimetres of malalignment of the hinge produces abnormal joint loading, pain, loss of motion and pin loosening — the axis pin is the single most important technical step in applying a hinged fixator.
    • The rotation axis is essentially fixed (not a changing instant centre) through the functional arc, which is what makes a simple hinge acceptable at all; there is a small amount of valgus carrying-angle change with flexion.
  • Heterotopic ossification.
    • Risk factors: traumatic brain or spinal cord injury, burns, elbow fracture-dislocation, delayed or repeated surgery, forceful passive manipulation.
    • NSAID prophylaxis (indomethacin) has weak and conflicting evidence after elbow trauma and carries a non-union risk; radiation prophylaxis increases non-union of fractures and olecranon osteotomies and is generally avoided in the acute trauma setting. Early controlled active motion is the best prophylaxis.
    • Timing of excision: the old teaching of waiting 12–18 months for radiographic "maturity" has been largely abandoned. Current practice is EARLY excision once the soft tissues are quiescent and the skin is healed, often at 4–6 months, because delay entrenches capsular contracture and cartilage loss; a CT defines the bone and its relation to the ulnar nerve.
    • Excision of HO around the medial side mandates ulnar nerve identification and usually transposition; the nerve is frequently encased.
  • Outcomes and expectations.
    • Contracture release typically gains roughly 40°–50° of arc, with most series ending near a functional arc; the gain achieved on the table is not fully retained, so the intra-operative arc should exceed the target.
    • Post-operative care is as important as the release: immediate continuous active and active-assisted motion, a brachial plexus or indwelling catheter for analgesia, and static progressive splinting from the outset.
    • Recurrence is real, particularly in intrinsic contractures and in patients with head injury; counsel accordingly.

Lateral epicondylitis

Natural history, prognostic factors and the evidence for treatment.

2 of 10 sittings2023, 2025

  • It is a tendinosis, not a tendinitis.
    • Lateral epicondylitis ('tennis elbow') is an angiofibroblastic tendinosis of the ECRB origin — a degenerative process with disorganised collagen and neovascularisation, not an inflammatory one (hence the more accurate term 'epicondylosis').
    • Peak incidence is 35–55 years, and most patients are not tennis players — the usual driver is repetitive occupational gripping and wrist extension.
    • The ECRB origin is the pathological structure; the EDC origin is variably involved.
  • Natural history is the single most important counselling point.
    • 80–90% resolve within 12 months regardless of treatment.
    • This is the basis for a legitimate 'wait and see' policy, and for insisting on a prolonged trial of non-operative care before any surgery.
  • Predictors of a poor outcome at one year are largely psychosocial.
    • High baseline pain intensity and long symptom duration.
    • Manual or repetitive occupational demands, and involvement of the dominant arm.
    • Coexisting neck or shoulder pain.
    • Poor coping and psychological distress — kinesiophobia and catastrophising.
    • A workers' compensation claim.
    • The point to take away: psychosocial factors predict outcome more strongly than physical findings.
  • Corticosteroid injection: better at 6 weeks, worse at 6–12 months.
    • Injection gives genuine short-term (2–6 week) relief.
    • But it produces WORSE outcomes at 6–12 months than physiotherapy or wait-and-see, with a higher recurrence rate — a landmark and frequently tested finding (Bisset, BMJ 2006; Coombes, JAMA 2013).
    • Eccentric strengthening and physiotherapy are the mainstay, and are what should be offered first.
  • The rest of the treatment ladder.
    • Counterforce bracing and topical or oral NSAIDs for short-term symptom control.
    • Extracorporeal shockwave therapy — mixed evidence.
    • PRP — inconsistent evidence, with no clear superiority to placebo in the best-designed trials.
    • Surgery only after at least 6–12 months of failed non-operative treatment: débridement of the degenerate ECRB origin, open, percutaneous or arthroscopic, with roughly 80–90% success.
    • Keep any release ANTERIOR to the midline of the radial head — extending posteriorly detaches the LUCL and produces iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability.
  • Always reconsider the diagnosis when it does not behave.
    • Radial tunnel syndrome — tender 3–5 cm distal to the epicondyle, pain on resisted supination and resisted long-finger extension.
    • Posterolateral rotatory instability — mechanical symptoms and apprehension rather than pure pain.
    • Radiocapitellar plica, cervical radiculopathy (C6) and osteochondral pathology of the capitellum.
    • Suspect these particularly when the pain is more distal than the epicondyle or does not follow the expected course.

Triceps tendon rupture

The rarest tendon rupture and its associations.

2 of 10 sittings2020, 2025

  • The rarest tendon rupture, and where it fails.
    • Triceps rupture is the least common of all tendon ruptures.
    • It typically occurs at the OLECRANON INSERTION as an avulsion, often carrying a bony flake, rather than at the musculotendinous junction.
    • Mechanism: an eccentric load on a contracting triceps, or a fall on an outstretched hand.
  • Associations to have on the tip of your tongue.
    • Anabolic steroid use and local corticosteroid injection.
    • Chronic renal failure with secondary hyperparathyroidism, and hyperparathyroidism generally.
    • Olecranon bursitis and previous olecranon surgery.
    • Rarely, after total elbow arthroplasty (triceps insufficiency following a triceps-reflecting approach).
  • Diagnosis: the flake sign, and the partial tear that fools you.
    • A palpable defect and weakness of active extension against gravity.
    • The 'FLAKE SIGN' — a small avulsed fleck of olecranon bone on the lateral radiograph — is pathognomonic.
    • A PARTIAL TEAR MAY STILL ALLOW ACTIVE EXTENSION AGAINST GRAVITY, because the lateral triceps expansion and the anconeus remain intact — this is why partial tears are missed.
    • Use the modified Thompson-type test (extension against resistance with the arm supported) and MRI when the diagnosis is in doubt.
  • Treatment.
    • Complete ruptures require surgical repair — transosseous suture through cruciate bone tunnels, or suture anchors, restoring the anatomic footprint.
    • Delayed repair is more difficult and may need a graft — Achilles allograft or anconeus rotation.
    • Partial tears with intact extension against gravity (less than about 50% of the tendon) can be treated non-operatively in extension splinting for 4–6 weeks, with good results in low-demand patients.
  • Rehabilitation and complications.
    • Post-operatively: protected extension with gradual reintroduction of flexion, avoiding RESISTED extension for 3 months.
    • The principal complications are re-rupture and loss of terminal extension.

Varus posteromedial rotatory instability

The anteromedial coronoid facet fracture pattern.

1 of 10 sittings2021 (x2)

  • The injury: a distinct pattern, and a different mechanism from the terrible triad.
    • Varus posteromedial rotatory instability (VPMRI) is produced by an axial load with a VARUS moment and INTERNAL rotation (posteromedial rotation) of the forearm on the humerus — the mirror image of the valgus/supination/external-rotation mechanism that produces a posterolateral dislocation.
    • The result is a shear fracture of the ANTEROMEDIAL FACET OF THE CORONOID (O'Driscoll type 2) together with avulsion of the LATERAL COLLATERAL LIGAMENT COMPLEX (LUCL) from the humerus.
    • The RADIAL HEAD IS CHARACTERISTICALLY INTACT, and there is usually no frank dislocation — which is exactly why the injury is missed. The presence of an intact radial head with a coronoid fracture should raise VPMRI, not terrible triad.
    • The anteromedial facet is the vulnerable part of the coronoid because it overhangs the ulnar shaft medially and is unsupported by the metaphysis — it is a cantilevered buttress that shears off under varus load.
  • Why it matters: an apparently trivial fracture that destroys the elbow.
    • The anteromedial facet is the medial buttress of the ulnohumeral articulation; losing it allows the trochlea to subluxate medially and rotate into varus, so the joint articulates on the medial edge instead of the concave sigmoid notch.
    • Untreated, this produces rapid, severe POST-TRAUMATIC ARTHROSIS within months, plus persistent pain and instability — a far worse natural history than the size of the fragment suggests.
    • Look for the radiographic clues on the AP: widening or incongruity of the MEDIAL ulnohumeral joint space and a small medial fleck. A CT is mandatory — the fragment and its size are consistently underestimated on plain films, and the plain lateral can look almost normal.
  • O'Driscoll type 2 subtypes.
    • 2.1 — anteromedial RIM only.
    • 2.2 — anteromedial rim + TIP.
    • 2.3 — anteromedial rim + SUBLIME TUBERCLE, i.e. involving the insertion of the anterior bundle of the MCL, which adds valgus instability to the varus pattern and is the most unstable subtype.
    • Only type 2 is the VPMRI pattern — type 1 (tip) belongs to the terrible triad and type 3 (basal) to transolecranon and Monteggia patterns.
  • Examination and stress testing.
    • GRAVITY VARUS STRESS TEST — with the shoulder in neutral rotation (arm at the side) and the elbow flexed, gravity applies a varus moment; fluoroscopy shows the medial ulnohumeral joint opening or the ulna rotating into varus.
    • Assess for posterolateral rotatory instability as well, since the LUCL is torn by definition — the lateral pivot-shift will typically be positive under anaesthesia.
    • Stress views should be done under anaesthesia and with fluoroscopy; the awake examination is unreliable.
  • Treatment: fix the facet from the MEDIAL side, and repair the LUCL.
    • Non-operative treatment is only acceptable for a genuinely small (type 2.1), non-displaced fragment in a CONCENTRICALLY REDUCED joint that is STABLE on gravity varus stress, and it requires weekly radiographs for 3 weeks to confirm the reduction is maintained. Any medial joint widening means surgery.
    • The fragment CANNOT be adequately buttressed from a lateral approach — this is the key operative point. Use a MEDIAL approach: the "over-the-top" (Hotchkiss) interval or the FCU-splitting (Taylor-Scham) interval, with the ULNAR NERVE identified and protected first.
    • Fix with a medial ANTI-GLIDE / BUTTRESS PLATE contoured over the anteromedial facet; a comminuted rim may need a small plate or a mesh, and screws alone are insufficient because the fragment is thin and shears.
    • Repair the LUCL to the isometric point on the capitellum, and reassess stability afterwards. If the sublime tubercle (type 2.3) is involved, the MCL may also need repair.
    • A hinged external fixator is added if the joint still will not stay concentric.
    • Early active motion once stable, as with any complex elbow injury.
  • Points that are easy to get backwards.
    • A coronoid fracture with an INTACT radial head is a varus posteromedial injury, not a terrible triad — and it needs a medial, not a lateral, buttress.
    • The fragment size does NOT determine the treatment; the joint's concentricity and stability do. A "small" anteromedial facet fracture can be a surgical emergency for the joint.
    • The LUCL is torn in VPMRI despite the varus mechanism — assuming the lateral side is intact because the injury is "medial" is the classic error.
    • The MCL is not universally involved — only subtype 2.3 (sublime tubercle) reliably disrupts its insertion.

Elbow anatomy & general principles

Carrying angle, articulation and basic kinematics.

4 of 10 sittings2017, 2018, 2019 (x3), 2024S

  • Three articulations in one capsule.
    • Ulnohumeral — a hinge (ginglymus) between the trochlea and the sigmoid (semilunar) notch; provides flexion-extension and is the primary bony stabiliser.
    • Radiocapitellar — a ball-and-socket contributing to both flexion-extension and rotation, and the principal secondary valgus and axial stabiliser.
    • Proximal radioulnar — a trochoid joint held by the annular ligament, giving forearm rotation.
    • Together this makes the elbow a trochoginglymoid joint. It is one of the most congruent and inherently stable joints in the body, which is why bony reconstruction takes priority over ligament repair in complex instability.
  • The stabilisers: primary, secondary, and dynamic.
    • PRIMARY (three): (1) the ULNOHUMERAL ARTICULATION (especially the coronoid and the olecranon), (2) the ANTERIOR BUNDLE OF THE MEDIAL COLLATERAL LIGAMENT, (3) the LATERAL ULNAR COLLATERAL LIGAMENT.
    • SECONDARY: the RADIAL HEAD (the most important of these), the COMMON FLEXOR and COMMON EXTENSOR ORIGINS, and the JOINT CAPSULE.
    • DYNAMIC: the muscles crossing the joint — anconeus, brachialis, triceps, and the flexor-pronator mass (FCU in particular) — which compress the articulation and are the basis of rehabilitation-based management.
    • Structures that are NOT stabilisers, and are the usual distractors: the transverse (Cooper's) ligament of the MCL (both ends attach to the ulna, so it cannot resist valgus), the annular ligament as regards ulnohumeral stability (it stabilises the PRUJ only), and the posterior bundle of the MCL outside deep flexion.
    • The "circle of stability" concept: soft-tissue disruption in a dislocation proceeds from LATERAL to MEDIAL, so the LUCL fails first and the MCL last.
  • Valgus and varus load sharing, by position.
    • VALGUS in EXTENSION: load is shared roughly equally in thirds between the MCL, the bony ulnohumeral articulation and the anterior capsule.
    • VALGUS in FLEXION (30°–120°): the anterior bundle of the MCL is the primary restraint, because the olecranon disengages from its fossa and the anterior capsule slackens.
    • VARUS in EXTENSION: the bony articulation carries about 55% of the load, with the anterior capsule and LCL sharing the rest.
    • VARUS in FLEXION (90°): the bony articulation carries about 75% — which is why isolated LCL injury rarely produces gross varus laxity but does produce rotatory instability.
    • The RADIAL HEAD contributes about 30% of valgus stability with an intact MCL, and becomes the critical restraint once the MCL is deficient — the reason radial head excision is contraindicated in a valgus-unstable or ligamentously injured elbow.
  • Osseous geometry and the axis of rotation.
    • The distal humeral articular surface is rotated approximately 30° ANTERIORLY relative to the humeral shaft, sits in about 5°–7° of VALGUS, and is internally rotated about 3°–5° relative to the epicondylar axis. Restoring these is the aim of any distal humeral reconstruction.
    • The sigmoid notch opens about 30° POSTERIORLY, matching the anterior tilt of the trochlea; the proximal ulna also has a normal DORSAL ANGULATION of roughly 6° and a varus bow of about 14° — failing to restore these in proximal ulna fixation is a common cause of a radial head that will not reduce.
    • The flexion-extension axis passes through the CENTRE OF THE CAPITELLUM AND THE CENTRE OF THE TROCHLEA, emerging medially at the anteroinferior medial epicondyle. It is close enough to a fixed axis through the functional arc that a simple hinge external fixator works.
    • The carrying angle is a normal valgus of about 11°–14° in men and 13°–16° in women, present in full extension with the forearm supinated and disappearing in flexion.
    • The bare area of the sigmoid notch (a transverse non-articular strip) is the deliberate site for an olecranon osteotomy and explains why a small central articular gap after olecranon fixation is tolerated.
  • Ossification and the paediatric elbow: CRITOE.
    • Capitellum (~1 yr), Radial head (~3), Internal (medial) epicondyle (~5), Trochlea (~7), Olecranon (~9), External (lateral) epicondyle (~11) — roughly every two years, and girls ossify about 1–2 years earlier than boys.
    • The order matters clinically: if the trochlea is ossified but the medial epicondyle is not visible, the medial epicondyle is displaced — classically trapped inside the joint.
    • The medial epicondyle is the LAST physis to fuse (around 15–17 years), which is why it is the site of the paediatric avulsion in the throwing athlete.
  • Lines, angles and surgical intervals worth having by heart.
    • ANTERIOR HUMERAL LINE — drawn along the anterior humeral cortex on a true lateral, it should pass through the MIDDLE THIRD of the capitellum; passing anterior to it indicates an extension-type supracondylar fracture.
    • RADIOCAPITELLAR LINE — a line along the radial neck must pass through the capitellum on EVERY view; it is the line that catches a Monteggia lesion.
    • Baumann's angle (humeral shaft to the lateral condylar physis, normally about 70–75°, compared with the other side) assesses coronal alignment after supracondylar fixation.
    • Surgical intervals: Kocher = anconeus (radial/PIN) and ECU (PIN) — keep the capsulotomy anterior to the radial head equator to protect the LUCL; Kaplan = ECRB and EDC, more anterior and closer to the PIN; Hotchkiss "over-the-top" and Taylor-Scham (FCU-splitting) medially for the coronoid; Henry anteriorly for the proximal radius.
    • The "safe zone" of the radial head for hardware is the arc between the radial styloid and Lister's tubercle with the forearm in neutral — it does not articulate with the lesser sigmoid notch.
